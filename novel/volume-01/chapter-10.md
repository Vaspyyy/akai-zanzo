# Chapter 10: Side Bet

At six my cap was still on the office counter by the till, where I'd put it at two, with the band done up on the tightest hole. I left it there.

I had my bag on my shoulder for school and was halfway out of the office when Kubo-san said, "When the wheels were off."

I stopped in the door.

"At Tanabe's." He was folding the newspaper in four, which he does when he's finished with it. "What was behind them?"

I hadn't thought about it since. But I'd seen it, the way you see things you don't know the names of yet. Tanabe-san had lifted the front of my car on the forks, and I'd spun the last nut off and pulled the wheel toward me, and behind it there'd been a steel disc standing on its edge, as big as a dinner plate. Brown with rust all over, except for a band round the outside that had been worn smooth, with fine grooves going round in it. Over the top of the disc sat a lump of iron that held it from both sides.

"A round plate," I said. "Rusty. Smooth in a ring round the outside, with grooves in the ring. And a ridge right at the edge, sticking up. A lip."

He waited.

"The iron thing on top. You could see down into it. There was a black piece in there against the plate, on a steel back. Thin." I thought about it. "Thinner than a 10-yen coin. On the left one the steel was nearly on the plate."

He put the paper under the counter.

"Pads," he said. "The fronts. The discs, we'll see when they're off." He looked out past the pumps at the road, which was only just going grey. "After Sunday it'll roll. Then it'll want to stop."

"How much?"

"Six thousand. About. I'll get them Saturday."

"I'll bring it Sunday."

He nodded. He took his coffee off the shelf, found it was cold, and drank it anyway.

"The Honda," he said, into the cup. "At four."

"High-octane. Full."

That was true. He looked at me over the cup for as long as it took the day man to find a pen.

"School," he said.

I went from the pumps to school. In homeroom Hotta-sensei read out the names of the ones whose company forms still hadn't come in, and mine was the last. "Tuesday, Hayama," he said, to the list. "Don't make me come to your house." The midterms weren't back yet. I stayed awake until sixth period, and then only lost a bit of it, and went home along the canal and lay down on my futon at a quarter past four in my uniform trousers.

* * *

The knock came at twenty to seven. Two knocks on the steel door, not loud, and then a woman's voice through it.

"Hayama-san? It's Sasaki. Mai's mother."

The fusuma between my futon and the kitchen hasn't shut all the way since Sota ran into it when he was seven, and through the gap I could see a strip of the kitchen: the stove with Mom at it, and past her the genkan and the door.

The frying stopped. Mom turned the gas off and wiped her hands on her apron and opened the door on the chain. Then she shut it, and slid the chain off, and opened it again properly.

Sasaki-san stood on the landing under the stair lamp in a beige coat with the brown cardigan under it. She was holding up a hanger by its hook, and on the hanger, in a clear plastic cover from the cleaner's with a paper tag stapled to the top, was a child's winter jacket. Navy, padded, with a hood.

"I'm sorry, at dinnertime," she said. "You never rang, so I thought, well, I'll just go round. The lady on the third floor told me which door. Through her chain." She laughed. "This is Ken's. He grew out of it over the summer, all at once. They do that. I thought, Sota-kun."

"Oh, no," Mom said. "We couldn't."

"It's only had the one winter. The zip's good. I had it cleaned." Sasaki-san lifted it a little, and the tag turned in the light. "It's no use to anybody in my cupboard."

"Sota has a coat."

Sota had come up behind her by then, in his socks, with his glasses on, and was looking at the jacket with his mouth open. His coat was the green one from two winters ago. In the mornings, on the stairs, he pulled the sleeves down over his wrists, and they came back up.

"I'm sure he has," Sasaki-san said. "It's only that it'll hang there, and the moths will have it, and then I'll feel bad about it every time I open the door. You'd be doing me a favor, Hayama-san. Honestly."

She held it out the third time. Not up by the hook now. Flat, across both her arms.

Mom looked at it for a long time. Then she put her hands under it and took it.

"Thank you," she said. "Thank you very much."

"Mai says hello to Sota-kun. She says it every day. And Ken says Fujita's tomorrow, two o'clock, and bring the one with the coins on." Sasaki-san smiled at Sota. "He won't say it himself. He's eleven."

"I'll bring it," Sota said.

"Thank you for coming all this way," Mom said.

"It's on my way home." Sasaki-san was already turning to the stairs. "Go on, your oil's getting cold."

Mom shut the door.

She stood in the genkan with the jacket in its cover held against her front. Sasaki-san's shoes went down the stairs, quick, a landing, another landing.

Then Mom bowed to the door. A real bow, from the waist, with the jacket in both arms. She straightened up and hung the hanger on the hook by the door, over Sota's green coat, and went back to the stove and lit the gas.

"Can I try it on?" Sota said.

"After you've eaten."

He sat down at the low table and looked at the hook.

I lay back down. When I got up at a quarter past nine, she'd gone to the supermarket, and Sota was standing at the kitchen window in it, with the cover off, zipped up to his chin with the hood up and his hands lost somewhere inside the sleeves.

"It's warm, Nii-chan," he said. "It's warm already. There's a pocket on the inside. With a zip."

"Does it fit?"

"It will." He turned round so I could see the back, and pushed the hood down.

Inside the collar, where you write your name, someone had written SASAKI in black marker. Over it somebody else had stuck a strip of white tape, neat, cut square at both ends. There was nothing written on the tape yet.

* * *

Saturday was the fourth Saturday of the month, so there was no school. I worked the Friday night, which was fish trucks and two taxis and Kubo-san's radio, where a man from Himeji wanted to know if he should forgive his brother. I came home at half past six and slept until one.

At ten past two I was at the low table with a bowl of yesterday's rice and hot tea poured over it. The flat was empty except for me. Aya was on the till. Sota had gone to Fujita's at a quarter to two in Ken's jacket, with the sleeves turned back twice and his car zipped into the inside pocket.

I heard Mom on the stairs. You can tell her step on the last flight, because she stops once on the landing, for about a second, and then comes on.

She came in and shut the door and stepped out of her shoes. She smelled of the factory, rice and frying oil. She didn't step up into the kitchen. She stood on the concrete in her socks with her bag still on her shoulder, and looked at the shoe cabinet.

"Daisuke."

I looked up.

"Can you give me twenty thousand yen."

It didn't go up at the end. I put my chopsticks down across the bowl.

"The hospital was more than I had," she said. "And the new medicine is every month. The doctor wants him on it till spring." She moved the strap of her bag on her shoulder. "The rent goes on Tuesday."

That was all of it. I've heard her say more to the man who reads the gas meter.

I got up. My knee caught the edge of the table, and tea went over the side of the bowl. I went behind the fusuma to the box where I keep my school things and took the dictionary out of it, and the envelope out of the dictionary. Four 10,000s, three 1,000s, two 100-yen coins. 43,200. I didn't count it. I knew.

I came back and stood at the shoe cabinet beside her and counted it out there, as I do on paydays. Two 10,000-yen notes. Two 1,000s. Four 100-yen coins out of my pocket, on top, so they wouldn't blow. 22,400. Wednesday's half, four days early. On Wednesday there'd be nothing more to put on the cabinet.

Then I put another 10,000 down next to it.

She looked at that one.

I thought she'd push it back at me. I'd have known what to do with that. She didn't. She picked up the notes and the coins, all of it, and squared the notes with two taps on top of the cabinet, and folded them once, and opened her purse and put them in the part with the zip.

Then she stepped up into the kitchen and knelt at the low table, across from my rice, and took her household notebook out of her bag. The brown one. She opened it and wrote, and from where I stood I could read it upside down: a line on its own, under all the others. *28 Oct. Daisuke. 32,400.*

She shut the notebook and put it away.

"Your rice is cold," she said.

She wet her towel at the sink, wrung it out, and went behind the fusuma to the room she shares with Aya. I heard her lie down.

I ate the rice. It was cold.

Sota's medicine tin was on top of the refrigerator, where it always is. I didn't need anything out of it. I took it down anyway. Under the pills and the measuring spoon was the pharmacy bag, folded flat, with the receipt inside: the brown inhaler, the pills, the blue one. 7,180. In August it had been 4,820. That was 2,360 more, every month till spring. November, December, January, February, March. 11,800. I folded the bag back along its creases and put the tin back on the refrigerator, square to the edge.

In the dictionary there was a 10,000 left, and a 1,000, and the two coins. In my pocket, 97 yen. 11,297.

Tanabe-san's seven thousand was due on Tuesday. Kubo-san's six, about, on Sunday. Thirteen thousand. Wednesday was payday, but Wednesday was the first of November, and Tanabe-san's seven was October's. A month with nothing in it, she'd said, and the car goes in the press. She'd said it once. She'd never said a number to me twice.

Kubo-san would wait for the pads. I knew exactly how he'd do it. He'd say "Wednesday" and go into the back and turn the radio up. He'd given me his afternoon at the yard in October for nothing, and he'd give me this Sunday's for nothing, and now he'd be buying my pads with his own money and waiting to be paid back.

I could pay Tanabe-san, or I could pay for the pads. Not both, not before Wednesday. I did the sum again in case I'd got it wrong. I hadn't.

I washed the bowl and dried it and put it away. Ten pays sixteen, ten pays twenty-five. I could still hear Okabe calling it, in his greengrocer's voice, and Goto fanning out nine notes for the lot to see. On the rice-shop calendar by the phone it was the twenty-eighth. Saturday.

Sota came back at five with the jacket on and both his heats lost. The coins had stayed on all the way round, he said, every lap, which was what counted, and Ken had looked underneath it twice when he thought nobody was watching. He hung the jacket on the hook himself, by the loop inside the collar, over the white tape. Mom got up at six and made dinner. At twenty-five past seven, in the genkan, while she put her shoes on, I said it to the top of her head.

"I'm sleeping at Kawamata's tonight. For the stock at six."

"Sleep, then," she said, doing the heel of the second shoe with her finger. "Don't sit up talking."

She went. I lay down at eight. At eleven I got up and put on my own jacket, the one without a name on it, with the gloves for six o'clock in its pocket. I took the 10,000 out of the dictionary and left the 1,000 and the two coins. Aya was in the bath. I said "Kawamata's" through the door, and she said something I couldn't hear over the water.

* * *

*Saturday. 11:15 p.m.*

I went the back way, behind the fish-market sheds, the way Nobu and I had gone in September, and I went it alone. I hadn't told Nobu. There was no moon. The pallets along the loading docks were dark shapes against darker ones, and my breath showed under the one lamp by the sawmill. It took forty minutes, the same as before. I came out at the silos and went between them, and over the rails under the single light, and down the long straight with the warehouse walls beside me.

The lot was smaller with no moon on it, or it felt smaller. The sodium lights did what they could down one side, and the one that had been out in September was still out. There were fewer cars than then, fifteen or so in the horseshoe with their doors open and their heaters running, and people standing close to them with their hands in their armpits. One stereo, not four. I counted thirty-one people with my mouth shut.

I stood at the back, against the containers, where Kubo-san had said to stand.

The Silvia wasn't there. Okabe's van was, in the middle, with its back doors open and Okabe sitting in the doorway with the grey cash box on his knees. A padded jacket now instead of the polo shirt. The reading glasses pushed up into his hair. The walkie-talkie on the floor beside him.

I knew two of the cars.

The white Starlet was near the mouth of the lot, and the same boy was sitting in it, alone, with the door open. In September he'd had fifteen thousand and nobody to put down the rest. While I watched he got out and went round his own tires with a gauge, crouching at each one, reading it, letting a little air out of the back left with his thumbnail, reading it again. When he stood up he wiped the gauge on his trouser leg and put it in his shirt pocket.

Over by the bollards, nose to the water, was the black Supra. The round one. There was nobody sitting on its hood.

Its owner was standing at the front of it. Short leather jacket, good shoes with the backs trodden down, hair that had been done for somebody earlier in the evening. He lit a cigarette, and the lighter took three goes. There was no wind.

At twenty past one the Starlet boy walked across the lot to him. I couldn't hear what he said. I saw him hold up two fingers. The Supra's owner looked at the Starlet, and at the boy, and laughed, and said something short, and then they both went to the van.

I went closer. Not to the van. To the edge of the people round it.

"Two 10,000s," Okabe said, counting the first envelope flat on the lid of the box. "Two 10,000s." The second. He wrote in the notebook, and both envelopes went into the box, and the key went round.

Forty thousand in the box. Four of it would stay there. Thirty-six for whoever came back first.

"Supra, ten pays thirteen," Okabe called. "Starlet, ten pays thirty."

The money started going to the van, 1,000s and 5,000s mostly, folded, and Okabe's pencil went, and the corners of pages came off one after another. Almost everybody said "Supra." Two hundred and eighty horsepower, the round one, against a little Toyota you'd fetch the rice in.

I walked away from the van, round the outside of the horseshoe toward the bollards, as if I were going to look at the water.

The Supra's front wheels were turned hard to the right. Coming past its nose, I could see the inside edge of the right front tire. You can't usually. It's on the inside. But with the wheel turned out like that, the inside was turned half toward the front of the car, toward me, and the light from the pole was on it.

There was a band along the inside edge, two fingers wide, where the tread was gone. Not worn low. Gone. The rubber went smooth, and then it stopped, and under it there was a weave, a criss-cross, the same as the cloth of the cotton gloves in my pocket. In two places there were wires in the weave, fine and bright, and a few of the ends stood up out of it.

Okabe had shown me the slip sign, the little triangle and the bar in the groove under it. There wasn't any groove on that edge to have a bar in. From the van you couldn't have seen it, and Okabe hadn't got down off the van floor all night.

I kept walking, out to the bollards, and stood looking at the water.

When I turned round, the owner was back at his car. He got in, started it, and before anything else he turned the wheel with both hands until the fronts were straight. Then he sat there with the door open and looked down at the right front through the gap, for a long time. He got back out and went to the cooler van and bought one of the little brown energy drinks. His thumb slipped off the cap the first time. He wiped his hand on his jeans and did it again.

I could only see the one tire. Nobody sees the inside of a back tire without lying down. But a man who'd run his fronts down to the cords probably hadn't been looking after his backs either, and the backs were the ones doing the pushing. It was the backs I kept thinking about.

That was all I had. One tire, a lighter that took three goes, a thumb that slipped off a cap. I thought I knew what that added up to. I'd thought I knew things in the back lot too, and stalled.

Goto had said one thing about the Chaser's new tires in September, to nobody, and it had gone round the lot in a minute, and turned the price over.

I didn't say anything to anybody.

My hand went into my pocket. In September I'd watched the Starlet boy's hand go into his back pocket at the van and come out empty, twice. Mine came out with the 10,000 in it.

My palm was wet. I wiped it down my jeans, and saw that I'd done what the Supra's owner did, and stopped.

I went to the van and held it out.

Okabe looked at the note, and then at me, over his glasses, for about a second.

"Tanabe-san's customer," he said.

"Starlet."

"Ten pays thirty," he said, so that I'd heard it. "Same tenth as the pot. Off what you win."

"Twenty-eight," I said.

He wrote in the notebook. Then he tore the corner off the page and wrote on that, an S and a 10 and a 30 and a squiggle that might have been his name, and gave it to me. My 10,000 went into the box with the others, and the lid came down.

I buttoned the corner of paper into my shirt pocket. Then I thought of the older policeman in September, bending down in the headlights, buttoning one just like it into his own pocket. I unbuttoned it and put it in my trouser pocket instead, and kept my hand on it.

I had 97 yen in coins and a corner off a page of a school notebook. At home in the dictionary there was a 1,000 and two coins.

The radio on the van floor said "Quiet" from the on-ramp every few minutes, in a bored voice, and every time it did, a few people looked up the road anyway. So did I. I haven't stopped doing that since September.

The Starlet took the left lane, by the warehouses, and the Supra the right. The kid with the flashlight was a different kid. I stood on the last bollard at the corner, where I'd stood before, twenty meters behind him. Somebody turned the stereo off.

I could hear them both from there. The Starlet's little four sat on one note, a high, steady hum, and stayed on it. The Supra's six went up and fell back and went up again and fell, never the same twice, never staying. His foot couldn't keep still on it.

The flashlight went up, and stayed up, and came down.

The Supra's back tires lit up, both of them, white smoke in the sodium light, and the car slewed sideways a meter and caught. The Starlet was away clean but small. Then the Supra found the road and came onto its turbos, one shove and then, higher up the gear, a second, harder one, and it went down the straight as if the Starlet were parked. From the bollard all I had was two pairs of red lights, and the dark between them opening, wider and wider, until the Supra's were small under the crossing light and the Starlet's were still well short of it.

At the crossing, the Supra's brake lights came on while the Starlet's were still dark. Early. A long way before the bump, and they stayed on, all the way over it. The Starlet's came on late and went off again quickly. By the time the road took them left behind the silos, the two pairs of lights had nearly closed up again.

Past the silos I couldn't hear either of them. There was only the radio now.

"Silos." A boy, and his voice kept cracking. "Supra, Starlet. Starlet's on him. Starlet's right on him." A breath. Then it went up. "He's out! Supra's out, he's round, he's gone right round! He's facing this way! Starlet's through. Starlet's through."

A noise went across the lot, mostly a groan. Okabe's pencil stopped over the notebook. He didn't write anything.

"He's moving," the radio said. "Supra's going again. He's... he's coming back. He's coming back down your way."

"On-ramp," a different voice said. "Starlet. On his own."

I heard the Supra before I saw it. A big six at almost nothing, coming up the straight from the crossing end, and under it a slapping, regular, slow, that went slower when the engine went slower. Then headlights at the crossing, under the light, pointing at us. They took a long time to come down the kilometer. Nobody said anything while they did.

"Curve," said the radio, from my overpass. "Starlet. Clean."

The Supra came into the lot at walking pace and rolled across to the bollards where it had been parked, and stopped. The right back tire was down on the rim. A long strip of it hung loose and had been slapping the inside of the arch every time the wheel went round. The corner of the front bumper was cracked and scraped white, where something at the silos had been in the way.

The owner got out and went round to the back and looked at the tire. In the light from the pole you could see right into it, where it had torn open along the edge, the cords bunched and broken and sticking out in a fringe. He sat down on the bollard next to it. Nobody went over to him. Two men he'd been talking to earlier stood by the cooler van with their cans, their backs to him.

"Exit," the radio said. "Starlet."

Headlights from the far end of the harbor road, the Higashi end. One pair, small and low. The Starlet came down past the flashlight kid on its own, with the little engine screaming at the top of a gear, and then the brake lights, and then the smell of his brakes. A few people cheered. Most of them had money on the other one.

The boy got his thirty-six thousand from Okabe in three 10,000s, a 5,000 and a 1,000. He stood at the van and counted them, twice. Okabe watched him do it and smiled.

I gave Okabe the corner of paper. He looked at it, and ran his pencil down a column in the notebook until it stopped.

"Twenty-eight," he said, and licked his thumb.

Two 10,000s, a 5,000 and three 1,000s, into my hand. He didn't count them twice. He put the corner of paper in the back of the notebook. Then he took his glasses off his nose and pushed them up into his hair and looked at me properly.

"You've got an eye," he said. "You went and looked at his front wheel, and then you came to me." He smiled. It was the nice smile, the one from September. "Ever bet on a sure thing?"

I didn't say anything.

"Yourself?"

"I haven't got a car that goes."

"Tanabe-san's Roadster." He nodded. "When it goes."

Then he looked past me, across the lot, to the man on the bollard.

"Monday," he called over. "Come by the shop. I'll do you a pair for the back."

* * *

I went back up the straight on the warehouse side, in the dark between the lights, with my hand in my trouser pocket on the notes. They'd been in Okabe's box with everybody else's. They were warm, and soft at the folds, and I kept turning them over with my thumb to feel that they were still there.

Our genkan. The same concrete as now. My father sitting on the step at night to take his shoes off, laughing at something, and when he stood up, silver balls came out of the turn-ups of his trousers and ran across the floor, three or four of them, into the corners by the door. Aya and I went after them on our hands and knees. They were heavier than they looked, and cold. He'd laugh more when we found them. I kept them in a jam jar under the sink, and by the time I was eight there were enough to rattle.

When I was nine there were two men at that door, in good shoes. They were very polite. They asked for him by his whole name. Mom kept the chain on and said he wasn't here, and one of them said that was all right, they'd come again. They did.

I don't know what happened to the jar.

I went over the rails and past the end of Tanabe-san's lane, which was dark, the fence at the bottom of it just a paler dark.

Between the silos something crunched under my shoe. Plastic, off the corner of his bumper, I thought. It was too dark to see the road, but I could smell it, rubber, still hanging there in the cold. I stood in it for a while. It didn't tell me anything. It could have been the back tire. It could have been his foot, or diesel off a fish truck, or the Starlet's lights filling his mirror. I'd only seen the front.

Up onto the footpath along the bypass, where the trucks go by at your elbow in the daytime and nothing went by now.

The overpass was empty. On the deck, by the railing over the middle of the curve, there were cigarette ends, eight or nine of them, and a scatter of ash. The lookout's, from the run. He'd been standing where I stand.

It was nearly three. I sat down with my back against the railing and my knees up and my jacket zipped, one hand in the jacket pocket on the gloves and the other in my trouser pocket on the money, and I don't know when I went to sleep.

When I woke up I was cold all the way through, and it was half past five. The lamp at the top of the stairs was still on. Behind the cranes the sky had stopped being black along the bottom.

I took the money out under the lamp and counted it. Two 10,000s. A 5,000. Three 1,000s. 97 yen in coins.

I took the 5,000 and two of the 1,000s and folded them together, small, and buttoned them into my shirt pocket. Tanabe-san's. Kubo-san's six would have to come out of a 10,000. He'd give me the change out of the till and not look at it.

Eighteen thousand. Two thousand more than the boy who'd driven. I did it in hours, because that's what I do. Of every seven hundred an hour, three hundred and fifty is mine. Fifty-one hours and twenty-six minutes at the pumps. Six nights, and a piece of a seventh. I'd stood on a bollard for about four minutes.

Below me the first car of the morning came round the long left-hander with its headlights still on, not fast, and went under my feet. Tanabe's was twenty-five minutes, walking.

I put the rest of the money back in my trouser pocket. Not in the jacket, with the gloves and Sota's list and the tooth.
