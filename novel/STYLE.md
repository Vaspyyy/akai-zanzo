# Style guide: Dai's voice

Read this before every chapter, and again before revising. The sample at the end is the target.

## 1. The voice in one paragraph

Daisuke "Dai" Hayama, 17, tells his own story in plain words, some time after it happened. He sits at the bottom of his class in his last school year, spent four years (ages 10 to 14) in a facility, and has never had money, so he counts it exactly and notices what things cost. He is dry, proud, quick to anger and slow to say what he feels. He sees cars and people with a sharp, physical eye: hands, feet, shoes, tires, the note of an engine. He is often funny without trying to be. He is sometimes wrong, and the narration doesn't step in to correct him.

## 2. Person, tense, distance

- **First person, past tense** [CANON]: "That night I was up there." Present tense only for things that are still true ("Kubo smokes like it's his job").
- He tells it from not long after. **No spoilers, no "little did I know".** At most one dry hindsight line per chapter, and it never gives away what happens.
- **Dai's eyes only.** He reports what he saw, heard or was told. Scenes without him (the manga's visor panel) don't exist in the novel until someone tells him.
- Akane: he calls her "Natsume-san" to her face and "Natsume" in narration until the outline says otherwise (Volume 3, when she says "Call me Akane"). He learns her first name in Vol 1 ch7.

## 3. How Dai sounds

- Short and medium sentences. A long one only when speed or memory runs away with him.
- Humor comes from understatement, exact numbers and other people's lines. He doesn't do jokes; he reports.
- **Money is always exact:** "39,200 yen", "110 yen for a coffee I didn't want".
- **The car eye:** he can name an engine by sound before he sees it. He watches what hands and feet do. Gear changes, tires, brake dive and engine notes are how he understands speed.
- **People:** hands, shoes and posture before faces. He reads people the way he reads cars, and is right about cars more often.
- **Feelings** come through the body and what he does. He never names a feeling and then explains it.
- **Vocabulary:** a bright kid with bad grades. Car words come from magazines and Kubo; early on he sometimes uses them a little wrong, later precisely (that's growth, track it).
- **Images** are few and come from his world: the station, the danchi, school, vending machines, the fish trucks, the factory. Nothing from poetry.

## 4. Chapter and scene format

- File `novel/volume-NN/chapter-NN.md`. First line: `# Chapter N: Title`. 5,000-7,000 words, 3-6 scenes.
- **Scene break:** a line containing only `* * *`.
- Put the reader in a concrete place and time in the first paragraph. Italic time stamps in the manga caption style (*Thursday. 1:50 a.m.*) may open a scene when time matters; three per chapter at most.
- Every chapter has at least one quiet, everyday scene: food, chores, sleep, work, money, family. Slow pacing is canon.
- Silence is allowed. A wordless manga panel becomes a short paragraph of pure observation.
- End on a turn, an image or a line of dialogue. Never on a summary or a lesson.

## 5. Interior thought

- About one part thought to three parts action, dialogue and observation.
- Thoughts are short and concrete, often a question he doesn't answer.
- One longer reflective passage per chapter at most (about 150 words), and only if earned.

## 6. Driving and races

- **Before a race,** lay out the ground in landmarks the reader can hold (the Loop: Pier 3 lot, warehouse straight, rail crossing, silo S-bend, on-ramp, the long left-hander under the overpass, Higashi exit) and say what's at stake: money, who's watching, police.
- **During,** place both cars every few paragraphs: who leads, by how much, inside or outside, which mirror. Then Dai's hands, feet, gears, revs, the engine note, the tires, the light (sodium orange, headlights, wet reflections). The reader must always know where the cars are.
- Short sentences at the limit; slightly longer on the straights, where he has time to think.
- **Physical truth.** The NA Roadster is light, balanced and underpowered: it gains in corners and on nerve, loses on straights. Right-hand drive: the shifter is under his left hand, the driver's window on his right. Heel-and-toe: ball of the right foot on the brake, heel or side of the foot blipping the throttle on the downshift.
- Nobody does the impossible. Mistakes have causes (worn tires, heat, lifting mid-corner, fatigue, fear).
- **After,** show the cost: tires, money, shaking hands, someone hurt, something broken.
- A race runs 1,500-3,000 words. One per chapter at most; most chapters have none.

## 8. Japanese terms and names

- **Follow the manga.** Sota calls Dai "Nii-chan". Dai and Aya call their mother "Mom". Honorifics stay: -san, -kun, -chan, -sensei. Dai says "Kubo-san", "Natsume-san", "Tanabe-san". Characters giving their names aloud say the family name first, as in the manga ("Hayama. Daisuke."). Full names in narration go given name first ("Akane Natsume"), as in the bible.
- **Keep** words with no clean English: danchi, genkan, tatami, fusuma, futon, bento, onigiri, gakuran, hanko, pachinko, oden, kotatsu, otoshidama, ozōni, shaken (explain once: the car's two-yearly inspection). Don't italicize them. Use "convenience store", not "konbini" (the manga does).
- "Eh?" and "Oi" are fine. No decorative Japanese ("baka", "sugoi", "nani"), no romaji sentences.
- **Akai Zanzō** with the macron. The scene says "the Akai Zanzō".
- **Numbers:** digits for money, times and speeds ("700 yen", "2:14 a.m.", "40 km/h"). Words for small counts in prose ("three Thursdays").

## 9. Period details (Aug 1995-Mar 1997)

- **Use:** pagers among teens (number codes: 0840 good morning, 4649 regards, 0906 running late); payphones and telephone cards; one phone in the kitchen; cassette tapes and CD singles; Eurobeat in racers' cars; the CRT TV with the Aum trials and Nomo in America on the news; VHS; car magazines read standing in the shop; canned coffee at 110 yen from machines that also sell beer and cigarettes; paper maps; film cameras (disposables, a used SLR); the 1,000-yen note with Natsume Sōseki on it (yes, Akane's surname); the Mini 4WD boom; PlayStation and Sega Saturn as things Sota can't have; consumption tax 3% (5% only from April 1997); bubble-burst bankruptcies.
- **Never:** mobile phones in teenagers' hands (rare and expensive: businessmen and rich kids only), internet or email for anyone in the story, digital cameras, GPS, LED lights, ENEOS stations (2001), Gran Turismo (late 1997), Civic Type R EK9 (1997), Silvia S15, Skyline R34, the second-generation Roadster, references to real racing manga.
- **Cars:** see `continuity/cars.md`. Right-hand drive everywhere. Check every model year before it appears.
- **Law:** license at 18, adulthood and alcohol at 20, minors need a parent's consent for contracts. Police drive white-and-black Crowns; checkpoints come with the safety campaigns.

## 10. Content and tone

- Gritty, not gratuitous [CANON]. Crashes are medically real (Akane's eye): spinal injuries, compound fractures, shock. The aftermath gets more words than the impact.
- Nobody is a villain. Every adult has a reason, and Michiko's view weighs as much as Dai's [CANON].
- Warmth and humor in every chapter, including the bad ones.
- Swearing moderate: Goto most, Dai rarely out loud. Alcohol and cigarettes period-normal, not glamorous.
- **Romance [CANON]:** nothing romantic until Dai is 18. Before that his crush shows as awkwardness and noticing. He never describes Akane's body: he describes her hands, eyes, voice and driving.
- The facility appears in short fragments until it has been researched properly (bible §2).

## 11. Don'ts: AI-prose habits

- **No em dashes, ever.** The creator dislikes them. Use commas, colons, periods or parentheses. No double hyphens either. Interrupted speech trails off with "..." as in the manga.
- **Banned:** testament, tapestry, "the weight of" (for feelings), "something shifted", palpable, visceral, electric (for mood), symphony, dance (for driving), delve, intricate, liminal, orbs, "a mix of X and Y", "I let out a breath I didn't know I was holding", "for what felt like an eternity", "in that moment", "time stood still", "the world fell away", "a smile tugged at", "eyes that held", "every fiber of my being", "the silence was deafening".
- Don't explain a feeling after showing it. Trust the scene.
- Don't repeat a beat. One image per idea. No three near-synonyms in a row; no tricolon habit.
- Don't make everyone witty in the same way. Banter is a voice, not a house style.
- Don't end scenes with a moral or a neat summary of what was learned.
- Don't over-describe. One or two exact details beat a paragraph.
- "Said" and action beats; no fancy tags ("he growled", "she breathed").
- Don't open a chapter by waking up, or with weather that doesn't matter.
- No strings of rhetorical questions; no paragraph after paragraph starting "And yet" or "But"; fragments only now and then.
- No modern idioms ("process", "toxic", "vibe", "closure", "trauma" in casual use, "reach out").
- No info-dumps of specs. Specs come through use: what the car does under his feet.
- Don't summarize stretches of time in big blocks. Use a bridge line or a scene break.
- Sentiment stays under the surface. Dai doesn't cry easily on the page, and the prose doesn't cry for him.

**Lessons from reviews (Ch1-5).** Reviewers kept catching these:

- "The way you..." similes: two or three per chapter at most, and few "as if".
- Every character ending a beat on a deadpan punchline. Give the quip to one person per scene; let the others be plain, tired or kind.
- Re-staging the previous chapter's beats (the same greeting, door, TV, arrival). One or two deliberate echoes, no more.
- Long summary montages. A short bridge line with a time marker, then the next scene.
- Stating the chapter's theme ("two futures") or recapping it before the big scene. Let the wait do the work.
- Explaining a reaction after the dialogue already showed it ("He was pleased with me.").
- Car-technical slips: heel-and-toe belongs to downshifts; the lever goes home before the clutch comes up; pop-up headlights follow the light switch, not the ignition.
- Dai perceiving more than he physically could. Distant cars reach him through lookouts' radios; his ear is for what's near and for changes in an engine note, not for kilometres.
- Sums and maps the money-exact narrator would catch. Do every sum (stakes, cuts, tips, change) and walk every route against OUTLINE §3 before writing it.
- One prop remarked on by everyone (Ch5's bandage drew six reactions). Let two or three people notice it; the others can see it and stay silent.
- Plants bunched in one stretch (a backstory, a phone call, a forbidden object and a hint, one after another) read like a character sheet. One plant per beat; move the rest to other scenes or cut them.

## 12. Sample passage (Volume 1, Chapter 1: the empty Thursday)

*About 850 words. Target voice, not final text.*

> On the last Thursday of August I was at the pumps by a quarter to ten with my cap on straight.
>
> Kubo-san looked up from his newspaper when I came in, looked at the clock, and looked at me.
>
> "Early," he said.
>
> "The bus was early."
>
> "You walked. Your shoes are white with dust." He turned a page. "Sit down. You're making the pumps nervous."
>
> I didn't sit down. I wiped both pump islands, all four nozzles, and when there was nothing left to wipe I did the office windows from the outside, so that Kubo-san had to watch me do it from the inside, with his coffee, the way you watch a fish in a tank.
>
> By then I knew the harbor road by ear. You learn it in three weeks of nights if you have nothing else to think about. The container trucks came first, the big tractors grinding down through the gears at the light by the rail crossing, a sound like someone dragging furniture across a floor upstairs. After midnight came the taxis, Crowns and Cedrics running on LPG, which sigh instead of revving. Then the drunks in their fathers' sedans. Then nothing, and the cranes, and the mosquitoes.
>
> None of them sounded like her car. Her car didn't sigh and it didn't drag. It went up. That was the only way I could put it, even to myself: every other engine on that road went along, and hers went up, like a voice.
>
> At 12:10 a kei truck came in for 1,000 yen of regular and the driver paid in coins. At 1:00 Kubo-san took his radio into the back room, where he listened to a late-night call-in show and argued with the callers. At 1:40 I bought a can of coffee from the machine by the air pump. 110 yen. I didn't want it. I wanted something in my hand.
>
> At 1:50 I started listening properly.
>
> I told myself it was nothing. Three Thursdays in a row isn't a law of nature. Kubo-san had said every Thursday, and I'd built my whole week around a sentence an old man said while he counted change.
>
> At 2:00 a car turned off the bypass and my heart did something stupid. Pop-up headlights. I saw them flip open far down the road, two little eyes waking up, and I was walking toward the pump island before the sound reached me.
>
> It was a 180SX. Silver, rust at the rear arches, a guy about my age driving, a girl asleep against the window. He wanted 2,000 yen of regular and the toilet key. I gave him both. I cleaned his windshield, and I did a good job of it, because it wasn't his fault he wasn't her.
>
> At 2:30 Kubo-san came out and stood next to me under the canopy. He lit a cigarette and held it the way he holds a dipstick, between two fingers and away from himself, like it might drip. He didn't say anything for most of it.
>
> "She's not a train," he said finally.
>
> "I know."
>
> "People get sick. People get busy. Sometimes a woman just doesn't feel like buying gasoline from a kid who stares."
>
> "I don't stare."
>
> He let the smoke out through his nose. It hung in the canopy lights, which were full of moths. I'd never noticed how many moths there were.
>
> "First Thursday she's missed since I started noticing," he said, and went back inside.
>
> I stayed under the canopy until it got light. The trucks started again around five, the first ones heading for the fish market with their refrigeration units rattling. The sky over the cranes went grey, then the color of weak tea. At six the day man came, chewing gum with his mouth open, and I signed the sheet and handed over the cap.
>
> I went home the long way, up the bypass on the footpath. I don't know why. Yes, I do. I stood on the overpass for a while and looked at the curve, empty and clean in the morning, nothing on it but a page of newspaper lifting and settling in the wind from the trucks.
>
> On the outside of the curve there was a section of guardrail newer than the rest. I'd stood up there maybe twenty nights and never seen it. It was brighter, and the bolts hadn't rusted yet, and below it, on the concrete at the foot of the overpass stairs, somebody had scrubbed at a dark scrape until the concrete went pale.
>
> Somebody had hit that hard.
>
> I went home. Mom had already left for the factory. Sota was asleep with his mouth open and his glasses still on, and I took them off and folded them and put them next to his inhaler, the way he likes.
>
> I lay down and closed my eyes and did the shifting again, four to three, three to two, the heel rolling over onto the gas, until I didn't know if I was practicing or dreaming.
>
> She didn't come. That was the whole night. I'd tell it shorter if I could.
