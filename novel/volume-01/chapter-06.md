# Chapter 6: Night Shift

She looked at me for about a second. Then she looked back down at the clipboard, and her pen made a small mark on it, and the clock over the orange chairs said 1:54.

"Are you the family?"

"His brother."

"Come in, please."

Please. I stood up. My legs had stopped shaking somewhere between my brother's name and the clipboard, and I only found that out when I was walking on them.

Behind the curtain it was smaller than it had sounded. A high bed with its back raised. A steel stand with a bag of something clear hanging off it, not connected to anything yet. A grey box on a shelf with green numbers on it and a cable running down to the clip on Sota's finger. The wall behind the bed was all sockets and dials, and the loud hiss came out of one of them, down a clear tube, into a mask on Sota's face. Under the mask hung a little plastic cup, and out of the holes in the sides of the mask came mist. Thin and white. It curled up past his ears and was gone.

Somebody had pulled a table on wheels across the bed and put a pillow on it, and Sota was folded forward over the pillow with his arms crossed on it and his chin on his arms. His shoulders were still up. Not as high.

He saw me and tried to sit up.

"Stay like that," Natsume said. "That's the best way."

It was to him, not me. And it was her voice, but slower, and lower, with the ends of the words left soft. I'd heard her say maybe a hundred words in my life, at a pump and in a car, and none of them had sounded like that.

The doctor was young, with his hair flat at the back where he'd been lying on it and the brown slip-ons I'd seen under the curtain. He had Mom's rice-cracker tin open on the end of the bed, and he held up a strip of Sota's pills.

"Theodur. Every day?"

"Morning and night."

"The last one?"

"After dinner. Mom said he'd had it, when she left. Half past seven."

He said something to Natsume with a number in it, and she wrote it down without looking at her own hand. He went out through the curtain. She turned to the wall and changed something on the dial. The hiss went down half a note.

"Natsume-san," I said.

"Sit there, please."

There was a round stool in the corner, next to a steel bin with a foot pedal. I sat on it.

She came round the bed and bent down with her face level with Sota's, close, so he didn't have to turn his head. She had a watch pinned to the front of her uniform upside down, so she could read it looking down, and she looked down at it.

"Sota-kun. You know birthday candles?"

He nodded into the pillow.

"Breathe out like you're blowing them out. Slowly. All of them, one after the other."

His eyes went to her, and his lips moved inside the mask.

"How... many?"

"Eleven."

He breathed out. It wasn't long. The mist came out of the side holes in a puff. She waited, with her eyes on the watch, and he did it again, longer.

"Good," she said. "Again."

I'd seen her hands do heel-and-toe, two in the morning on the bypass, the right foot rolling over and the left hand going down through the gears. Now they were bare. Short nails. They took Sota's wrist and held it, her fingertips on the inside, and she looked at the watch and counted with her lips shut, and let go, and wrote. That was all.

She'd had a shift. The Thursday she didn't come. This was the job.

"Nii-chan." Sota had found me in the corner. "Glasses."

I took them out of my shirt pocket and unfolded them and put them on him, carefully, over the elastic of the mask. The lenses went white with the mist at once.

He turned his white lenses toward me anyway.

"Leave them," Natsume said, before I could take them off again.

The green numbers on the grey box said 93. In the ambulance it had been 89, then 90. Every time Sota's heart went, the box beeped, and the beep wasn't always the same note. When the number went up, the beep went up. I heard the beep climb, and looked, and it said 94.

The doctor came back with a steel tray and a little board, the size of a ruler, wrapped in gauze.

"A drip," he said to Sota. "In the back of your hand. It'll sting, and then it won't."

Natsume took Sota's left hand in both of hers and laid it flat on the board and held it there, and put her face down by his again.

"Candles," she said. "Look at me, not him."

He looked at her. The doctor put a rubber strap round Sota's wrist, slapped the back of his hand twice, and went in. Sota's whole body went tight. He didn't make any noise. The doctor said "Hm," and moved the needle, and said "Hm" again, and pulled it out and pressed a cotton ball on the spot.

"Sorry," Sota said, into the mask.

"Other hand," the doctor said.

Natsume let go of the left hand only when she had the right one flat on the board. The second time it went in. Dark blood came up into the little window at the back of the needle. The doctor slid the needle out and left a soft plastic tube behind in the vein, and drew blood back through it with a syringe, and filled three small tubes, one after the other, and gave them to her. She labelled them faster than I could read what she wrote. Then she taped the hand to the board, flat, with strips of white tape she tore off the roll with two fingers, every strip the same length, and she was finished before the doctor had connected the bag.

The clear stuff started dripping in the chamber under the bag. One drop. One drop.

"Did you blow them all out?" she said.

Sota held up his free hand. All five fingers, and then again, and then one.

"Good boy."

Then she stood up and was someone else again, and went past me through the curtain with the three tubes, and didn't look at me, any more than she looked at the bin.

For a while then it was only Sota and me and the hiss.

Under the edge of the curtain I could see the feet in the next bay: a man's black business shoes, one of them off, and a sock with a hole in the heel. The man in them was singing. Not loud. The same two lines of an enka about a harbor in the rain, over and over, and every time he got to the end of the second line he lost the third and went back to the start. A woman's voice told him to hold still. He said he was holding still.

Then other shoes came and stood next to his. Black, plain, polished. A radio on somebody's belt crackled and said a number.

My back had gone straight on the stool before I'd thought anything. Pier 3, the chit, the doorway. Then the man above the polished shoes spoke, and it was a young voice, bored, asking the singing man for his name for what sounded like the third time, and the singing man told him the name of a ship. It wasn't anything to do with me. I made my back go round again. It took a while.

The doctor came back with a stethoscope and listened to Sota's back, moving the disc from place to place and saying "Out. Out. Out." He straightened up and said to Natsume, "Moving air." She filled the little cup under the mask again: a few drops from a small brown bottle, drawn up in a syringe with no needle on it, and then a little plastic ampoule she snapped open with her thumb. The mist came back thicker.

Sota turned his head on the pillow, toward me. His voice came out small and wet inside the mask, but it came out, three or four words at a time.

"Did it... have the siren on? The ambulance."

"All the way."

"I didn't... hear it."

"It was loud."

He thought about that, with the mist going up past his glasses.

"I missed it," he said.

* * *

At about 2:20 two men pushed in a machine on wheels with an arm and a box on the end of it, and one of them said, "X-ray. Family outside, please," and put a heavy apron over Sota's lap.

I went out. There was a green public phone on the wall down the corridor, past the orange chairs. I was standing in front of it before I knew why. Then I knew why. Aya had gone back up the stairs at home, to phone Mom, and say he's breathing, and nobody had told her where.

I had 757 yen. I put in a 10-yen coin and dialed.

It didn't ring. Aya picked it up before it could.

"It's me."

"Where are you?"

"Municipal."

"I know. Mom rang the fire department. She's in a taxi." She breathed in. "Is he..."

"Better. He's got a mask with mist in it and a drip. They're doing an X-ray." I looked back at the curtain. "It says 94."

"What says 94?"

"A box. It was 89 in the ambulance."

"What's it supposed to say?"

"I don't know. More."

She didn't say anything for a while. I could hear our kitchen behind her: the refrigerator, and the clock.

"You went down the stairs with your laces undone," she said. "I watched you. You'll kill yourself."

"I'm fine."

"Do them up. Phone again if..." She didn't finish it. "If anything."

She put the phone down before I could say I would.

Mom came at 2:35. I saw her through the glass doors of the emergency entrance, running across the forecourt from a taxi that was already pulling away, and at the doors she stopped running and walked, fast, because it was a hospital. She was in the supermarket's green vest with HAYAMA on the name tag, and her shoes were the white rubber ones they wear on the shop floor. She hadn't changed them. Her bag was over her shoulder, the one with the thermos in it. Half her hair had come out of the bun.

She saw me. She looked at my face, and then down at the floor, at my laces, and then at the curtain.

"Behind there," I said.

The X-ray men were wheeling their machine out. She went in through the gap they left. I went in after her.

Sota had pulled the mask down to his chin. Natsume was saying "Back on, please," and putting it back on, and through it he said "Mom," and something else that might have been "sorry". Mom put her hand on his head, on his wet hair, and left it there. She didn't say anything. She looked at the drip, and the board, and the tape, and the box with the numbers, one after the other, as if she was going to have to pay for them separately.

Then she took his glasses off and wiped the mist off them on the front of her vest, and put them back on him. They went white again straight away. She took them off and wiped them again.

The doctor came and stood at the end of the bed and talked to her. It had been a big attack, he said. A major one. The drip was medicine to open his chest up, and a steroid, and he was getting better on it, but slowly, and they wanted to keep him. Some days. They'd see.

"Some days," Mom said.

"He'll go up to the children's ward when there's a bed. The nurse will do the forms with you."

The nurse was Natsume. She had a new clipboard and stood next to Mom, not across from her, so they were both looking at the same paper. Name. Date of birth. Address. Allergies. The insurance card. Mom had it in her purse. She always has it in her purse.

"Who'll be with him in the daytime," Natsume said.

"I'm at the factory from half past five. Until two." Mom said it to the clipboard. "Today I'm off, I swapped. Then the supermarket from eight till twelve. I can come between. Two till half past seven. His sister can do evenings, some evenings."

Natsume wrote it down. All of it. She didn't look up while she did.

"The night window's by the main doors," she said. "They'll take a deposit for tonight."

"How much?"

"They'll tell you."

The night window was a hatch in the wall by the locked main entrance, with a sliding glass panel and a sign over it, NIGHT AND HOLIDAY RECEPTION, and behind it an older man in a cardigan with a radio on very low and a tray of rubber stamps. He looked at the admission paper Mom slid under the glass and said, nicely, as he must have said it all night:

"Ten thousand tonight, please, as a deposit. The rest is settled at the cashier when he goes home."

"Ten thousand."

Mom opened her purse on the little ledge.

She took out a 5,000-yen note and three 1,000s, and put them in the tray, and smoothed them flat. That was the notes. The taxi had been the rest of them. Then she opened the coin part. Two 500-yen coins. Seven 100s. That made 9,700. Then the 10s, which she keeps for the phone, thirty of them, the last three hundred yen, and she counted them out into the tray one at a time with one finger, sliding each one off the edge of the purse. The man in the cardigan didn't hurry her. He stacked them as they came, in piles of ten, a little tower of brown coins, and then a second one.

Somebody walked past behind us in white shoes. I turned my head. Natsume, going down the corridor toward the lifts with a folder under her arm. She didn't slow down. But her eyes went to the ledge, to the two piles of 10-yen coins and the third one Mom was still building. Then they went to me. Then she was round the corner.

Mom put down the last coin. The man counted the third pile with his fingertip and nodded and wrote a receipt with a carbon underneath it, and stamped it twice, and slid it out.

"Thank you very much," Mom said, and bowed to him over the ledge.

In my English dictionary, on the shelf at home, at the end of Sota's futon, there were 42,400 yen.

She folded the receipt into the purse, next to the insurance card. The purse shut with a click. There wasn't much in it to stop it shutting.

* * *

At twenty past four a nurse from upstairs came down, and they unplugged Sota's mask from the wall and plugged it into a small cylinder, and hung the drip on a hook on the bed, and wheeled the whole thing out with Sota on it to the lift, with Mom walking beside him holding the rail. I went too. Fifth floor. The children's ward smelled of milk and disinfectant, and somewhere at the end of it a baby was crying and a woman was singing to it, very tired.

The night nurse up there was round and kind and quick, and at the door of the room she put her hand on my arm.

"One family member overnight," she said. "Mother, I think. Big brother can come back at visiting time."

Sota lifted his hand, the one without the board. I lifted mine. The door closed.

The first bus wasn't until after six. I went back down in the lift, which was huge, for beds, and smelled of somebody's cigarette. I sat on the bench by the night entrance, across from the vending machine.

A man in grey overalls came down the corridor behind a floor polisher, swinging it from side to side in long slow curves, and the floor came up shining behind him. He did the whole corridor and went round the corner, and I could hear him start the next one. I sat with my hands between my knees. Every time I shut my eyes I was on the bench in the ambulance with the siren going, so I kept them open.

*Tuesday. 5:02 a.m.*

The vending machine had changed over. The bottom row of buttons had gone red, which they do when the weather turns, with HOT on them in white: coffee, tea, a can of corn soup with a picture of corn on it. The top rows were still blue. In the glass of it I could see myself on the bench, small, with my hair short, and the corridor behind me going off empty to the ER doors.

The ER doors opened, and Natsume came out.

She had a navy cardigan on over the white uniform. She walked to the machine without looking at the bench, put in a 100 and a 10, and pressed the red button with the corn on it. The can came down. She picked it out of the slot and held it in both hands for a moment. The first hot can I'd seen this year.

She didn't sit down. She stood a meter and a half from the end of my bench, with her back to the machine, and opened it and drank.

I'd had something ready to say since four o'clock. Now that she was standing there, it was gone.

"Natsume-san."

"There's a machine for home," she said. "A nebulizer. It makes his medicine into mist, like downstairs. Mornings and nights. Some kids it helps a lot. Some it doesn't."

She was looking at the doors, not at me.

"How much is it?"

"Ask his doctor."

She drank. Then she tapped the bottom of the can twice with two fingers, sharp, and tipped it back. She knew about the corn. Everybody leaves the last of the corn stuck at the bottom of those cans, and she knew how to get it out.

A car came into the forecourt outside and went round and out again. Somebody lost.

"Your mother works two jobs," she said.

I didn't say anything.

"Your brother can't breathe." She turned the can round in her fingers and looked at it. "And you want that road."

It came up the back of my neck. I let it.

"You drive it."

She didn't answer that straight away. She finished the soup and stood holding the empty can.

"Nobody waits up for me," she said.

She dropped the can in the bin by the machine. It hit the other cans in there, and that was the loudest thing in the corridor. Then she went back through the ER doors, and they closed behind her by themselves, and I was looking at the red buttons, the coffee and the tea and the corn.

I had 747 yen. I didn't buy anything.

* * *

At a quarter past five Mom came down and found me on the bench, and said "Come outside," and we went out through the night entrance and sat on the bench by the bus stop, under the hospital's name on the wall. The sky over the car park was going from grey to the color of dishwater. The streetlights were still on. They hadn't been told.

She opened her bag and took out the thermos and the two onigiri in foil, the ones she'd taken to the stocktaking at half past seven last night. She hadn't eaten either of them. She gave me one and kept one, and unscrewed the cup off the thermos and poured tea into it, and gave me that too.

The onigiri had umeboshi in the middle. The tea was barley tea, which at home we only ever drink cold. She'd made it hot at seven last night, for the cold storage, before any of this, and it was still hot.

"He's asleep," she said. "Sitting up. With his glasses on. The nurse is going to take them off when he's properly gone."

"He folds them himself."

"I know."

She ate some of her onigiri. Her hands were red at the knuckles from the freezers at the supermarket, and there was a black mark on her thumb from some price gun.

"The doctor said you rang at the right time," she said.

"I waited the twenty minutes."

"The paper says twenty."

"The ambulance man said I shouldn't have."

She looked at the car park for a while.

"I wrote the paper," she said.

I drank the tea.

"Aya said you told her what to do," she said. "She said she couldn't move, and then you told her, and she could." She nearly smiled. "She was very angry about it."

A newspaper boy came round the corner on a motorbike, stopped at the hospital's mailbox, and went.

"Your father never sat in a hospital with any of you," Mom said. "Not once. Any of you, when you were sick, he'd drive us to the door. Then he'd go and play pachinko until it was over. He said he couldn't stand waiting rooms."

She said it to the car park. Then she seemed to hear what she'd said, and she stopped, and looked down at the onigiri in her hand.

I'd stopped chewing. I started again. The rice had gone hard in my mouth, and I swallowed it anyway.

"Eat the rest," she said. "Then go home and sleep. I'll ring the school at eight and say you're sick."

"I've got work tonight."

"Then sleep more."

She opened her purse, for the bus. She looked into it. I saw her look.

"I'll walk," I said.

"It's an hour."

"I know the way."

She closed the purse. I bent down and did up my laces, both of them, double knots. She watched me do it. She took my empty cup and shook the last drops out on the ground and screwed it back on the thermos. Then she put her hand on the back of my head, just for a second, on the short hair where the bangs used to come down, the way she'd put it on Sota's.

"Daisuke," she said. "Thank you."

Then she went back in to sit with him, in her white shop shoes, and I walked home. By halfway it was light, and I hadn't seen it happen, because I was looking at my feet.

* * *

At home Aya had put my futon out and gone to work. There was a note on the low table in her square handwriting: *RICE IN THE COOKER. YOU STILL OWE ME 3,000.* I lay down in my clothes. When I woke, the clock said ten to three and the refrigerator was humming to nobody.

Visiting time was three till eight. I walked back the way I'd come.

Mom was asleep in the chair by his bed, still in the green vest, with her head against the wall and one arm through the bed rail, her hand round Sota's ankle. Sota was asleep too, sitting up, with the mask on and the mist going. His glasses were on the locker, folded, the left arm over the right. That's how he does it. I didn't know if he'd done it himself, or a nurse who'd watched him do it once.

The box on the shelf said 96. Its beep was higher than it had been downstairs. I stood at the end of the bed and listened to it for a while. I didn't wake either of them. At half past five I walked home again, and at ten I was at the pumps.

I told Kubo-san that night: my brother, the ambulance, Municipal. He listened to all of it with his coffee, and didn't ask anything.

On Wednesday I went to school straight from the pumps. Nobu was at the shoe lockers, putting on his indoor shoes with the heels trodden flat.

"You weren't here yesterday. Hotta-sensei said you were sick." He looked at me properly. "You look sick. You look like the guy with the Chaser."

"My brother's in hospital. Asthma. Monday night."

Nobu opened his mouth, and shut it again. He put his outdoor shoes in his locker side by side, carefully, which he never does.

At lunch he put a plastic container on my desk. Six gyoza, cold, stuck together at the edges.

"My father says they're yesterday's," he said. "They're not yesterday's. He made them this morning. He just says that." He pushed them an inch closer. "It's not the passing gyoza. That's different gyoza. This is just gyoza."

I ate all six. He watched me eat them and didn't say one horsepower figure.

After school I took the bus to Municipal. 160 yen. 587 left.

The children's ward was awake today. Somebody had stuck paper fish on the windows of the playroom, with crayon scales, and there was a television on in there with nobody watching it. Sota was in a room of six beds by the window. The bed next to his had a little girl in it, asleep, with her mother asleep in the chair beside her with her mouth open.

Mom's cardigan was over the chair by his bed, and her bag under it, but not Mom. She'd gone down to the payphone to ring the supermarket about tonight, Sota said.

He was sitting up on his own pillow with his glasses on. The drip was still in, in his right hand on its board, and a tray of hospital lunch was pushed to the end of the bed with most of it still there: rice, fish in a white sauce, a cup of something orange. No mask. Just a thin tube under his nose, with two little prongs.

"Nii-chan." His voice was hoarse. "I didn't tell."

"I know."

"Not anybody." He looked over at the sleeping mother next to us, and dropped his voice to nearly nothing. "Is it really blue?"

"Faded. You rub it with your thumb and it comes back. Then it goes again."

He thought about that.

"What's the cat called?"

"It isn't anybody's. It hasn't got a name."

"Everybody's got a name," he said, shocked. Then he coughed. It was the small cough, the dry one, and it stopped on its own, and we both waited to see if it would, and it did.

He showed me a new inhaler on his locker. Not the blue one. A different color, with a plastic tube you fitted it into and breathed in through.

"For every day," he said. "Morning and night. Even when I'm fine. The doctor said."

I picked it up. It didn't have a price on it. Medicine never has a price on it. It goes on a piece of paper somewhere else.

Sota watched me put it back.

"Nii-chan." He pulled the sheet up a bit, over the board on his hand. "The ambulance was free."

"Yes."

"Is the hospital free too?"

The little girl's mother shifted in her chair and went on sleeping. On the television in the playroom somebody was laughing.

"Yes," I said.

He let his head go back on the pillow. He was asleep before I'd worked out what to do with my hands.

* * *

On Thursday night I didn't know if she'd come.

I'd never thought before about where she was on the other nights. Now I knew. On the other nights she was in that corridor in white shoes. Maybe after Tuesday she'd start buying her gas at the bypass station, at 114 yen, and save herself a yen a liter and me.

Kubo-san asked about Sota at ten.

"Still in," I said. "He's eating."

He nodded at the road, which is what he does instead of saying good. I hadn't told him on Tuesday who the nurse was. I didn't tell him now.

At midnight a delivery van came in for 2,000 yen of regular. At one, nothing. At half past one I swept the forecourt, which didn't need it.

At 2:06 she came in off the bypass. Pop-ups first, far down the road. Then the sound. Then the red car under the canopy lights, stopping at the pump with the filler on the right side, exactly where it always stopped.

The window came down.

"Regular. Fill it up."

I put the nozzle in. Kubo-san was in the office door with his can of coffee. I went round and did the windshield, and the pump ran, and I watched the numbers. When I came back round to her window she was looking straight ahead through the glass I'd cleaned.

"Your brother," she said.

"He's on the ward still. They say he can come home on Saturday, if he keeps on like this."

"Did they start him on anything?"

"A new inhaler. For every day. You breathe it through a tube."

"Good." The pump clicked off.

24.8 liters. 2,852 yen. She gave me three 1,000s and I gave her 148 yen, the 100 first and the small ones on top, and she put it in the tray under the radio without counting it.

Then she didn't go.

She started the car, and pulled forward off the pump, and turned in under the end of the canopy, and stopped by the air pump, by the wall where the light isn't so good. She switched the lights off and the pop-ups went down. She got out with a tire gauge in her hand and crouched by her front wheel.

Kubo-san put his can down on the counter. He went out across the forecourt, not fast, with his bad knee, lighting a cigarette as he went, and stood next to her by the air pump.

A truck came in at the same time, a big one, a refrigerated fish truck with its unit rattling, and the driver wanted 15,000 yen of diesel and his windshield done and the toilet key, and I did all of it, and in between I looked over. I couldn't hear anything. The fish truck's unit was going, and the compressor on the air pump came on and off, and they were twenty meters away, by the wall.

When I came round the front of the truck with the squeegee, she was at her second tire, and Kubo-san was standing over her with his cigarette held away from himself, talking. When I'd hung the nozzle back up, he was still talking. I'd never seen him talk that long. Then I had to fetch the toilet key from the office, and when I came out with it she was standing up with the gauge, and he was pointing the cigarette down the harbor road toward the silos, and she was looking down it.

She looked over at me. I was holding the fish truck's toilet key on its block of wood. I put the key down.

The fish truck left. It took six minutes by the office clock from when she pulled off the pump to when she got back in the car. The pop-ups came up. She went out onto the harbor road and back toward the bypass, and the sound went up, the way it does, and then it was gone.

Kubo-san came back across the forecourt. He finished the cigarette on the way and put it out on the sole of his shoe and put the end in his pocket, because he doesn't let anybody drop them on his forecourt, not even himself. He went past me into the office and picked up his coffee, and found it had gone cold, and drank it anyway.

I stood in the door.

"What did she want?"

He looked at the road for a while, where she'd gone.

"Sunday," he said. "Six a.m. Tanabe's. She says bring gloves."
