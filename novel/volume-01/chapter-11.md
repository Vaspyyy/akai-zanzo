# Chapter 11: The Other Tarp

I got to Tanabe's at three minutes to six with my shirt pocket still buttoned.

The office light was on. Tanabe-san sat at the desk in her coat, with the kettle ticking on the ring behind her and the first cigarette of the day in her mouth, not lit yet. I didn't knock. I unbuttoned the pocket and put the notes on the desk in front of her: a 5,000 and two 1,000s, folded small, still warm from being against me all night.

She didn't unfold them. She looked at me instead, at my jacket and the knees of my jeans, where the grit off the overpass deck had gone into the cloth.

"You look like something they'd pay me to take away," she said.

She got up and took the marker off its nail by the poster, and wrote on the plywood wall under the line from September, in the same square capitals: *MINUS 7,000. 29 OCT.* She put the notes in the desk drawer without counting them. Then she lit the cigarette.

"That's October," she said.

The red car came down the lane at six with its pop-ups down, the shells cracking under its tires, and went past the press and round the end of the Corolla stacks. I was in the back lot before she'd switched off.

She got out and looked at me. Not at my shoes this time. At my face.

"You haven't slept," she said.

"Some."

She got in on the left.

I set the seat, three clicks, and the mirrors, and the belt. I did the brake and the roll of the heel twenty times with the engine off before she could tell me to. Then we did the fence: first, second at the corner, twenty along the fence, brake level with the old tire, clutch, blip, lever, clutch up.

My feet knew the order. They did it a beat behind, every time, like a tape on a cheap player when the batteries are going. The first three runs jerked. The fourth didn't, and the fifth didn't. She said "Again" after both of them, the same as after the bad ones.

On the sixth, when I braked, the nose went left.

Not much. A hand's width, away from the fence. I caught it with the wheel and finished the downshift, and it jerked, because I'd been thinking about the wheel.

"Again."

Along the fence in second the wheel was heavier than it should have been at twenty. It wanted to go left, a little, all the time, and I'd been holding it straight without knowing I was doing it. At the tire I braked, and the nose went left again, further.

"It's pulling," I said. "Left. When I brake. And the wheel's heavy."

She looked at me. Then she said "Stop," and I stopped, and she got out.

She went round to the front left and crouched by it, with her gloved hand flat on top of the tire. Out of her jacket pocket came her gauge, a steel thing the size of a fat pencil, with a clip on it. She put it on the valve and read it, and read it again.

"Forward," she said. "The length of your arm."

I got back in and let it creep forward in first, about that far, and stopped it without a jerk, which nobody noticed. She was still crouched by the tire. The part that had been down on the shell had come up where she could see it, and she put her thumb on something in the tread.

I got out and looked over her shoulder. A screw. Just the head of it, bright, with a cross cut in it, sitting in the groove between two blocks of tread.

"That's my yard," Tanabe-san said from the forklift. She'd been sitting up there with her coffee the whole time. "My yard's made of those. I'm not paying for it."

"Not on the shell," Natsume said. She was looking at the ground.

"Concrete in front of the office." Tanabe-san put her cup down on the floor of the forklift, by her boots. "Boy. The trolley jack. Behind the tire wall, by the back fence. Bring it round."

Natsume got in behind the wheel. I went on foot, round the Corollas and then the short way, between the press and the end of the tire wall. Behind me she brought the car out in first, at walking pace, with the soft tire pressed flat at the bottom.

Behind the tire wall there's a strip of weeds and broken glass along the back fence, two meters wide, where nobody goes but me and the broom. The trolley jack lay on its side against the bottom row of tires, red, with a long handle and dead leaves in its wheels. Past it, at the end of the strip, against the fence, was the grey tarp.

The wind off the harbor had got under one corner. The rope on that side had worked loose, and the old tire that held the corner down had slid half off, so the canvas lifted, and lay down, and lifted again. Each time it lifted there was black paint under it, dull with dust, and the edge of a headlight lid, shut down flat into the nose.

I stood the jack up on its wheels and took hold of the handle.

Then I let go of it, and went to the corner, and lifted the tire off and put it in the weeds, and took the canvas in both hands.

It was heavy, and stiff with damp along the bottom, and under it the air smelled of wet canvas and old oil. It came up slowly and folded back on itself as I walked it along.

I knew the car before I had the canvas past the front wheel. I'd seen it in Nobu's magazines, and on the road a few times: Toyota's small one, with the engine behind the seats. Low and wedge-shaped, the nose coming to a blunt edge with no grille in it, the doors set well forward and a long flat deck behind them where the engine sits. An MR2. Black. The dust came off on my fingers in a grey film, and under it the black went deep.

I walked the canvas back along the side that faced me. The right side. The driver's side.

The front wheel on that side had been pushed back into its arch and turned in, the wrong way, and the tire on it was flat and cracked grey. The fender behind the headlight lid was creased in two places. Then came the door.

It wasn't a door any more. Something upright, not much wider than me, had gone into the middle of it, and the whole side had folded round that into a deep U, from the sill to the roof. I could have put my arm into the bottom of the U up to the elbow. The sill under it was bent up off the ground. Above it the roof came down into a kink, and the window was gone. Only a fringe of little cubes of glass was left in the rubber along the bottom, green at the edges where they'd broken.

I looked in through where the window had been.

The door came in as far as the middle of the seat.

The steering wheel was right there. It wasn't the one Toyota puts in. It was small, with a thick leather rim and three spokes, and on the horn button in the middle it said MOMO in white letters.

The windshield hadn't fallen out. It had gone white. There was a star in it, high up on the right by the pillar, and lines ran out from the star to every edge, so many that the only place you could see through was the bottom corner on the far side.

From the mirror in the middle of it hung a charm on a cord. A little brocade bag, the kind you buy at a shrine at New Year, red on one side and faded to pink on the other, where the sun had been at it through the glass. The wind came in at the window past me and turned it round on its cord. TRAFFIC SAFETY, in gold thread, down the front. On the back, smaller: SHIOMI HACHIMAN-GŪ. The shrine up the steps behind the covered street. Half the cars in Shiomi have one from there.

Behind the door, the vent where the engine breathes had gone in with it.

The canvas pulled at my arms whenever the wind got into it.

Then I heard an engine I knew.

At idle, just under a thousand, close behind me. Not moving.

I turned round with the canvas still up over my head.

The red car had stopped at the end of the tire wall, where the strip opened out toward the press. She was out of it. She stood in the open door with one gloved hand on top of it and the engine running.

She wasn't looking at me. She was looking at the car.

Her face didn't change. Her hand on the door didn't move. The wind took the canvas and I held on to it, and my arms started to shake, and I didn't put it down.

She got back in. She shut the door. The car went on, slowly, on the soft front left, past the office and the concrete in front of it, where Tanabe-san was standing now, and out through the gate without stopping. I heard the shells in the lane, and then I didn't.

I let the canvas down. I pulled it straight along the side, over the door and over the nose, and put the tire back on the corner with the loose rope pushed under it, because I didn't know how it had been tied.

Then I took the jack round to the office, because I'd been told to. Its wheels wouldn't turn on the shell, and I carried it by the handle with the other end dragging.

Tanabe-san was on the concrete with her coffee. I put the jack down at her feet.

"She's gone," I said.

"I've got eyes."

"Whose car is it?"

"Not my story."

She went into the office and shut the door. Through the window I saw her sit down at the desk under the Miyazaki poster and turn the radio on, and not drink her coffee.

At seven she opened the door.

"The Bluebird," she said. "Doors, glass, the lot."

* * *

I took the seatless Bluebird's doors off, all four, and the glass out of them. I dropped my ten-millimeter socket into the weeds three times and found it three times. Whenever a car went by on the harbor road I stopped with the wrench on a bolt until it had gone past the end of the lane. None of them was hers. At noon she put half her rice and a pickled plum on a plate on the office step and went back inside without a word, and I ate it standing up.

At one I swept the strip behind the tire wall. The tarp had new rope on it. Two more lines went across it now, over the roof and the nose, pulled tight and tied off low on the fence posts, and there was a fifth tire on top, in the middle. The canvas didn't lift anywhere. I swept round it.

Kubo-san's Cub came down the lane at ten past two with the toolbox roped on the rack and the three boxes from the back room roped on top of the toolbox, and a fourth, small and flat, on top of those. He stopped by the office and got off one leg at a time. He looked at me.

"Hm," he said.

He untied the boxes and gave them to me one at a time, and took the toolbox himself, and we went round the Corollas to my car.

He drained the water first, into a pan, green and clean enough to see the bottom through. "It goes back in," he said. Then the fan belt, and the pulley off the front of the crank, and the covers, top, middle and bottom, the ten-millimeter bolts in a row on the fender.

Behind the covers was the part we hadn't seen in October. Low down, the small toothed wheel on the end of the crank. Higher, two plain pulleys for the belt to run round, and between them, at the back, the water pump, with a crust of rust round its edge.

He put a finger on the left-hand pulley and spun it.

It went round with a noise like sand in a coffee grinder, and it rocked on its shaft. You could see it rock.

"There," he said.

The tensioner. He had it off in a minute and dropped it into my palm, and I spun it there and felt the grit going round inside it, through my fingers.

He gave me a flashlight out of the toolbox, a long rubber one, without saying what for, and I held it where his hands were.

"Kubo-san."

He had a scraper on the face where the old water pump had been, taking the last of the gasket off in grey flakes.

"The car under the grey canvas. At the back, by the fence."

"Light," he said. "Hold it still."

I held it still.

The new pump went on with the new gasket under it. He took the cam pulleys off and the toothed wheel off the crank, and drew the old seals out from behind them with a little hooked tool, and tapped the new ones in with a socket the same size as the seal, a light tap at a time, going round. The pulleys went back on.

Then the marks.

"Crank first," he said. He put the bar on the big bolt in the middle of the crank and turned it slowly, the way the engine goes, until a dot on the toothed wheel came round to an arrow cast into the metal behind it. On each cam pulley there was a letter stamped, I on one and E on the other, with a little mark beside it, and behind each pulley, on the plate, there was a notch. He put a spanner on the hex behind the first pulley and turned the cam until its mark sat on its notch. Then the other one.

The new tensioner went on next, with its spring pulled out long, and he did its bolt up to hold it there, back and out of the way. Then he took the new belt out of its bag. Black, every tooth square. He put it round the crank first, then the idler, then up and over the two cam pulleys, holding it tight between them with his thumb so that not one tooth could jump, and last of all round the tensioner. On that side it hung a little slack.

"Now." He held the bar out to me. "Two turns. The way it goes. Slowly."

I put the bar on the crank bolt. In October it had gone round heavy and easy, with that sigh like a bicycle pump, and never really fought me. Now it did. The bar went round a little way and then the engine leaned back against it, hard, and I leaned on it, and it went over the top and ran on easier, and then leaned back again. Four times in the two turns.

"That's the valves shut," Kubo-san said. "That's what it didn't have."

I stopped with the dot back on the arrow. He put the light on the cam pulleys. The I sat on its notch. So did the E.

"Keep going," he said. "Till I say."

I went on round, nearly two turns more, and he said "There" with the dot a little short of the arrow, on a second mark in the metal that I hadn't seen. He loosened the tensioner's bolt. The spring took up the slack with a small click, and the belt went tight all the way round, and he did the bolt up hard.

"Two more," he said. "And the bit."

I turned it on, past the arrow, round, past it again, round, and stopped with the dot on it. The I on its notch. The E on its. He pressed the belt with his thumb, halfway between the two cam pulleys, and watched how far it went. About a centimeter.

Covers on, pulley on, fan belt on. The coolant went back in out of the pan, and he topped it up with water from Tanabe-san's tap in a bucket, and then he stood and held the light on the new pump for a long time, looking for drips. There weren't any.

The key was in the ignition, where it had been since October. I looked at it.

"No," Kubo-san said. He hadn't looked up. "No battery. Plugs want looking at. That gasoline's been sitting in there since March."

"When?"

"Sunday."

He wiped his hands and opened the fourth box. Pads.

"Jack," he said.

I went round to the office and got it.

He put a plank under it on the shell and jacked the front up under the crossmember, and sent me to the pile by the press for two short blocks of old beam, black and heavy. He set one under each side of the frame, behind the front wheels, and let the car down onto them, and leaned on the fender to see that it didn't move.

"Never under a jack," he said.

I took the front wheels off, and he laid them flat under the sills. The old pad on the left had a bright line along the edge of its steel back, where it had started to touch. He ran his fingernail across the disc, over the grooves and the lip at the edge.

"They'll do," he said. "Till spring."

The new pads went in, one side and then the other, and I put the wheels back on, the nuts in a star, twice, the second time harder.

"Get in," he said. "Pump it."

I got in. The first time I pushed the brake it went to the floor, soft, with nothing under it at all. By the fifth it stopped high, and stayed there however hard I pushed.

"Now it'll stop," Kubo-san said.

He jacked it up off the timber, and I pulled the blocks out and the wheels from under the sills, and he let it down. The car settled back onto its own four wheels. It was a quarter to five. The sun was low over the town side, and the stacks laid long shadows across the weeds. The cat came out from under the Corollas and sniffed the front tire Kubo-san had just tightened, and went back to the passenger seat.

Kubo-san roped the toolbox back onto the Cub and went without saying anything else. I heard his note go up the lane.

Tanabe-san was standing in the office doorway with the marker, looking at the wall. She went in and wrote under the morning's line, and I could see it from the step: *MINUS 20,000. SUNDAYS.* A line under that. Under the line: *13,000.*

"Thirteen," she said. "Seven by the end of November. Six by the end of December. Customer."

She looked at it. Then she looked at the calendar on the nail beside the poster, a tire company's, still on October, with a girl in a swimsuit sitting on a tire. She lifted it off the nail and tore October away along the top and hung the rest back up. November. Under the girl the days were in rows of black numbers, the Sundays in red, and the 3rd in red as well, on the Friday.

She stood looking at it with the marker still open in her hand.

"Twenty-two," she said, to the calendar. "On Friday. That girl."

Then she put the cap on the marker.

"Sweep the office," she said.

Friday was the 3rd. Culture Day. No school.

I swept the office.

At home Mom was at the stove. She put rice in front of me and the end of last night's fish, and didn't ask about the stock at Kawamata's. I washed with the grey soap and lay down at seven and didn't hear her go.

At ten, at the station, there was a receipt from the parts dealer on the bypass lying on the counter by the till. FRONT PAD SET 5,800. Tax 174. 5,974.

I put a 10,000 down on it. Kubo-san opened the till and counted the change into my hand, four 1,000s, two 10s, a 5 and a 1, without looking at it, and wrote PAID across the receipt and gave it to me. It went in my jacket pocket with the other one.

* * *

On Monday I went from the pumps to school and held on until lunch with my thumbnail in my palm, and lost most of fifth period anyway.

When I got home the Sanko form was lying on the low table, straight along the edge.

Mom had done her part. Her name, in the careful hand she keeps for the ward office. The address. *Mother*, in the box that asked. Beside her name, a red circle with HAYAMA inside it, pressed on hard and straight. The line under that, for my name, was empty, and her pen lay across it.

Behind the fusuma she was asleep with the damp towel over her eyes.

I knelt at the table and wrote my name. *Hayama Daisuke.* I wrote it slowly, and the last stroke went off the end of the line anyway. I folded the form back along its creases and put it in the envelope it had come in, and put the envelope in my bag inside the English textbook, flat.

Sota had watched me do all of it from across the table, over his fractions, in Ken's jacket with the zip undone.

"Is that the factory?"

"Yes."

He did a fraction. Then: "Did it get the belt?"

"It's got the belt. On Sunday it gets a battery."

He put his pencil down. "Can I come?"

"If you can keep your mouth shut till Sunday."

He pressed his lips together and nodded at his fractions, and kept them pressed together until it was time to do the inhaler, and then he had to open them.

On Tuesday I put the envelope on Hotta-sensei's desk in the staff room before homeroom. He took the form out and looked at the seal, and at my name under it, and put it in a brown folder with SANKO written on the front. There were four others in it already.

"Good," he said.

I was at the door when he said, "Hayama."

I turned round.

"Ono-sensei tells me forty-one." He was already reading something else. "In mathematics. That's a pass."

I'd never had forty-one in mathematics. I went to homeroom and sat down, and Nobu looked at my face and said "What?" and I didn't tell him until lunch.

I worked the Tuesday night. At six on Wednesday the brown envelope was on the counter with HAYAMA on it in pencil: 44,800, for eight nights, all of it mine this time. I went from the pumps to school with it in the inside pocket of my school jacket, and all day I kept putting my hand on it through the cloth.

That afternoon nothing went on the shoe cabinet. That had been done on Saturday, in her notebook. I put the envelope in the dictionary, with the 1,200 and a 10,000 out of my pocket. There had never been that much in it.

* * *

At twenty to twelve on Thursday a bakery's white van came in for 2,500 yen of regular, and the driver wanted a receipt with the bakery's name on it, and spelled the name for me twice, and then a third time while I was writing it. After him there was nobody.

By midnight I knew what I was going to say.

I was going to say I was sorry. I'd never said it to her. I tried it on the pump island a few times, under my breath, between a kei truck and nothing, and every time it came out sounding like Sota when he's broken something and wants it to be over quickly.

Then I was going to ask. Whose car it was. Why she'd stood there with her hand on the door and looked at it, and not at me.

I had them in order. I said them to the canopy lights.

My cap was on the counter by the till, where I'd put it the Thursday before. Kubo-san had been dusting round it.

At one he took his radio into the back. At half past, one of the tubes in the canopy, the one over the second island, started to flicker, and went on flickering, so that the island went bright and dark and bright.

Kubo-san came out and stood under it with his cup and looked up. Then he went round the back and came out with the stepladder, and a new tube in a long cardboard sleeve. He set the ladder under the light and put his hand on it and looked down at his knee.

"I'll go up," I said.

He gave me the tube.

From the top of the ladder you can see the whole harbor road both ways, down to the light at the crossing and up to the turn for the bypass. I gave the old tube a quarter turn the way he said and it came out of its ends, grey at both tips, and I passed it down to him and he passed up the new one. I had my arms over my head, holding it into its ends, at two o'clock, when nothing came down the road from either direction.

The new tube caught. It blinked twice and came on, white and steady, and the island under me was light again all the way to its edges.

I came down. Kubo-san folded the ladder and carried it round the back.

At three I sat down on the stool in the office with the door open, where I could still see the road. When I shut my eyes it wasn't the questions I saw. It was the door, coming in as far as the middle of the seat. I opened them again and looked at the road.

At four the road should have been filling up with fish trucks. It stayed empty.

"Holiday," Kubo-san said from the office door. "No fish."

The cranes came out of the dark first, as they always do, and then the warehouses. At six I signed the sheet.

"Holiday," Kubo-san said again. "Sleep."

I didn't go home. I went up the bypass on the footpath.

* * *

The bypass was nearly empty. A holiday's worth of traffic: one car, then nothing, then two together. Behind the cranes the sky had gone from grey to a thin yellow along the bottom, with no sun in it yet, and my breath went out white in front of me and stayed there a moment before I walked through it.

I went up the stairs and out along the deck to the middle, to the railing over the curve, where I always stand.

The red car was down there.

It was on the far side, at the bottom of the other stairs, pulled onto the strip of concrete between the stairs and the road, just short of the new guardrail. Pop-ups down. Nothing coming out of the exhaust in the cold.

She was standing at the guardrail with her hands in her jacket pockets, looking along the curve.

I stood on the deck over her. She didn't look up.

I went back down the stairs I'd come up. At the bottom on the town side there's a drinks machine by the bus stop, with a dent in its side where somebody once kicked it for a can it kept. Its HOT row had come on with the cold weather, red labels under the buttons. Corn soup, 110. I had 123 yen in coins. I fed it a 1,000-yen note, and it spat the note out and took it on the second go, and I pressed the button twice. Two cans came down one after the other, loud in the empty morning. 780 came back into the tray. That left me 4,903, and the 56,000 at home in the dictionary.

They were too hot to hold. I held them anyway, one in each hand by the top and bottom, and went up the stairs and back across the deck and down the far side.

She heard me on the stairs. She turned her head when I was halfway down, and looked at me, and at what I was carrying, and then back at the curve.

I came down past her car. The front left tire was a new one. The shoulders were still black and sharp.

I stopped a meter from her and held one of the cans out.

"Happy birthday," I said.

She looked at the can. She didn't take it.

"Tanabe-san," she said.

It wasn't a question, so I didn't answer it.

She almost laughed. It got as far as her breath, one short puff of white, and stopped there.

She took the can and opened it, and I opened mine. We stood at the guardrail with them. In front of us the new section of rail ran along the outside of the curve, its bolts still bright, and the old rail went away from it on either side, duller, with rust coming through round its bolts. The foot of the stairs was beside us, square grey concrete. Low down on the side that faced the road was the pale place, where somebody had scrubbed.

The sun came up over the harbor while we stood there. The light came along the bypass flat and yellow and lit up the side of the stairs.

"Two years ago a friend of mine went into that pillar," she said. "I was in front of him."

She was looking along the road when she said it. Not at the pillar.

I looked at the pillar.

I opened my mouth, and shut it again. A truck came round the curve, the first one, a flatbed with nothing on it, and went past us close enough that the wind of it pulled at our jackets. Then it was gone toward the Higashi exit, and the road was empty again.

I drank my soup. Beside me she finished hers, and tapped the bottom of the can twice with two fingers, sharp, and tipped it back.

At the bottom of mine there were kernels that wouldn't come out. I tipped my head back and tapped the bottom of the can with my palm, and waited, and they came, two, then one more, and then no more however long I waited.

She stood with the empty can in her bare hand and the other hand still in her pocket.

"Get your car running," she said. "I'll teach you to drive it. In the yard."

Then she looked at me.

"You promised, kid."

"I know."
