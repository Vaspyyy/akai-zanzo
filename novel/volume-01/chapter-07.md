# Chapter 7: Clutch

At a quarter past two on Friday morning Kubo-san finished the cold coffee and stood the empty can on the counter, and I was still in the office door, waiting for the rest of it.

"Gloves for what?" I said.

"She didn't say."

"What did you tell her?"

"What she asked." He dropped the can in the bin under the counter. "Where you go on Sundays."

"She doesn't know Tanabe's."

He looked at me then, for the first time since he'd come back across the forecourt.

"Everybody round here with a broken car knows Tanabe's," he said.

In the back room the call-in show had gone to the commercials. A loan company, three girls singing a phone number. A taxi went by on the harbor road with its roof light off.

"Did you tell her about Pier 3?"

That one he took longer over. He picked up the station pencil and wrote the fish truck's diesel on the sheet, 15,000, and the time, and drew a line under it.

"You asked me where it was," he said. "Then you never asked me anything about it again." He put the pencil down. "Boys who don't go, ask."

So that was the six minutes by the air pump. Everything he knew, and the parts he'd worked out for himself. I waited to be angry with him. It didn't come.

"Is she going to teach me?"

"I don't know."

He went into the back room, and the show came back on: a man from Chiba who thought the weather forecast was being written by somebody in America.

Nothing came in until four, when the trucks started back. I did their windshields. In between I stood under the canopy and looked at my left hand. The Band-Aid had come off in the bath on Wednesday night, and I hadn't put a new one on. Across the heel of the palm, under the thumb, there was a pink line now, with a brown crust at the thumb end and shiny tight skin round it. When I made a fist it pulled. But it closed.

At six the day man came in with his newspaper under his arm. I signed the sheet and hung up the cap and walked to school.

My left hand was in my jacket pocket. It went into second, and the cut didn't say anything.

* * *

On Saturday school finished at half past twelve. On the way home I went into the hardware shop in the covered street behind the station, across from the bookstore. She'd said bring gloves. Tanabe-san had a box of fifty in the office, and I didn't want to turn up in hers.

The work gloves hung on a board inside the door. Yellow leather ones, stiff as cardboard, 780 yen. Long rubber ones for the fish market. A bundle of white cotton ones tied up in twelves with string, and next to the bundle, one pair on its own in a plastic bag. 98 yen.

I thought about hers. Black, with the knuckles cut out, gone soft and shaped to her fingers on the lever.

I bought the white ones. 98 yen, 100 with the tax. 487 left.

I knew Sota was home before I got to the stairs. His futon was hanging over the balcony rail on the fourth floor, the blue one with the trains on it, airing in the sun. Mom does that after anybody's been sick.

She was at the low table with her household notebook open and a long envelope from the hospital beside it, with the hospital's name across the top in green. She'd swapped her factory shift again. When I came in she closed the notebook with the envelope inside it.

"Wash your hands," she said. "Before you go near him."

I washed them at the sink, under the clinic paper on the side of the fridge. Sota was in the other room, on a cushion by the window, in his pajamas and two cardigans, his own and an old one of Aya's with the sleeves turned back. The new inhaler and its tube were on the windowsill next to his glasses case, lined up straight.

"Nii-chan." His voice was still hoarse at the edges. "I'm home."

"I saw your futon."

"Mom hit it with the carpet beater. Mrs. Arai looked out."

I dried my hands and went back to the table. Mom was putting the notebook in her bag.

"How much was it?" I said.

"It's paid."

She did up the clasp. Then she looked through the open fusuma at Sota, who was reading a comic with his lips moving, and kept her voice down.

"He asked me on the bus if the hospital was free," she said. "He said you told him it was."

I didn't say anything.

"I said yes." She stood up, with a hand on the table. "Then he asked how much the bus was."

She went behind the fusuma to sleep before the supermarket, and put the damp towel over her eyes. I lay down on the tatami in the kitchen in my uniform and was gone before I'd taken the second sock off.

At seven Sota woke me by sitting next to my head and waiting. He had the inhaler and the tube.

"I have to do it now," he said. "Morning and night. Even when I'm fine."

"I know."

"You can count."

He shook the inhaler hard, the way they'd shown him, and pushed it into the hole at one end of the tube. He breathed out, all the way, until his shoulders went down. He put his lips round the mouthpiece at the other end and pressed the inhaler once, and breathed in slowly, and there was a little flap inside the tube that ticked as the air went past it. Then he held it, with his eyes on me.

I counted on my fingers, where he could see them. He got to ten. He let it out through his nose.

"Again," he said. He shook the inhaler hard first, the same as before, and did the whole thing over, and I counted.

Then he got up and went to the sink and rinsed his mouth out and spat, carefully, in the middle of the sink.

"You have to rinse," he said. "Or you get mold in your mouth. The nurse said."

"Which nurse?"

"The round one upstairs. Not the downstairs one." He sat back down next to me. "The downstairs one only talked to me about candles."

He put the inhaler back on the windowsill, in its place, and then he took a folded card out of his cardigan pocket and gave it to me. It was a menu card from the ward, with a picture of a smiling rice ball at the top. On the back, in pencil, in his careful square writing, there was a list.

*TORA. CHAIRO. MARU. HANBUN (because of the tail).*

"For the cat," he said. "You can't keep calling it *the cat*."

"I don't call it anything."

"You have to ask it, though." He ran his finger down all four. "Which one. You can't just pick for it. It's its name."

I folded the card and put it in my jacket, in the pocket with the gloves.

Mom left at half past seven in her green vest. Aya came home at half past nine and put her ear to the fusuma and listened to Sota breathing for a long time without opening it, and then went and soaked her feet. I lay on my futon in the dark. On a night off my body still thinks it's at the pumps, and that night it thought so more than ever. At three I was still listening to the refrigerator. At half past four Mom's alarm went behind the fusuma, and I was already dressed.

She came into the kitchen with her hair half up and saw me sitting in the genkan doing my laces.

"Kawamata's?"

"His father does the stock early."

She looked at me a moment longer, holding the hair clip in her teeth.

"Take your jacket," she said, round it. "It's gone cold."

* * *

I went the back way, behind the fish-market sheds, so I wouldn't pass Kubo-san's station. At the silos the sun came up out of the water.

Tanabe-san was on her crate outside the office with a can of coffee, and the radio was on inside, low, a man reading the fishing forecast.

"Early," she said.

"Is she here?"

"Is who here?" She drank. The cigarette moved to the other corner of her mouth to let the can in. "Sit down. There's coffee in the office."

I didn't sit down. I walked as far as the end of the Corolla stacks and looked round the corner into the back of the yard, and it had changed since last Sunday. The engines on pallets that had stood there in a row had been moved. The flattened cars that had been lying about in ones and twos had been lifted and stacked along the far end, four high, a wall of them, white and grey and white. Across the open ground in the middle, in the dew on the weeds, there were the long double stripes of the forklift's tires, going back and forth, back and forth.

Somebody had cleared it. Not this morning. The dew was on the stripes too.

I looked at Tanabe-san. She was looking at the gate.

At six the radio in the office gave the time signal, the three short pips and the long one. On the long one I heard it in the lane.

It didn't sound the same in daylight. It sounded smaller, coming slowly over the shells and the potholes, in first gear, with the gravel popping under the tires. Then the red car came through the gate. Pop-ups down. It went slowly past the press and the tire wall and stopped by the office, and the engine ticked over for a second and stopped, and the dust it had brought in off the lane settled on its sills, white on red. I wanted to wipe it off. I kept my hands in my pockets.

She got out.

I'd never seen her in daylight. I'd seen her at night, under the canopy lights, through a car window, and under the hospital's lights in white. Now she had a dark jacket zipped to the chin and her hair tied back low, the same as always, and the black gloves with the knuckles out. Flat canvas shoes with thin soles. The right one was worn at the outside of the heel, a grey patch where the canvas had gone thin. From standing that heel on a floor mat and rolling the foot over onto the gas, I thought. Thousands of times.

Tanabe-san had stood up off her crate. She looked at the red car, and then at the driver.

"Two years, girl," she said.

"Tanabe-san."

She bowed. Not much. Tanabe-san took the cigarette out of her mouth, which I had never seen her do, and looked at the end of it, and put it back.

"The back's clear, Akane-chan," she said. "I put the Crowns along the end."

"Thank you."

Akane.

I'd been filling her car every Thursday since August. I'd heard Kubo-san call her "her" and "the girl" and nothing else, and I'd never asked, because I hadn't wanted to find out he didn't know either. Akane. I said it once, inside, and then I put it away somewhere, and didn't take it out again.

"Kid."

She was looking at my hands. I took them out of my pockets.

"Gloves," she said.

I got the paper bag out of my jacket and took them out of the plastic. White cotton, 98 yen, still creased in squares from the packet. She looked at them for about a second.

"Put them on."

I put them on. She got back in the car, and started it, and drove it slowly round the end of the Corolla stacks into the cleared ground, and I walked behind it in the stripes of the forklift, over the weeds, through the cold.

The back lot was about fifty meters long and half as wide. Flattened cars on three sides, three and four high, and the fence on the fourth: the Corolla stacks across the near end, where we'd come in; the new wall of Crowns across the far end; older stacks down the left side, vans and a Bluebird with no seats; and the yard's back fence down the right, corrugated steel. The ground was packed earth with crushed shell rolled into it, white and grey, and in the low places it was soft and dark where the dew had collected. You couldn't see the harbor road from in there. You couldn't see anything but cars that didn't go anywhere any more, and the sky, and the tops of the harbor cranes a long way off, and the forklift, which Tanabe-san had driven round after us and parked at the corner where we'd come in, and was sitting on, with her coffee, with the engine off.

The red car stopped in the middle, with its nose to the Crowns. The engine went off. She got out and walked round the front of it and opened the passenger door, and got in on the left, and shut the door.

I stood on the right side of her car with my hand on the roof.

"Get in," she said, through the window.

I got in.

It wasn't like my car. My car smelled of mold and cat and mackerel. This one smelled of almost nothing: clean vinyl, and a little gasoline, and the cold. The seat held me round the sides. The steering wheel was right there. The lever stood up out of the middle with a black knob on top, and the pattern on the knob was all there, every number. The pedals were down in the dark past my knees.

"Seat," she said.

I felt about under the front of the seat. There was a bar.

"Pull it up. All the way back."

I pulled it up and pushed back with my feet, and the seat ran back on its rails and hit the end.

"Left foot. Clutch to the floor."

I pushed. My leg ran out before the pedal did.

"Forward."

I pulled the bar again and brought the seat forward with my heels, a click at a time. At the third click the pedal went down under my foot to the floor and stopped there, and my knee was still bent.

"Knee."

"Bent."

"Arms out. Wrists on top of the wheel."

I put my arms out straight and laid my wrists on top of the rim, and to reach it my shoulders came forward off the seat.

"Shoulders back."

There was a lever on the side of the seat, by the door. I pulled it and leaned, and the backrest came up two clicks, and when I put my arms out again my wrists lay on the rim with my shoulders flat against the seat.

"Hands."

Nine and three. I put them there. The cotton was slippery on the rim, and I closed my hands a little more.

"Thumbs along the rim. Not round it."

The book had said why. A front wheel hits a hole, the wheel kicks back, and a thumb hooked round the spoke goes with it. She didn't say why. She waited until my thumbs were lying along the rim, and then she looked at the mirror.

"Mirror."

I reached up for it with my whole hand.

"Not the glass."

I took my fingers off the glass and moved it by the edges until I could see out of the back window, the whole of it, and the Corollas behind us. I wound my window down, with a handle, not a button, the same as in mine, and reached out to the right door mirror and pushed it with one finger. She wound her own window down and reached out to the left one with her gloved hand, and tipped it, a little at a time.

"Say when."

"There."

She wound her window back up.

"Belt."

I found the buckle myself and pulled the belt across and clicked it in. She'd done it for me, the last time I was in this car. I didn't know if she remembered. She was looking straight ahead through the windshield.

"That night," she said. "On the bypass. What did my feet do?"

I didn't have to think. I'd done it on my futon, and on the pump-island curb, and under the dinner table against Aya's shin.

"Every time you slowed down," I said. "Brake first, with the ball of your right foot. Then the clutch, all the way. Lever out. Then the heel goes over onto the gas, quick, more like a hiccup than a push. Lever into the lower gear. Clutch up. And your right foot never came off the brake while you did it."

"Under the overpass."

"Nothing. You'd already done it all. Right foot flat on the gas, all the way round. Left foot on the floor beside the clutch."

She didn't say yes or no.

"When we left Kubo's," she said. "Off the forecourt. What did my left foot do?"

I opened my mouth.

I could see the forecourt. The canopy lights going over us, and Kubo-san in the office door, and the harbor road coming. I'd been looking at the road coming. I hadn't looked down once.

"I don't know," I said.

"No."

She put her hands in her lap.

"Clutch in. Hold it. Neutral. Start it."

I pushed the clutch to the floor and held it there and wobbled the lever from side to side, the way the book said, to feel that it was loose in the middle. I turned the key. The starter went round once, and the engine caught, and went up, and came down, and sat there. The rev counter needle sat just under the one. Through the seat and the wheel and the pedal under my left foot I could feel all four of them going, small and quick and busy.

I'd sat in my own car with my hands at nine and three. It had never done this.

"First."

Forward and away. The lever went in with the small hard click, the same as mine.

"Handbrake down."

I let it down.

"No gas. Leave your right foot on the floor." She was looking out of the windshield. "Bring the clutch up until it moves. When it moves, stop your foot."

I brought the clutch up.

I'd thought about it for five weeks. I'd thought about every part of it except this part, because this part was the easy part. I'd watched a girl on the driving-school hill get it wrong five times in a row and thought: how? My foot came up the way you let go of a light switch. Somewhere about halfway the car jumped. The nose dipped, the whole thing lunged half a meter at the Crowns, my head went forward and back, and the engine coughed once, deep, and stopped.

Two red lights had come on in the dash, a battery and an oil can. Outside, a gull. The tick of something hot under the hood. Behind us, on the forklift, Tanabe-san's voice:

"One."

"Clutch in," Natsume said. "Neutral. Start it. Handbrake."

She'd pulled the handbrake on herself, while I was still looking at the red lights.

The second time I went slowly. So slowly that my leg started to shake, holding the pedal, from the thigh down. Then, near the top, the engine note went down a little, and the car under me gave a small heave, like it had remembered something. It frightened me. My foot jumped off the pedal. The car leapt and stalled harder than the first time, and the belt caught me across the chest.

"Two."

"Heel on the floor," Natsume said.

"It is."

"It's in the air. Your whole leg's lifting." She still hadn't looked at my feet. "Heel on the floor. Move the front of the foot. Not the leg."

I put my heel down on the mat, hard, and moved only the front of my foot, which is a different thing. I'd never known it was a different thing. It felt like holding a pencil with the wrong hand. I got it up near the top and felt the heave again, and I held my foot there. I thought I did. The car started to shake, a fast small shiver through the seat, and the needle sank, and the note went down and down, and the car rocked forward a finger's width and shuddered and stopped. When I looked down, my foot was higher on the pedal than where I'd stopped it.

"Three."

"You stopped," Natsume said. "Then you kept going. It told you to stop."

"It didn't say anything."

"It shook."

The fourth time I wanted it so much I didn't look where the lever was. I pulled it forward, not far enough across, and brought the clutch up as slowly as I could. The car moved this time. It moved about a hand's width, very heavily, with a long low hammering coming up through the floor, and died.

"Which gear?" she said.

I looked down. It was in third.

"Four," Tanabe-san called.

My face had gone hot under the cold. Natsume sat with her gloved hands in her lap and waited.

Clutch in. Neutral. Key. Handbrake.

First gear. Across and forward. I looked at it with my eyes and felt it with my hand.

Heel on the floor. The front of the foot. Up, slowly. Slower than I thought. The note went down, and the car heaved, and this time I made my foot stop and stay stopped, with my leg aching, and then let it up a hair more, and a hair more.

Something crackled under the front tires. Once, and then again. A clump of foxtail by my door went slowly backwards past the window, and I understood that we were moving, with no gas and my foot only half up, on our own, and the Crowns were in front of us, and nobody had told me what came next.

I stamped on the brake. The car stopped dead and shook all over and stalled, a meter and a half from where it had started, because I hadn't put the clutch down.

"Five," said Tanabe-san.

Natsume didn't say anything at all. She reached across and pulled the handbrake on.

I looked at my hands on the wheel, the white cotton gone grey already across the palms from the rim.

"Again," she said.

* * *

Some of them I can't tell apart now. I could then. Every one had its own shape.

After that she let me have gas. "A little," she said. "Up to two. Then the clutch." The pedal under my right foot was tied straight to the engine, with nothing soft anywhere in between, and I touched it and the needle was already at four and the engine was screaming between the stacks. I let the clutch up to get away from the noise, and the back wheels spun on the shell and threw it rattling against the cars behind us. I took my foot off the gas in fright with the clutch still half up. The car fell on its face and died.

"Mind my Corollas!" Tanabe-san shouted.

I turned round in the seat. She was standing up on the forklift step with her coffee held out to one side.

"Six."

"Two," Natsume said, when I turned back. "Not four."

I started it again, and put it in first, and touched the gas up to two, and brought the clutch up. The car squatted down on its back wheels and pulled against them, and a hot smell came up from somewhere, and it stalled.

"Handbrake," she said.

It was up. She'd pulled it on while I was turned round looking at the forklift, and I hadn't seen her do it.

"You put it on."

"Yes."

The next one moved. I got two meters, and this time I didn't brake. I was so pleased about the two meters that I went for second, the way I'd done it on my futon a thousand times, clutch down, lever straight back. Clutch up. In second at walking pace the car started to hammer, a big slow shaking right through the floor and the wheel and my teeth, and I kept my foot where it was because I'd got it into second and I wasn't going to give it back, and it hammered harder and died.

"It shook," she said.

"I know."

"You felt it shake. You kept going."

I don't remember the one after that at all. Tanabe-san said "Nine," and sounded pleased about something. The cat had come out of the weeds by then and was sitting on a tire at the end of the stack, watching us with its tail wrapped round its feet, the half of it there was.

Then my left leg went. I'd been holding the clutch near the top for so long, waiting for the heave, that my calf started to shake on its own, a fast shiver I couldn't stop, and the shiver went down into the pedal. The car went forward in three hops. One. Two. Three. On the third one, it died.

"Ten," Tanabe-san said, flatly. Then, louder: "I had you down for nine!"

I turned my head. "Had me down for what?"

"I bet myself a coffee. Now I owe myself." She got down off the forklift, slowly, and stretched her back with both hands. "Don't look at me. Look at your feet."

The cat got off its tire and went back into the weeds.

I turned back round. My hands were wet inside the gloves. I could feel my heart in the cut. The sun had come up over the stacks and was in the mirror, and I didn't move the mirror.

I didn't know what a clutch cost. I knew it wasn't mine.

"Clutch in," Natsume said. "Neutral. Start it."

I started it. I put it in first without looking, and it went into first, and I brought the clutch up fast and gave it gas all at once, to make it go, to make it just go, because I was sick of the shaking and the hopping and the voice saying numbers. The car bucked, and her hand went out flat against the dash, and the engine stalled with a bang from somewhere underneath, and we rocked forward and back on the springs, twice, and were still.

Nobody said anything.

The red lights. The gull. The hot tick under the hood. Somewhere a long way off, a fish truck going down through its gears on the harbor road.

"Eleven," Tanabe-san said, from wherever she was. "Fall down seven times, get up eight, they say." The lighter clicked. "Nobody says anything about eleven."

I sat with my hands on the wheel. My right knee had started going up and down, and I put my hand on it, and it stopped.

Natsume reached across and pulled the handbrake on, and took her hand back.

"Feet on the floor," she said. "Both of them."

I took my feet off the pedals and put them flat on the floor.

"Shut your eyes."

I looked at her.

"Shut them."

I shut them. It was red behind my lids, from the sun.

"Start it."

I found the clutch with my left foot and pushed it to the floor, and found the lever with my hand and felt it loose in the middle, and turned the key. The engine caught, and went up, and came down, and sat there, small and quick and busy, under the seat and under my hands.

"First," she said. "Handbrake down. No gas."

The click. The handbrake.

"Clutch up. Slowly. Don't move. Tell me when it bites."

My heel on the floor. The front of my foot. Up. Up.

I listened to the engine. I'd done that every night since August. I could tell an LPG taxi from a diesel van before either of them was off the road. With my eyes shut there was no stack coming and no numbers, only the engine, and somewhere near the top of the pedal its note dropped. Not much. The smallest step, like someone in the next room had leaned on a table. And the seat pressed into my back, as far as a coin is thick.

"There," I said.

"Hold it."

I held it.

"Open your eyes."

I opened them. The stack was where it had been. Nothing had moved. The note was still down that one small step, and the car was leaning on my foot, waiting.

"A little gas," she said. "Up to two. Then let it up. Slowly."

I put my right foot on the gas. Up to two, the needle steady on the two. I let the clutch up, slowly, and slower.

The shell crunched under us.

We moved. Not a jump, not a hop. We rolled forward off the spot where we'd been for twenty minutes, and the engine's note went with us instead of dying under us, and I let the clutch up the rest of the way and we were going, across the lot, on our own.

"Steer."

I steered. The Crowns came. The wheel was heavy, so heavy at that speed that I had to haul it round with both arms, slowly, and the nose came away from the stack a little at a time, and the corner of the yard went past.

"More gas. Not much."

The needle went past two. The crunch under us went quicker and thinner. The speedometer, which I'd forgotten was there, said twenty.

"Second."

I'd gone for second too early once already. This time she'd said it. Clutch down. Lever straight back into second, all the way, the small hard click, home. Then the clutch up. The car gave one nod, and the needle dropped back below two, and nothing hammered. The engine held one low steady note and went on.

We went round the back lot in second gear.

The wall of Crowns went past on my side, white and grey and white. At the corner I turned the wheel hand over hand, and it came round lighter than it had at the start, now that we were going. Then the old stacks down the long side, a van with its sliding door still on. Then the corner and the Corollas, and through a gap between two of the stacks, for a second, a hump of grey vinyl over the weeds beyond, with a tear in it. Mine. Then the forklift, empty, and Tanabe-san standing next to it with her cigarette, turning to watch us go by, and the tire where the cat had sat. Then the fence, the long way, and the Crowns again. Twenty kilometers an hour, maybe a bit more, the needle creeping up toward two, and the same low note the whole way. Under us the shell went on crunching, all the way round, and it was the best sound I had ever heard.

"Breathe," she said.

I hadn't been. I breathed.

We came round to where we'd started.

"Clutch. Brake."

Clutch down. Brake. The car stopped, gently, nose down a little, and the engine went on running, with nothing to do.

"Neutral. Handbrake."

I put it in neutral and pulled the handbrake on, and took my feet off the pedals, and the engine sat there ticking over under us, just under the one.

She didn't say good. She didn't say anything for a while.

"Switch it off," she said.

I switched it off.

It was very quiet in there with the engine off. I could hear the radio in the office, a long way behind us, and the shell under the tires settling.

She didn't look at me while she said it. She looked out of the windshield.

"Here," she said. "Sundays. Six o'clock. Until you have a license, you drive here, and nowhere else."

"Yes."

"Not the harbor road. Not the lane. Not round the block once at night when nobody's looking." She stopped. "Never the bypass."

"Yes."

"Lie to me once and we're done."

I didn't say yes to that one straight away. I wanted to say it right.

"Here and nowhere else," I said. "Never the bypass. I won't lie to you."

She looked at me then. Not long. About as long as she'd looked at the white gloves.

"Out," she said.

I got out. My legs had gone soft at the knees, and I stood holding the top of the door until they came back. I took the gloves off. The palms were grey from the wheel and the left one had a small brown mark on it, in the middle, where the crust on the cut had come away. I put them in my pocket with Sota's list.

She came round the front of the car and got in on her side. She pulled the bar under the seat and pushed it back with her feet, one click, two, and stopped there, one short of the end. She put the backrest down two clicks. She reached up and put the mirror back with two fingers on its edges, a little to the left. She wound her window down and moved the right door mirror with the side of her gloved hand. Then she leaned all the way across, wound the passenger window down, and moved the left one, and sat back and looked at it, and leaned across and moved it once more.

Everything back where it had been, as far as I could tell. I watched her do all of it and didn't help.

"Why?" I said.

She started the car.

"Six o'clock," she said. "Next Sunday."

The pop-ups stayed down. She drove out round the end of the Corolla stacks, slowly, in first, and I walked after her as far as the corner, where the forklift was. From there you could see past the office to the gate. Tanabe-san was standing by the forklift with her cigarette.

The red car went past the press and the tire wall. At the gate it stopped. Her window didn't come down. Then she went out into the lane, over the shells, and I heard her go up through second and into third on the harbor road, and the sound went up, the way it does, and then I couldn't hear it any more.

Tanabe-san went on watching the gate after there was nothing in it.

"Eleven," she said.

"I know."

I was looking at the gate as well.
