# Chapter 9: Type R

Sota's car weighed less than a can of coffee. He weighed it on Mom's kitchen scale on Thursday evening, while she was frying eggs for the rice, and wrote it down on the back of his fractions: 118 grams, with the batteries in. A can of coffee is 190. It says so on the can.

It sat on the low table between his homework and the soy sauce. Blue, about as long as my hand, four fat little tires, a clear plastic body with a yellow lightning bolt down each side. Underneath: two AA batteries, a motor the size of the end of my thumb, and at each corner a little white plastic wheel lying flat, sideways, not touching the ground. The front tires pointed straight ahead. I pushed them sideways with my finger. They didn't go.

Aya had bought it for him in June, for his birthday, and said she'd only bought it because it was on sale.

"Saturday," Sota said. "Two o'clock. At Fujita's. There's a race."

"Fujita's?"

"The hobby shop. Over the shoe shop, in the covered street." He pushed the car along the table with one finger, and the gears ticked. "It's a hundred yen to go in. Ken says you can't win anything without a tuned motor. But you can go in."

"Who's Ken?"

"From the hospital. His sister had the bed next to mine. Mai-chan. She's five. Ken came every day at visiting time, and he knows everything about motors. He's got a tackle box."

I remembered the bed by the window, and the little girl asleep in it. I hadn't seen a boy.

"Mom's at the factory till two," Sota said, to the car. "Then she sleeps. And Aya's working."

Mom turned the gas off under the eggs. She didn't look round.

Saturday after school was the only part of my week with nothing in it at all.

"Two o'clock," I said.

Mom went to the supermarket at half past seven. At a quarter past nine, when I went to put my shoes on, there were two onigiri on the shoe cabinet, wrapped in plastic film, on the place where I leave her half. There was nothing written next to them. I put them in my jacket, in the other pocket from the gloves.

I ate the first onigiri at midnight. Salted plum. I was halfway through the second, by the pump island, when the pop-ups showed a long way down the harbor road, and I heard her change down for the station, and I put my hand with the onigiri in it behind my back.

She stopped at the pump and wound the window down.

"Regular. Fill it up."

I filled it. 25.2 liters, at 115, 2,898 yen. I did the windshield one-handed. She gave me three 1,000-yen notes, and I gave her a 100-yen coin and two 1s, still one-handed, the other behind me holding the rice.

She looked at my shoulder, where the arm went round the back.

"Eat it," she said.

I brought it round. Half an onigiri, with the film still round the bottom of it. I ate it, standing at her window under the canopy lights, with her looking out through the windshield at the road.

"It's dinner," I said, when I'd swallowed. "Or breakfast. I don't know which it is at two."

"I don't eat breakfast."

"None?"

"Coffee."

She put the change in the tray under the radio, without counting it, and wound the window up, and went. I stood there with the plastic film in my fist.

Coffee.

* * *

Fujita's was up a narrow flight of stairs beside the shoe shop, and on Saturday at ten to two you could hear it from the street: a high thin whining, lots of it at once, and under it a rattle, plastic on plastic, and boys shouting.

Upstairs it was one long room with the windows painted over. Model kits in boxes went up the walls to the ceiling, and along the counter at the back hung little plastic bags of parts, each with a price on a sticker in marker. 280. 350. 600. The middle of the floor was the track: white plastic on short legs, three lanes side by side with walls as high as a matchbox, going round a long loop with a tight corner at each end. Halfway along the near straight the lanes went up a ramp and over a hump, a bridge, and came down the other side swapped round, inside to outside. Ken explained later that this was so nobody could say his lane was shorter. Nobody had asked him.

There were about twenty kids round it, a few fathers, and a man in an apron with FUJITA on it at the start with a stopwatch. Sota paid him the hundred yen himself, ten 10-yen coins counted out of a film canister into the man's hand.

"Sota!"

A boy pushed through from the far side of the track. Sota's age, bigger, with his hair cut so short you could see his scalp, and a green plastic tackle box held against his chest. He stopped in front of us and looked at me, up and then down.

"This is my brother," Sota said.

"The one who rode in the ambulance," the boy said. "I'm Ken. Sasaki Ken." He looked at Sota's car, in Sota's hands. "Is it still stock?"

"I put the stickers on."

"Stickers aren't parts."

He opened the tackle box on the floor to show us. Every compartment had something in it: little metal wheels, motors with colored labels, springs, a tiny screwdriver. He named them the way Nobu names horsepower, fast and happily and to nobody in particular. His own car was red, with holes cut in the body to make it lighter, and a little metal wheel at each corner instead of a white plastic one.

"Those are the rollers," Sota told me, pointing at the flat wheels on his. "They run along the wall. That's how it gets round."

"It can't steer," I said.

"Nobody's can," Ken said. "It's a Mini 4WD."

Before the race you could run your car for practice. Sota stood at the start with it held in a lane, the wheels screaming in the air, and let it go. It went much faster than something that size had any right to, rattling round the first corner on its little white wheels, down the far side, round the second corner and up the ramp. At the top of the hump it left the track, all of it, nose up, and came down on its back wheels with a click, and ran at the next corner with its nose still up, and its front rollers climbed the wall and went over. It went end over end across the carpet and stopped upside down against a man's shoe, with its wheels still going.

"Course-out," Ken said. "It's too fast for its rollers."

Sota went and got it from beside the shoe. The man didn't look down.

It flew off twice more before the race, at the same corner. The one after the bridge.

The race was in heats of three, three laps each. In Sota's heat the man in the apron said "Ready," and on "Go" the boys let go. A little girl in a white cotton mask, standing on a stool at the far side of the track, yelled "Go!" after him, at the top of her voice, every time. Sota's car was in front out of the first corner. It was in front at the bridge. It came off the top nose-up and went over the wall at the corner, and the other two went past underneath it while it was still in the air.

Sota picked it up and switched it off. He didn't say anything. He came round the track to me holding it in both hands, and stood next to me, and watched the next heat with his mouth shut tight, the same as when he's waiting to see if a cough will stop on its own.

Ken won his heat by half a lap and came over to tell us what Sota's car needed, and what each part cost on the counter.

"It's not the corner," I said.

They both looked at me.

"It's the bridge." I took Sota's car out of his hands and turned it over. "It lands on its back wheels with the nose up. It gets to the corner before the front's come down, so the rollers hit the wall too high."

"So you need a brake," Ken said.

"So the front needs to be heavier."

"There's weights," Ken said. "On the counter. Three hundred yen."

I had 487 yen in my pocket. I took out two 10-yen coins and showed Sota.

"Can you put those on?"

He looked at the coins, and at me, and then he understood, and his whole face went up behind his glasses.

The man in the apron gave us a strip of tape off a roll by the till without asking what it was for. I laid the two coins flat under the front of the car, one on top of the other, between the front wheels, as low as they'd go, and Sota taped them down and pressed the edges flat with his thumbnail. The car weighed more in my hand. About as much as two 10-yen coins more.

Ken watched with his tackle box shut. "That's not allowed," he said. "Only real parts. Coins aren't parts."

"This isn't a real race any more," Sota said. "We already lost."

He took it to the start and switched it on and put it down.

It went round the first corner and the second, a bit slower than before, I thought, and up the ramp. It came off the top. The nose went up, a little, and came straight down again, and all four wheels hit the track at once with a flat click, and it ran into the corner with the front rollers down where they should be, rattling along the wall. It went round the whole track, and over the bridge again, and round that corner again.

Sota made a noise. He put his hands over his mouth.

It went round a third time. Nobody was racing it. It just kept going, round and round on its own, with twenty yen taped to its chin, and the boys at the start had to wait for it to finish with their own cars in their hands.

"It doesn't even know where it's going," Sota said, behind his hands. "It can't steer, Nii-chan. It just goes till the wall tells it."

That was when I laughed. I don't know why that was the thing. I laughed out loud in Fujita's, with a hand on the edge of the track, and Sota took his hands off his mouth and laughed too, the real one, the one that comes up from the bottom. The car went round again. Ken stared at us. Then his mouth started to go at one corner, and he turned round so we wouldn't see.

"You're the big brother."

A woman had come down the side of the track, holding the girl from the stool by the hand. Permed hair down to her shoulders, a brown cardigan with the cuffs pushed up, and red knuckles, the kind you get from hot water. The last time I'd seen her, her head had been back against the wall of the children's ward and her mouth had been open.

"You came at visiting time," she said. "In your uniform. You didn't bring him anything."

"You were asleep."

"I'm always asleep in that chair. I wake up when anybody new comes in, and I'm back asleep before they've noticed." She smiled. "Sasaki. Ken's mother. And this is Mai."

The girl looked up at me. I'd only ever seen her lying down, with her eyes shut. Awake she was mostly cheeks. They were round and shiny, rounder than a five-year-old's ought to be, and she'd pulled the mask down under her chin to get a lollipop into one of them.

"It's the medicine," Sasaki-san said, before I'd said anything. "She eats like a sumo wrestler on it, and she catches everything going, so it's the mask. She's home now, till the next time." She said it the way you'd say it might rain on Tuesday. "How's your mother?"

"She's all right."

"Hayama-san came every afternoon in her work clothes, straight from the factory, and sat in that chair, and went to sleep sitting up with her bag on her knees. I said to her, you go home, I'm here anyway, I'll keep an eye on him. She said thank you. She didn't go." She looked at Sota, who was standing at the bridge with Ken, watching his car go round. "He's got his color back."

"Yes."

She let go of Mai's hand and opened her purse, and found a pen, and a receipt from the till here, and wrote on the back of it, on her knee, in round handwriting that leaned backward, and held it out.

"That's us," she said. "For nights. My husband drives a taxi."

I took it. It said SASAKI, and a number, and under that, smaller, *Kazue*. Under that: *For nights.*

"Give it to your mother," she said. "Not to Sota. He'll lose it." She picked Mai up onto her hip. "Ken! Five minutes."

Ken didn't hear her. Sota's car came off the bridge again, nose down, and went round the corner, and round, and round. Mai pointed at it with her lollipop and yelled "Go!"

On the front of the receipt it said FUJITA in purple ink, and the date, and AA ALKALINE ×2, 380.

I put it in my jacket pocket, with the gloves and Sota's list of cat names and the rubber tooth.

At home Mom got up at six and washed her face and came into the kitchen to start the rice. I gave it to her at the sink.

She read the front first, the batteries and the price. Then she turned it over and read the name, and the number, and the two words under them.

"Mai-chan's mother," I said. "From the next bed. At the hobby shop."

She dried her hands on the towel, without putting the receipt down. Then she went to the little shelf by the calendar, and lifted the phone off the phone book, and opened the phone book near the front, and put the receipt inside, and shut it, and put the phone back on top.

She went back to the rice. She didn't say anything about it, then or at dinner.

Sota ate with his car beside his bowl, upside down, so the coins showed. I didn't ask for my twenty yen back, and he didn't offer it.

* * *

On Sunday at six the sky behind the cranes was only going pale, and the red car came slowly down the lane over the shells with its pop-ups down. I was in the back lot already, with my white gloves on, hopping from foot to foot in the cold, and my breath showed.

She got out, and looked at my feet before she looked at anything else.

My sneakers. The right one held together with Kubo-san's silver tape, the left one going the same way at the toe. Fat rubber soles, gone smooth underneath.

"Those are too thick," she said.

"They're the ones I've got."

She didn't say anything else about them. She drove the car to the end of the lot with its nose to the Corollas, and got out, and got in on the left.

I set the seat and the mirrors and the belt. She didn't watch me do it now.

"Engine off," she said. "Right foot on the brake. The ball of your foot. The left half of the pedal."

I put it there.

"Harder."

I pushed. The brake was stiff with the engine off, and went down slowly, a little.

"Harder. Like you mean to stop from a hundred."

I stood on it, as hard as my leg would push with the knee still bent where the three clicks put it. The pedal sank until it was level with the gas, the two of them side by side at the same height. That was what the hundred was for.

"Now roll your heel over. Don't lift the front."

I rolled my foot. The ball stayed on the brake, and the side of my foot, down by the heel, came over onto the gas pedal. Or it was meant to. My sole was so wide that it caught the edge of the gas pedal and pushed it down sideways, and slipped off.

"Again."

That was what she meant about the shoes. In hers you'd feel the edge of the pedal through the canvas. In mine I was feeling for it through a centimeter of rubber.

I did it twenty times with the engine off. Then she said "Start it," and I did it in neutral, with the engine running and my foot hard on the brake, rolling the side of my foot onto the gas and off, and the needle went up.

"Three," she said. "No more."

Up to three. Off. Up to three.

"Now it moves," she said. "First. Then second, along the fence. Twenty. When you're level with the tire, brake. Clutch in. Blip. Into first. Clutch up. On the bypass it's four to three. Here it's two to one. The feet are the same."

She'd moved last week's old tire to the fence, halfway along.

I pulled away, clean, the shell crunching, and went into second at the corner, and turned along the fence with the needle on one and a half and the speedometer at twenty. The tire came. I braked, clutch down, lever out of second, and put it into first, and brought the clutch up.

I'd forgotten the blip, because I'd been thinking about the brake. The car lurched when the clutch came up and slowed all at once, the engine dragged up to first gear's speed by the wheels, and both our heads went forward.

"Blip," she said.

"I know."

The second time I blipped too early, before the clutch was down, and the lurch was the same. The third time I got the order right, brake, clutch, blip, lever, clutch, and the blip took my foot off the brake. I felt the nose rise and dip again.

"You nodded," she said.

"What?"

"The car. It nodded. Your brake came off when you blipped." She was looking straight ahead. "The brake doesn't move. Only the side of the foot."

After that the car told me through the seat before she did. A blip too small and it jerked, like a rope pulled tight. Too big and it surged. Tanabe-san sat on the forklift at the corner with her coffee and didn't say anything this time. The sun got up over the Crowns, into my eyes on every run along the fence.

Near the end, on a run I didn't count, I braked level with the tire and pushed the clutch down and rolled the side of my foot onto the gas, a short hard roll, and while the needle was still up I put the lever into first, and it went in, all the way home, and I let the clutch up. And the car didn't jerk. It gave a little shrug, that was all, and went on slowing in first with the engine humming, and the nose didn't come up because my foot had stayed on the brake the whole time.

I didn't breathe. I didn't look at her.

"Again," she said.

The next one jerked. So did the one after that.

At a quarter to seven she said "Enough," and I stopped by the Corollas and switched it off, and sat there with my right foot aching along the outside, from the little toe to the heel, where it had been rolling. I hadn't known you had muscles there.

We changed sides. She put everything back the way it had been, the seat one click short of the end, the mirrors with the side of her glove. Then she sat with the engine running and the door still open and looked at my feet again, at the silver tape, and shut the door, and went.

When the red car had gone through the gate, Tanabe-san got down off the forklift and looked at the plywood wall through the office window, without going in.

"Six," she said. "One more Sunday. Then you're a customer."

"I'm still paying you after that."

"Customers pay. That's what makes them customers." She drank the last of her coffee. "Ishiguro rang. By the timber yards. He's got a Roadster in with the front burned out. Engine's gone, the roof's good. Frame and all. He'll do it for five thousand, because he owes me."

I looked through the gap in the Corolla stacks at the grey vinyl over the weeds, and the tear the cat used for a door.

"When?"

"He keeps it till Monday. After that he'll sell it to the first man with six." She threw the can in the drum by the office step. "I'm not driving to Ishiguro's for a roof. You've got legs."

At ten that night, at the station, there were three cardboard boxes on the shelf in Kubo-san's back room, next to the radio, one long and flat and two square. The flat one said MAZDA in blue on the end. On top of them, held down by the tide table, there was a receipt from a parts dealer on the bypass, with every line written out.

TIMING BELT 4,600. TENSIONER 5,200. IDLER 3,900. WATER PUMP 10,800. OIL SEAL ×3, 2,100. GASKET 400. Then a line, and 27,000. Then the tax. 27,810.

On Friday night Kubo-san had said, without looking up from his paper, "Twenty-eight. About." So I'd brought 33,000 from the dictionary: 28,000 for him, and 5,000 for Ishiguro, since I'd be going from the pumps to school and from school to the timber yards. I put two 10,000s and eight 1,000s on the counter. He gave me 190 back out of the till, a 100, four 10s and a 50, and wrote PAID on the receipt in pencil, and gave it to me.

"The labor," I said.

"What labor?"

"Your afternoon."

He looked at the boxes.

"Next Sunday," he said. "After two." He went into the back and turned the radio up. A woman from Kobe was talking about her son, who wouldn't come home.

That left 43,200 in the dictionary and 5,657 in my pocket. At half past two I went into the back room while Kubo-san was out at a truck and opened the flat box. The belt lay coiled in a plastic bag, black and new, every tooth square and there. I counted forty before I lost my place, and put it back.

* * *

On Monday I told Nobu at lunch that I needed help carrying something after school, and he said "What?" and I said "A roof," and he said "Yes," before he'd asked anything else.

We took the bus out toward the river, the Sanko bus, 160 yen each. Nobu paid his own. He talked the whole way: his father had told him that if his midterm marks were bad again there'd be no license money, half or otherwise, and a girl in 3-C had looked at him twice in the corridor, or at the fire extinguisher behind him. I let him talk. I'd come from the pumps, and the bus was warm.

Ishiguro's was a body shop in a tin shed between two timber yards, with the smell of cut pine coming over the fence on both sides. The Roadster was out at the back, a white one, black all down the front, with the smell of burned plastic still in the paint. The top was already off, folded on a pallet, frame and all, in a blue tarp tied round with string.

Ishiguro was a thin man with a pencil behind each ear. He undid the string so I could look. The vinyl was whole, black, with no tears and no cracks at the folds, and the plastic rear window was clear. Not yellow, like mine. You could see the timber stacks through it.

"Wiring fire," he said. "Under the dash. The man got out all right."

I gave him the 5,000 note. That left 497 in my pocket.

"Tanabe-san's boy," he said, counting it. "Tell her that's us square."

The top was heavier than it looked, and longer. The frame stuck out from the tarp at both ends. We carried it between us with one end each, like a stretcher, along the road beside the river, and when the bus came the driver looked at us through the door and shut it again.

"An hour," I said. "If we walk."

Nobu looked at the top, and at me.

"What is it off?" he said. "You've got to tell me what it's off."

"A Roadster."

"I can see it's off a Roadster. I can see the frame. What's it going on?"

"A Roadster."

He walked for a while with his end. He kept changing hands.

The road went along the river toward the harbor, past the timber yards and a cement works and then a long grey fence with a gate in it, and over the gate, in red, SANKO BRAKE INDUSTRIES. We got there at a quarter to five, with the sun low and orange behind the timber yards. A bell went somewhere inside, and a minute later the men started coming out through the gate on foot and on bicycles, in grey jackets with SANKO in red over the pocket, with their hands grey in the creases. Somebody was laughing. The smell came out after them, hot and sharp, the smell of a car that's braked too hard down a long hill, except it was everywhere and nobody was braking.

The men had to go round us. We stood there in the road with the roof between us until they had.

"That's your place," Nobu said. "Did they write?"

"Last week."

"And?"

"April."

He stopped. He put his end down on the road, so I had to put mine down.

"You passed," he said. "You passed and you didn't say." He picked his end up again. "That's the gyoza. My father said."

We went on. Nobu kept saying "April" to himself. The sun was down by the silos. He sat down on the curb and looked at his palms, which had a red line across each one from the frame, and I pointed down the lane of crushed shells at the corrugated fence with TANABE AUTO DISMANTLING on it. He looked at it for a long time.

"A scrapyard," he said.

Tanabe-san was on the forklift with its lights on, moving a van. She stopped when she saw us come in through the gate with a roof between us, and switched off.

"Who's this?"

"Kawamata. He's carrying."

"Kawamata Ramen? By the station?" She looked at Nobu over her cigarette. "Your father's soup's too salty."

"Yes, ma'am," Nobu said, and bowed, with his end of the roof still in his hands, so that I had to bow too.

We carried it round the back of the Corolla stacks, through the weeds, and put it down on the ground next to my car.

Nobu didn't say anything. That was how I knew. I'd known him since August and I'd never heard him not say anything for this long.

He walked round it, past the white fender and the primer hood and the headlight stuck halfway up, and the back folded in. He looked in through the tear in the top. The cat looked back at him from the passenger seat.

"An eighty-nine," he said, very quietly. "The first year. The one-six." He touched the faded blue on the door, and rubbed it with his thumb, and the color came back for a second, and went again. "Mariner Blue. They only did Mariner Blue in the first..." He stopped. "Whose is it?"

"Tanabe-san's," I said. "Till I've paid for it."

"How much?"

"Sixty thousand."

"Does it go?"

"No. Not yet."

He went round it a second time. Then he swung his bag round off his shoulder and unzipped the side pocket, and his hand came out with the disposable camera in it, and lifted it.

I put my hand over the front of it.

He looked at me over the top of my hand.

"No," I said.

"Just one. For when it goes. Before and after. The magazine loves before and after."

"No."

He lowered it.

"Does your mother know?"

"She thinks I'm at yours on Sundays."

He looked at me. "Doing what?"

"Your father does the stock early."

"My father does the stock at six," Nobu said. "On his own. He won't let me near it." He put the camera back in the pocket, and zipped it, and put his hand flat on top of the bag. "I swear on this. I've got twenty-six left on it, and I swear on all of them. If your mother asks, you peel onions. You're bad at it. You cry."

I took my hand away.

"Thank you," I said.

"Does it go next week?"

"I don't know. A man's putting a belt on it on Sunday."

He sat down on the end of the roof, on the tarp, and the cat came out through the tear and sat on the hood and looked at him.

"It's looking at me," Nobu said.

"It does that."

We walked back to the station in the dark with nothing to carry, which felt like floating.

Kawamata Ramen was eight stools at a counter, and steam, and the smell of pork bones I'd been smelling on Nobu since August. His father stood behind the counter in a white T-shirt with a towel tied round his head. He had Nobu's round face, with all the softness boiled out of it.

"Hayama," Nobu said to him. "He passed Sanko. April."

His father looked at me. Then he took two bowls down off the shelf.

"Sit," he said.

He put down two bowls of ramen and a plate of gyoza between us, eight of them, stuck together in a row along the bottom, brown and crisp where they'd touched the pan. When I put my hand in my pocket he looked at the hand until I took it out again.

"Passing gyoza," Nobu said.

"Eat," his father said, and went back to his pans.

I ate the ramen and drank the soup with the bowl up at my face, and it was salty, the way Tanabe-san said, and I drank it to the bottom anyway. Nobu ate three gyoza and gave me the rest one at a time with his chopsticks, saying "Pass" every time, until I told him to stop.

Then he got his math out.

"Midterm tomorrow," he said. "First thing. Ono-sensei. You don't know any of it."

"I know some."

"You've slept through all of it. I've watched you." He opened the textbook on the counter, between the bowls, at a page with a curve drawn on it, a hump. "It's a review paper. First-year stuff. Half of it's the parabola. Y equals minus x squared, and everything they do to it."

I looked at it. The curve went up from the left and over the top and down on the right, the same both sides.

"It's a car coming off a jump," Nobu said, and drew it in the air with a chopstick. "It goes up, it slows down, it stops going up at the top, and it comes down the same way it went up. Same shape both sides. All you have to find is where the top is and where it lands."

"Where's the top?" I said.

He showed me four times, on the back of a chopstick wrapper and then in my notebook, while his father wiped the counter round us and didn't tell us to move. The fifth time I found the top myself.

At eight I walked home along the canal with my palms still red from the frame. I lay down at nine and didn't hear Mom's alarm at all.

* * *

The midterms were Tuesday, Wednesday and Thursday, three papers a day.

On Tuesday morning in math there was a parabola on the second page. I found the top. I didn't find where it landed, and I wrote what I had anyway, and moved on. In Japanese history I knew three shoguns and the year of one of them. I slept from one until nine, worked the Tuesday night, and went from the pumps straight to Wednesday's papers with my eyes stinging. In English I kept my eyes open and wrote, and Hotta-sensei walked up and down between the desks and didn't stop at mine. Every morning before the papers I washed my hands with the grey soap. Wednesday night was off. I slept eleven hours.

On Thursday at a quarter past nine I went down the forty-eight steps to go to the shift.

At the bottom of the block, by the drinks machine, there's a public phone in a box with a glass door. The light was on in it. Aya was inside in her coat, her bag on the floor between her feet, with her back to the steps, and the receiver held in both hands.

She was giggling.

I stopped on the bottom step. Aya laughs at the TV, short, through her nose, and she laughs at me. I'd never heard this. It was high and it went on, and she bent over the phone with it, her forehead nearly on the glass.

She turned round. She saw me through the door.

The giggling stopped. She looked at me. Then, without a word, she turned her back again, and lifted one hand behind her and waved it at me, a small flat wave, the way you shoo a cat off a step.

I went on to work. Behind me, before I'd got to the corner, it started again.

She came at five past two. There was nothing else on the forecourt. She said what she always says, and I filled it. 23.8 liters, 2,737 yen. She gave me three 1,000s, and I gave her 263 back, and while she put it in the tray I did the windshield. On the passenger seat lay a paperback, small and fat, with a pencil in it for a bookmark and a plain brown paper cover from the bookshop, the kind they fold round for you at the till. The title didn't show.

"What is it?" I said.

She looked at the book.

"A book."

"What's it about?"

"Trains."

I waited.

"And a timetable," she said.

"Where do you read it?"

"In the car. Before a shift."

Then she looked at me properly through the window, which she didn't usually do, from my face up to the station cap and back down.

"That cap makes you look twelve," she said.

She wound the window up and went, and the pop-ups went away down the harbor road toward the bypass. I took the cap off and looked at it. Navy, with the oil company's mark on the front, and the plastic band at the back done up on the tightest hole. I put it on the counter in the office, and Kubo-san looked at it, and at me, and said nothing about it.

I didn't put it back on.

At four the fish trucks hadn't started yet. The road was empty both ways, and the canopy lights hummed, and I was wiping down the second pump island for the third time when a car came off the harbor road from the town side, slowly, in a gear too high for the speed it was doing, and turned in under the canopy.

I didn't know the engine. That was the first thing. By then I knew what came down that road at four in the morning, and this wasn't any of it. It was quiet at that speed, smooth and even, with a small hard edge on every beat, like a well-made thing that hadn't been used yet.

It was white. Brand new, the tires still black and shiny on the shoulders where nobody had cornered on them. A low coupe with a wide wing standing up off the back, and headlights narrow and slanted across the front, and on the nose, instead of the silver badge Hondas have, a red one. Red behind the silver H.

I'd seen it on the cover of every car magazine in the bookshop that month. I'd never seen one with my own eyes.

It stopped at the pump. The engine went on running, very quietly. The window came down.

"Good evening." A young man's voice, polite, standard speech, the kind they use on the TV news. "High-octane, please. Full."

I filled it. While it ran, I looked. Not a chip on the paint, not even along the nose. At the back, in red: TYPE R. Inside, on the floor under the pedals, there was still a sheet of white paper, the kind the dealer puts down so nobody gets the new carpet dirty, with HONDA printed on it in red, scuffed now at the heel where his right foot had been.

He was older than Aya, not by much. Short neat hair, a sweater over a white collar, a watch on a metal strap. His hands lay on the wheel at nine and three, exactly, thumbs along the rim, while the car stood still with the engine running. Nobody keeps his hands like that at a pump. He did.

32.5 liters. High-octane was 126 at Kubo-san's. 4,095 yen.

He gave me a 10,000-yen note, new and flat, folded once. I went to the office and got the change, and counted it into his hand at the window: five 1,000s, a 500, four 100s, and a 5-yen coin. 5,905. He counted it again after me, with one finger, and put it in a wallet.

I looked at the rev counter, past his hands. The red didn't start until past eight.

"I can't show you," he said. He'd seen where I was looking. "It's being run in. The book says keep it under four thousand for the first thousand kilometers. It's easier to keep to at night." He smiled a little. "It's done four hundred and twelve."

I didn't know what you said to that.

"Excuse me." He hadn't put the window up. "I've been asking at all the stations between here and the bypass. Does a red Roadster fill up here? An NA. Red, on silver seven-spoke wheels. At night, I'm told."

"No," I said.

It came out before I'd thought about it at all.

He looked at me for a moment, with his hands back at nine and three. Then his eyes went down to my chest, to the name tag on my station jacket, where Kubo-san had written my name in marker the night he hired me.

He put the car in first. I heard the lever go home. Then, with his foot still holding the clutch down, he looked back up at me through the open window.

"You're a bad liar, Hayama-kun," he said, pleasantly, in the voice you'd use to tell somebody his shoelace was undone.

The window went up. He took up the clutch with nothing wasted, and turned onto the harbor road toward the bypass, the way she always went. I listened for the top of it, for what that engine did when it was let go. He changed up early, and again early, on the same low note, and the note went away between the warehouses and never once went any higher, and then I couldn't hear it at all.
