# Chapter 12: Ignition

On Saturday there was school until half past twelve, because it was the first Saturday of the month. I went to it from the pumps, and from it to Tanabe's, and when I came round the end of the Corolla stacks at a quarter past one, Tanabe-san was standing at the back of my car with a steel bar as long as my leg.

"You took your time," she said.

On the roof of the car, on a sheet of newspaper, lay two taillights.

They weren't new. They'd been on a car. Red and amber and clear in one long piece each, the way the Roadster's are, with the rubber seal still round the edge and four studs sticking out of the back. One had a scratch across the red.

"Ishiguro came by on Thursday with a door for somebody," she said. "Those were in his van. Off the white one. The front's burned, the back's fine. Two thousand. He says now he owes me nothing. He said that last time."

I gave her two 1,000s out of my pocket. She folded them into her coat without looking at them.

"Lever," she said.

The lever for the trunk is on the floor by the driver's seat, next to the one for the fuel flap. I'd pulled it in September and nothing had happened. I pulled it now. It came up loose, with nothing on the other end of it, and nothing happened again.

"Thought so." She fitted the flat end of the bar under the edge of the lid, at the corner where the peak was worst. "Lean on that. When I say. Not before."

I leaned on it when she said. The lid creaked, and the bar slipped and bit again, and the creak went up into a groan. On the third go something inside let go with a bang. The lid jumped a hand's width and stopped there, crooked, and the cat came off the passenger seat and went over the Corollas in two jumps.

Inside it smelled of damp carpet and something sour. The spare wheel lay in its well. Beside it, in a plastic tray on the right, sat the old battery, black, with a white crust grown up round both posts, and under it a dark ring in the carpet where something had leaked and dried. In the corner by the spare there was a carrier bag. A car-wash sponge in it, gone hard, and a bottle of wax with a third left.

Tanabe-san undid the clamp and lifted the battery out by its handle. She carried it off toward the office against her hip.

"That's mine," she said, going. "The lead's worth something."

I took the carrier bag out and put it on the step of the office. It wasn't mine to throw away.

From inside the trunk you could see the backs of the two holes where the lights go: ragged openings in the panel, with the old sockets hanging out of them on their wires and bits of red plastic still caught in the edges. I picked the bits out. Each socket hung on its wire, whole, with its bulbs in.

The right light went in the way it was meant to. Through from the outside, four studs through four holes, and from the inside four nuts. Ten millimeter. The sockets twisted into the back of it.

The left one didn't. The fish truck had come in at that corner, and the panel round the hole was pushed in, so the light sat crooked, with one corner standing out and two studs that wouldn't meet their holes.

Tanabe-san came back with a lump hammer and a block of wood. She held the wood against the inside of the panel, and I hit the wood, and she moved it, and I hit it again.

"You're not killing it," she said. "You're asking it."

Ten minutes. The left light went in with its outer corner a finger's width proud of the body, and stayed.

The top came next. Three bolts each side under the trim behind the seats, where the frame hinges, and a row of screws along the back holding the edge down under a strip of metal. The old one came away grey and split, and for a minute the car was open to the sky, and the seats were full of cat hair and leaves. We laid the old top in the weeds. The new one came out of Ishiguro's blue tarp black and whole, frame and all, and it took the two of us, her on the far side holding and swearing, me on my side with the screwdriver, until the sun was down on the town side of the yard. When it was on, I pulled it up over us and did the two catches at the top of the windshield. It was tight. Through the clear back window you could see the Corollas.

The rear plate was bent into a V. One of its bolts has a seal over it, and you don't take that off, so I held the block of wood behind the plate and beat it flat where it was, or nearly. A crease went across the numbers.

The trunk lid wouldn't shut any more. Whatever had let go was the catch. Tanabe-san found me a length of wire, and I tied the lid down through the hole where the catch had been to a bracket underneath, twice round, and twisted the ends with the pliers.

I went round to the front and looked back down the side of it. A white fender, a grey hood, blue in between. A black top. At the back, two taillights, one sitting a little proud.

The cat came back while I was putting the tools away. It went to the passenger side and stood on its back legs with its front feet on the door, and looked up at the new top, where the tear used to be. It sat down. It looked at me.

I opened the door for it. It got in and lay down on the seat.

It would have to use the door from now on.

* * *

On Sunday Sota had his jacket on by ten. Kubo-san had said two.

I'd told Mom on Saturday night that I was taking Sota to the harbor to look at the ships. She'd said, "Take the blue one as well," and gone to the supermarket. He'd done his morning inhaler at eight, with me counting ten and ten, and rinsed and spat. At twelve I put the blue one in the inside pocket of Ken's jacket and did the zip up myself.

"I know where it is," he said.

"I know you know."

We walked. It was a clear day, with no wind in the streets and some by the water, and I went at his speed, which on the hill behind the station is slow. He asked eleven questions on the way. I answered four.

Nobu was sitting on the curb at the top of the lane with his bag on his knees. He stood up when he saw us, and bowed to Sota, and Sota bowed back, and then they looked at each other.

"Kawamata," Nobu said. "From school."

"I know," Sota said. "Nii-chan says you talk."

Nobu looked at me. I looked at the lane.

Kubo-san's Cub was already outside the office, with the toolbox on the rack and a cardboard box roped on top of it, and a red ten-liter can roped on top of that. Kubo-san was standing at the office door with Tanabe-san, not talking. He looked at Sota, and at Nobu, and at me.

"Hm," he said.

He gave me the box and took the toolbox, and Tanabe-san took the can, and we went round the Corollas.

The box had a battery in it. A new one, smaller than I'd thought, with a white plastic case you could see the acid line through. Under it in the box, four spark plugs in their own little cardboard boxes, and the receipt.

Kubo-san didn't look at the battery. He looked at the right headlight, the one on my side, that had stood half up since before I'd ever seen the car. Then he lifted the hood and took the lid off a black box at the front of the engine, next to the red rubber cap where he'd put his leads in October. Inside it were fuses in rows, with words printed on the lid over each one. He ran his thumb along them and stopped, and pulled one out with his fingernails and held it up against the sky. Through its clear top you could see the little strip of metal inside, broken in the middle and black at both ends.

"Lights," he said, and put a new one in from a tin in the toolbox.

I untwisted the wire on the trunk lid and lifted the battery into the tray. The clamp went over it. Kubo-san stood behind me with his hands at his sides.

"Red first," he said. "Black last."

The red clamp went on the post with the plus beside it. I did the nut up with the ten-millimeter until it stopped, and then a little more, and stopped, because the post was lead and I didn't know how soft lead was. Then the black.

As the black clamp touched the post, something at the front of the car whirred.

I jumped. So did Sota. Nobu said "Eh?"

The right headlight was going down. Slowly, with a little motor noise, until its lid lay flat in the hood, and stopped.

Nobody said anything. In March a man had sat at the red light by the fish market in the dark, with his battery going, turning the key. Maybe he'd switched his lights off to save what was left for the starter. The left lid had got down, and the fuse had gone before the right one did. It had waited since then to finish.

"Gasoline," Kubo-san said.

He pulled a hose off a pipe at the back of the engine and put its end into the neck of an empty can Tanabe-san had brought from the office. He took a short wire out of the toolbox with a clip at each end, found a little black plug on a lead by the side of the engine, with letters printed on its cap, and clipped the wire across two of the holes in it.

"Two clicks," he said to me.

I got in and turned the key two clicks. Behind the seats, in the back of the car, something began to hum.

"What's that?" Sota said.

"Fuel pump," Kubo-san said.

The gasoline came out of the hose into the can in a thin, steady run. It was the color of the barley tea in the Sanko canteen. The smell came up off it, and it wasn't the smell from the pumps. It was sour and flat, more like old paint. Sota took a step toward it, and I put my hand on his shoulder and walked him back to the Corolla stack, upwind, where the wind off the harbor would take it the other way.

"Here," I said.

"I can't see from here."

"You can see."

He could. He stood with his back against the bottom Corolla and went up and down on his toes.

It ran for a long time. One can, and then most of another, and then the hum behind the seats went higher and the hose spat and coughed air, and Kubo-san said "Off," and I turned it off. He took the red ten-liter can off Tanabe-san and opened the flap on the right rear fender, and poured. The gasoline went in with a glugging noise, clean, the smell I knew. Ten liters. He'd filled it at the station.

He took the wire off and put the hose back on its pipe and tightened its clip.

The plugs next. Kubo-san pulled the leads off one at a time and put a long socket down each hole, and the plugs came up out of the engine on the end of it. He laid them on the fender in a row. The first one's tip was dark brown, crusted, with a spot of rust on the little bent piece of metal at the end. The third one was black and wet.

"What's wrong with them?" Sota called, from the Corollas.

"Old," Kubo-san said.

He pulled another fuse out of the black box and put it in his shirt pocket.

"Turn it," he said. "Ten seconds. It won't go. Watch the oil light."

I turned it. The starter went round, fast and free with the plugs out, and the oil light glowed red in the dash. Then it went out. I let go.

"Oil first," Kubo-san said. "Then fire."

The new plugs went in by hand first, as far as his fingers would turn them, and then a little more with the socket. The leads went back on, each one to its own. The fuse went back in.

Kubo-san wiped his hands on his rag and stood at the front of the car with his head down and his hands by his sides. Not looking at the engine.

Tanabe-san had come back and was leaning on the end of the Corolla stack with her coffee, with the cigarette in. Nobu stood beside Sota with his bag held in front of him in both arms. I saw his hand go to the side pocket where the camera lives, and stay on it, and not open it.

Sota was hopping. From one foot to the other, on the shell.

I got in. I set the seat, back to the end and three clicks forward, the way she'd taught me in hers, and put my hands on the wheel at nine and three. There was cat hair on the passenger seat. The cat itself was up on top of the Corolla stack, sitting with its tail round its feet, where Tanabe-san had put it by the scruff.

Clutch to the floor. The lever wobbled loose in the middle. Neutral. I turned the key two clicks, and the two red lights came on, the battery and the oil can.

"Now," Kubo-san said.

I turned it.

Click.

That was all. One click, from somewhere under the dash, and the two red lights went dim and came back.

Sota stopped hopping.

"Terminal," Kubo-san said. "Plugs out, it'll turn on anything. Plugs in, it wants all of it."

I got out. I went round the back and unwound the wire on the trunk lid, and lifted it, and put my fingers on the black clamp. It turned on the post. Not much. A little. I put the ten-millimeter on the nut and did it up, past where I'd stopped before, until the clamp didn't move under my fingers however hard I pushed. I didn't look at anybody. I wired the lid back down and got back in.

Clutch. Neutral. Two clicks. Two red lights.

I turned it.

The starter went round, and this time it was heavy, and it was even, uh, uh, uh, uh, every beat pushing back the same. Then one of the beats was a bang. A small one, a cough, and the starter went on, uh, uh, uh.

"Keep it turning," Kubo-san said.

I kept it turning. Another cough. Two together.

Then it caught.

It caught ragged, on two, and then three, and the car shook on its springs, and stumbled, and I thought it would stop. It didn't stop. It found the fourth, and all four went together, and the needle on the rev counter jumped up past two thousand by itself, and the whole car hummed under me, through the seat, up through the steering wheel into my hands.

"Leave the gas," Kubo-san said. I hadn't known my foot was going for it.

In the mirror, through the new back window, a cloud went up behind the car, white and then blue. The smell came into the car, hot oil and dust burning. And over everything, from the top of the engine, came a ticking, fast and loud and dry, a hundred small hammers that couldn't agree.

The needle came down. Down past a thousand, and the engine sagged, and shook, and I thought it would stop, and then the needle climbed back up to fifteen hundred and hung there and fell again.

"Lifters," Kubo-san said, to the engine. "They've been dry since March. Let them fill."

I sat with my hands on the wheel and let them fill.

The ticking went quieter. Not all at once. A hammer dropped out, then another. The needle went up and down less, and then less again, and then it stopped somewhere a bit over a thousand and stayed there, and the engine sat on one note. Not a good note. Not hers. A bit rough, a bit flat at the bottom, but one note, the same note, over and over, and the steering wheel buzzed under my hands with it.

The temperature needle came up off its peg.

Sota was standing absolutely still against the Corollas with both hands over his mouth.

Kubo-san put one hand flat on the front fender, the white one, and kept it there.

Tanabe-san took the cigarette out of her mouth.

"Flowers on a dead tree," she said. "My mother used to say that about old men who married again." She put the cigarette back. "She didn't mean it nicely."

I took my hands off the wheel and looked at them. Then I put them back.

* * *

Kubo-san let it run for ten minutes while he lay on the cardboard and looked under it, and held the flashlight on the new pump, and took a rag to the joint where the hose went back on. Nothing dripped. The temperature needle came up to the middle and sat there.

"Go on, then," he said, getting up one knee at a time. "Round the back. Slowly."

"Sota," I said.

Sota was at the passenger door before I'd finished his name.

There was cat hair all over that seat. I'd known that since September and only thought about it then. I got out and started beating it with my glove, and the hair came up in a cloud in the sun, which was worse, and I stopped. Tanabe-san put her coffee down on the Corolla and walked away to her truck without a word. She came back with the towel off its passenger seat, shook it out once, and laid it over mine.

Sota got in onto the towel. I did his belt, and pulled it to see that it held, and he let me.

"Inhaler," I said.

He patted the jacket over it.

I got in. Clutch down. First. The lever went into first with a clunk, not the click hers makes. I let the clutch up slowly, slowly, feeling for it with my foot and with the seat, and it wasn't where hers was. It was higher, much higher, nearly at the top, and when it came it came all at once, and the car gave one hop, and Sota grabbed the dashboard with both hands.

Then we were moving.

Through the weeds, with the tall yellow stuff going down under the bumper and coming up again behind. Round the end of the Corolla stacks, where her car always came in. The shell crunched under the tires. My tires. The steering was heavy, heavier than hers, and the wheel kicked a little in my hands at every pothole.

Into the back lot. The Crowns at the far end, four high. The old stacks on the left. The fence on the right.

Second at the corner. I put the clutch in and moved the lever, and it found second through the wobble, and the clutch came up and the needle dropped back. Twenty along the fence, with the speedometer needle shaking a little on it.

Sota was looking at everything at once. The fence going by. The Crowns coming closer. The dashboard, the needles, my hands, my feet. His glasses had slid down and he didn't push them up.

At the Crowns I turned left, along the wall, and left again along the old stacks, toward the Corollas. In the gap between two Corolla stacks I saw the weeds where the car had been, a flat oblong of pale dead grass with nothing on it now.

Sota laughed.

I don't know what at. At nothing. At the oblong of grass, maybe, or the shell noise, or the cat on top of the Corollas turning its head to watch us go past. It came up out of him all at once, high, the way he laughed when he was small, and it went on.

I waited for the cough.

When he laughs like that, there's always a cough at the end of it. The small one, the dry one, and then the two of us wait to see if it will stop on its own. I had my left hand ready to come off the wheel for the zip of his pocket.

He laughed all the way along the Corollas, past the forklift corner, where Kubo-san and Tanabe-san and Nobu were standing in a row, and round onto the fence again.

It didn't come.

He stopped laughing because he'd run out, and took a breath, a big one, all the way down, and let it out.

"How fast are we going?"

"Twenty."

"Is that fast?"

"No."

He thought about that. Then he laughed again, shorter, and pushed his glasses up.

We came round to the forklift corner. I stopped where Natsume always made me stop, with the bumper level with the end of the stack, and put it in neutral, and the engine sat there on its rough, flat, single note.

Sota looked at me.

"Again," he said.

I took him round again.

* * *

At four, when Kubo-san had gone up the lane on the Cub and Nobu had gone home, Tanabe-san came round to the back lot with her coat buttoned. The car stood by the forklift corner with the engine off, ticking as it cooled. Sota was sitting on the forklift's step with the cat about two meters away from him, the two of them looking at each other.

"Don't touch it," I said to him. "Your chest."

"I'm only looking."

Tanabe-san held out her hand. On a loop of wire there were two keys and a plastic tag from a tire company.

"Padlock," she said. "Office." She shook the loop, so they rang. "From tomorrow I lock up at four. The light's gone by five, and I'm not sitting in that office in the dark so you can come and look at your car. That cat eats at five. Half a tin."

I took it.

"The chain goes through the gate twice," she said. "Then the lock. Not once. Twice."

"Twice."

She looked at me a while longer with the cigarette going up and down as she breathed. Then she went to her truck, and the truck went up the lane, and I heard it turn onto the harbor road toward town.

Sota and I walked home. He asked nothing the whole way. Halfway, on the canal path, he took my hand, which he hadn't done in years, and let go of it again before the danchi.

At ten I was at the station. The receipt from the parts dealer was on the counter by the till, where he always leaves them. BATTERY 6,800. SPARK PLUG ×4 1,600. Tax 252. 8,652. Under that, in pencil, in Kubo-san's small square numbers: *Regular 10.0 L 1,150.* And under that: *9,802.*

I put a 10,000 down on it. I'd taken it out of the dictionary before I came.

He opened the till and counted the change into my hand without looking at it. A 100, a 50, four 10s, a 5, three 1s. 198 yen. He wrote PAID across the receipt and gave it to me. That left 46,000 in the dictionary, with Tanabe-san's seven in it, and 3,101 on me.

"Don't spend it all on gas," he said.

He went into the back and turned the radio up.

* * *

Monday I went from the pumps to school, and from school to Tanabe's.

The gate was shut. The chain went through the two halves of it twice, with the padlock hanging in front. I unlocked it and unwound it and let myself in, and wound it round again from the inside and closed the lock, and stood there for a moment with my hand on the cold steel.

The cat was on the office step. It ate half a tin of mackerel off the lid while I watched and walked off without thanking me.

I drove the back lot until there wasn't enough light to see the old tire by the fence. First, second at the corner, twenty along the fence, brake level with the tire, clutch, blip, lever, clutch up. Then again.

It wasn't her car. I'd known it wasn't, and I still didn't know it until I tried to do in mine what I'd learned in hers. The clutch came up near the top and all at once. The lever wobbled in the middle and had to be found. The brakes were new pads on old discs, and they did nothing for the first bit and then too much. My shoes were still too thick. The first blip I tried was too big, and the car surged at the Crowns, and I sat there afterwards with my heart going, alone in the lot, and nobody said "Again." I said it.

Tuesday I did the same before the shift, and slept from six to nine with my uniform trousers on.

Every lap, coming round past the forklift corner, you can see out past the end of the office to the gate. Every lap I saw it.

On Wednesday night I went back.

I'd fed the cat at five and come home and slept, and after dinner I lay on my futon with my eyes open until half past nine. Mom was at the supermarket. Aya wasn't home yet. Sota was on his futon with a comic.

"Are you going to the car?" he said, when I got up.

"To feed the cat."

"In the dark?"

"Cats eat in the dark."

He looked at me over the comic, and then went back to it.

The moon was up over the cranes, round, or nearly, and the harbor road was white with it between the sodium lights. I let myself in and chained the gate behind me.

In the moonlight the yard was all edges. Stacks and shadows of stacks. The press with its lid up, like a mouth.

I got in and started it. In the quiet of the yard it was very loud.

I turned the light switch one click. Behind me, the Corolla stacks went red. Two patches of it on the white and red of the old Corollas, one a little higher than the other.

I turned it again. With a whir, at the front, both lids came up, and the lights came on, and the Crowns at the far end of the lot jumped out of the dark at me, flat, four high, every crushed roof and squashed door frame.

I turned it round on the shell and drove at walking pace out of the back lot, round the end of the Corollas, past the press, past the office with its window black, and stopped with the front bumper a meter from the gate.

The headlights lit it up: the corrugated steel gone grey on the inside, with rust coming through in lines, and the padlock hanging bright in the middle.

On the other side of the gate the lane went up between its weeds to the harbor road. The harbor road went one way to the silos and the bypass, and the other to the rail crossing and the warehouse straight. I knew every meter of it by ear. I'd stood on it for three months listening to other people drive it.

I sat for a long time. I don't know how long. Long enough for the heater to blow warm and for my feet, in their thick shoes, to get hot on the pedals.

Then I put it in reverse, and backed it all the way round to the back lot, slowly, with my arm over the passenger seat and my head turned right round. I turned the switch off, and the lids went down, and the red went off the Corollas. I turned the key. The yard was very quiet afterwards, and my ears rang in it.

I locked up and went home.

On Thursday she didn't come.

I had two words ready this time, not a speech. *It goes.* At two o'clock I was standing on the island where I could see furthest down the road. At three I was still standing there. The road had trucks on it and taxis, and one kei truck that wanted 1,000 yen of regular in coins, and nothing that went up.

At four Kubo-san said, "Fish," and the trucks came.

Friday she didn't come either. On Saturday there was no school, and I slept. On Sunday I went to the yard at six in the morning and drove the back lot by myself until the sun came up over the Crowns, and then I went home and slept again, and worked the night.

* * *

*Tuesday. 2:05 a.m.*

Mom had come in at twenty past twelve. I'd heard her on the last flight of the stairs, the stop on the landing, the door, the water running at the sink. Then the fusuma to her room, and then nothing. Aya had been asleep since eleven. Sota was asleep on his back with his mouth open, and his glasses folded on the windowsill by his inhaler, left arm over right.

At two I got up. I'd gone to bed in my jeans. I put on my own jacket, the one without a name on it, and felt in the pocket for the gloves and the list and the tooth, and the loop of wire with the two keys, which had been in there with them since Tanabe-san gave it to me. I took my shoes from the genkan and put them on outside on the landing, in the dark, with the door shut behind me, and went down the forty-eight steps without the light.

Mom gets up at half past four.

I went the back way, behind the fish-market sheds. Forty minutes. My breath went out white in front of me under the lamp by the sawmill. The moon stood over the cranes, a bit more than half of it. I came out at the silos and went between them, past the old houses by the block works, all dark, and down the lane, on the edge of it, where the weeds are, so the shells wouldn't crunch.

The padlock was cold enough to hurt. I turned the key, and took the lock off, and unwound the chain, once, and again, and laid it on the ground by the post. The gate scraped when I pulled it open, louder than anything all night. I stood still and listened. Nothing. A dog, far off, and then not even that.

The cat was on the passenger seat. When I opened the driver's door it looked at me, and got up, and walked across the handbrake and out past my knees, and away under the Corollas.

I sat down. Seat, three clicks. Mirrors. Belt.

The fuel needle had stood at a quarter the Sunday it started. Now it was a finger's width above E. I looked at it for a while.

Clutch. Neutral. Two clicks. Two red lights. I turned it, and it caught on the second beat and shook and settled, and the lifters ticked for a moment and stopped. I let it sit until the needle on the rev counter stopped looking for its place.

One click on the light switch. Two clicks. The lids came up.

I drove round the Corollas and past the press at walking pace, and out through the gate with a meter to spare on each side, and up the lane.

The shells went under my tires all the way up. I'd heard that sound from the back of the yard, holding a tarp up over my head, when her car went up this lane and didn't stop. Now it was coming up through the floor into my feet.

At the top of the lane I stopped. The harbor road was empty both ways, orange under the sodium lights. To the right were the silos, and past them, round the bend, Kubo-san's canopy. Kubo-san knows every engine on this road.

I put the indicator on. Left. It ticked, loud, inside the car, for nobody at all, and I turned left, away from the silos.

First. Second. The needle went up and the note went up with it, and my hands were wet on the rim, and I had the wheel so tight my thumbs hurt. Third. The lever found it through the wobble. I let the clutch up and the car went on, on its own, on a public road, with me in it, and the speedometer needle came round to forty and I held it there.

Forty. The sodium lights came toward me and went over the roof one after another. The wind came in at the window, which I'd wound down a hand's width so I could hear, and it was cold on the side of my face, and it smelled of the harbor and of my own car's exhaust. The engine sat on its one rough note, the needle a bit over two thousand. On my left, fences, and the dark where the water was. On the right, sheds with their shutters down.

I was laughing. I don't know when I'd started. I was shaking, too, my knee on the clutch side going on its own, and I was laughing, and the laugh came out of me in bits, and I couldn't stop either one.

Up ahead the traffic light before the crossing was red.

I braked for it a long way early, the way you'd brake with a policeman in the back seat, and stopped with the bumper on the white line. Neutral. Foot on the brake. The handbrake. Nobody else was waiting. Nobody else was anywhere. The light hung over the road on its arm, red, and beyond it the single lamp on its pole by the rails, and the rails going away both ways into the dark.

Then lights came into my mirror, from back toward the silos, and got bigger. Under my own engine I heard a rough four, and every time it lifted, it popped.

I knew it before it reached me.

The lights filled the mirror, and swung out, and the Silvia came up on my right, on the wrong side of the road, where there was nobody to mind, and stopped level with me. White. The big wing. The dent in the passenger door, close enough to touch if I'd put my arm out of the window. His passenger window slid down by itself.

Goto was leaning across his passenger seat with a cigarette in his mouth, looking at my car. At the grey hood. At the faded blue of the door beside him. All the way along it, and back.

Then he looked at me.

He looked away, at the light. And then he looked again.

He laughed. He took the cigarette out to do it properly, and the laugh filled the inside of his car and came out of the window at me.

"Oi," he said. "Bridge kid."

I didn't say anything. I had both hands on the wheel.

"Nice car."

The light went green.

His exhaust banged, and the Silvia went. It went across the rails with its brake lights flicking once before the bump, and its nose stayed level over it, the way it had in September, and then it was a pair of red lights going down the warehouse straight, small, and smaller, and its popping coming back to me off the walls.

My right foot stayed where it was.

I sat at the green light with my foot on the brake. It went amber, and red again, and the empty road waited with me. Then it went green again. I turned the car round across the empty road, in three goes, with the indicator ticking, and drove back the way I'd come. Forty. Third. The note sat where it was.

I put the indicator on for the lane. I went down it on the shells, and through the gate with a meter each side, and round the end of the Corollas to the back lot, and stopped by the forklift corner.

I turned the switch off. The lids went down. I turned the key, and the engine stopped, and in the quiet my knee was still going.

I sat there until it stopped too.

Then I got out and pulled the gate shut, and it scraped, and I didn't stop to listen this time. I put the chain through, once, and then again, and the lock, and pressed it till it clicked, and pulled on it to be sure. The key went back in my pocket with the gloves and the list and the tooth.

Over on the Corolla stack, the cat was sitting with its tail round its feet, looking at me.

It was Tuesday the fourteenth of November.

Eighty-seven days to eighteen.
