# Chapter 3: Pier 3

At four on Saturday morning the station was so quiet I could hear the price sign ticking as it cooled. I was hosing down the forecourt. Kubo-san sat on his stool by the oil rack with his coffee and watched the water run toward the drain.

I turned the hose off.

"Kubo-san. The cars from the bypass. The Silvia and those. Where do they go on Saturdays?"

He didn't look up from the drain.

"Who wants to know."

"A guy at school. He read it in a magazine."

That was true. Nobu had come to my desk at lunch on Friday with *Street Tune* folded open at the reader page and put his finger under two lines from somebody in Shiomi, nineteen, no name, asking if it was true about Saturday nights at the harbor. Nobody had answered that one either. "Somebody knows," Nobu had said. "Somebody always knows. And you say you rode in the Akai Zanzō, so." He'd said it with his eyebrows up. I'd slept through fifth period, after the Thursday shift, and woken up with my cheek stuck to my English book and the dare still there.

Kubo-san drank some coffee.

"Pier 3," he said. "The big lot at the end. After one."

I knew the lot. She'd stopped there after the lap, two weeks ago, and sat with her hands on the wheel while the engine ticked. There'd been nobody in it but us, and black circles all over the concrete, rubber laid down in loops on top of loops. I'd thought they were from trucks turning.

"How do you know?"

"I sell them gas." He put the can down on the oil rack. "Sunday mornings the day man takes their bottles out of my bins. Energy drinks. The little brown ones."

"Have you been?"

He didn't answer that. He got up off the stool with one hand on the rack, the way he did now, and took the hose from me and coiled it again, because I'd coiled it wrong.

"It's no place for a boy without a license," he said. "If you go, you stand at the back. Somebody shouts, you don't wait to hear what he shouted."

"Why would anybody shout?"

He hung the hose on its hook and looked at the drain.

"Leaves," he said. "Your drain's blocked."

* * *

On Saturday at twenty past eleven I put on my black T-shirt, the one without the station's name on it, and told my sister I was going to study.

Mom was at the supermarket until midnight. Aya was at the low table with her feet in a washing-up bowl of cold water, which is what she does after a day on the till, and a bowl of rice with green tea poured over it that she was eating very slowly so it would last. The window over the sink was open. The moon was too high to see from the table, but it lit the water tank on the next block so white you could have read the rust on it. On the sill Sota had put five stalks of pampas grass in the plum jar where the cicada skins used to live, because his teacher had said it was the harvest moon and you were supposed to. There were no dango. Nobody had said anything about dango.

"Study," Aya said.

"For Sanko. At Kawamata's."

"Who's Kawamata?"

"Guy in my class. His family's got the ramen shop by the station."

"At eleven at night."

"He works in the shop till ten."

She lifted one foot out of the bowl and looked at it. Her feet were red across the top where the shoes pressed.

"Does his family feed you?"

"No."

She put the foot back. "It's Saturday."

"So?"

"So Friday came and went and you're still in. I owe you a haircut."

"You don't have to."

"I said I would. The night before the test." She picked up her bowl and drank some of the tea out of it. "Are you coming back tonight?"

"Late. Or I'll sleep there."

"Then I'll tell Mom you're staying over. So she doesn't sit up."

I sat down on the genkan step and put my shoes on. The silver tape on the right one had gone grey at the toe, and a thread was coming loose at the edge of it. I pressed it flat with my thumb.

"Nii-chan?"

Sota's voice, from behind the fusuma, thick with sleep.

"Are you going to work?"

"Studying. Go to sleep."

"Did you see the moon?" A rustle, his futon. "It's the fifteenth night. I can't see it from here. You have to look at it for me."

"I'll look at it."

"Properly, though."

"Properly."

Aya didn't say anything else. When I shut the steel door, I held the handle down until it was closed, and went down all forty-eight steps without the sole slapping once, because of the tape.

* * *

Kawamata Ramen had its shutter down and its red lantern off, and Nobu was standing under the lantern in a white short-sleeved shirt buttoned all the way up to his throat. He had on track pants and a pair of rubber kitchen sandals, the black kind cooks wear on wet floors, at least two sizes too big. His bag was over his shoulder and full.

"You came," he said. He sounded surprised, and then embarrassed to sound surprised.

"I said I would."

"People say that." He looked at my T-shirt. "Is black right? I didn't know what you wear. I thought, white looks older." He touched his top button.

"It's fine."

He'd brought: a vocabulary book, in case his father checked the bag; the magazine; a flashlight; the disposable camera, out of its cardboard now; and two onigiri in foil that his mother had made "for studying". He gave me one. It was umeboshi, and so salty my eyes watered, and I ate it in five bites walking.

"What did you tell your father?"

"That I'm studying at yours. For your factory test. I have to be back before he gets up for the stock. Six." He swallowed. "He said it's the first useful thing I've done all year. He said when you pass he'll do you gyoza."

I didn't say anything to that for a few steps.

"I told my sister I'm studying at yours."

Nobu thought about it. You could watch him think. His lips moved.

"So you're at mine," he said, "and I'm at yours."

"Yes."

"Then nobody's anywhere." He said it quietly, and he looked very happy about it.

I took us down the back road behind the fish-market sheds instead of along the harbor road. That way we didn't pass the station. I didn't know who worked Saturday nights there, and I didn't want to find out it was Kubo-san.

It took forty minutes. The back road ran past loading docks and a closed sawmill, with the moon so bright that every stack of pallets had a hard black shadow under it, and came out on the harbor road at the cement silos. We walked between them, where the road bends one way and then the other, and on the far side we crossed the freight line where it cuts across the road, a single light on a pole and the rails shining both ways into the dark. Nobu stopped and looked down the rails one way and then the other for a long time. Then he trotted to catch up, sandals clapping.

He talked the whole way, in a whisper, though there was nobody to hear. The magazine said the harbor scene in Osaka ran a loop on the expressway. The magazine said the police there had started taking cars. The magazine said a lot of things. Halfway down the long straight past the crossing he stopped talking, and I heard why.

Music, first. The same song coming out of three or four cars at once, a little out of time with itself, so it sounded like it was arguing. A woman singing in English over a fast beat, *night* and *fire* and *heart*, the only words I caught. Under the music, engines idling. I picked them out walking, one at a time. A big six, smooth. A four with a hole in its exhaust. Something that wasn't a piston engine at all, a high buzzing burble that I'd heard only once, on the bypass, and Nobu said "Rotary" and grabbed my arm, and we went round the end of the last warehouse and there it was.

The lot at Pier 3 was the size of the sports ground at school. Sodium lights on tall poles down one side, one of them out. Containers stacked three high along the back. On the open side, a row of bollards and then black water with the moon broken up on it. Between the bollards and the containers were cars, maybe twenty of them, parked in a loose horseshoe facing the middle with their doors open and their stereos on, and more turning in off the road behind us. It wasn't half past twelve yet. People everywhere, leaning, sitting, smoking. I counted fifty-three and then they kept moving and I stopped.

The cars I knew from the road and from Nobu's magazine, which is not the same as knowing them. A dark blue Skyline, the R32, low and square, with its four round taillights. A white RX-7, the older shape, which was the rotary. A lowered Cefiro so close to the ground I didn't know how it got over the rails. A nearly new Supra, black, the round one, and a girl in a white jacket sitting cross-legged on its hood while its owner stood next to it with his hands half up, not touching her, not touching the car, not knowing which one to save. A white Starlet, the little turbo one, with a boy sitting in it alone with the door open.

At the end of the row was a big white van with a cooler box in the back, and a man beside it selling canned beer at 300 yen. The machine by our danchi sold them for 220. I'd bought one there in August. He was charging 80 yen a can more than the machine, and he'd sold six while I watched.

Somebody's pager went off and four people checked their belts.

Under all of it were the black circles. Hundreds of them, on top of each other, the whole lot printed with them. I stood at the edge and tried to find the place where she'd parked after the lap, nose to the water, the engine ticking. I couldn't. It had been empty then. Now there was a Mark II there with its trunk open and a speaker in the trunk the size of a fridge.

"Fifty people," Nobu said in my ear. "Fifty. At least. And nobody's ever written about it. Not one line."

"Fifty-three."

"You counted?"

In the middle of the horseshoe there was another white van, older, its back doors open, and a man sitting on the floor of it with his legs hanging out. On his knees he had a grey metal cash box. Beside him, on the van floor, was a walkie-talkie, and in his hand a school notebook, the blue kind every kid has, with a pencil in the spiral. On the side of the van somebody had painted over a name in white that didn't quite match the white of the van, and in the sodium light you could still read it underneath: OKABE TIRE.

People went to him. That was what I noticed first. They didn't stand around him; they went up, did something and left. A man in a golf shirt took a 10,000-yen note out of his wallet, folded it lengthwise, and held it out between two fingers. The man in the van took it, said something, laughed, wrote in the notebook, tore the corner off a page, wrote on the corner and gave it to him. The 10,000 went into the cash box. The box closed. The golf shirt put the scrap of paper in his breast pocket and patted it.

"Okabe-san," somebody called across the lot, and the man in the van lifted his pencil without looking.

He was in his forties, with a soft round face and reading glasses pushed up on his forehead, and a polo shirt with nothing written on it. His hands were the first thing I looked at. Tire hands: black in the creases of the fingers, but the nails cut short and clean. He licked his thumb when he counted. He never counted the same notes twice. He didn't need to.

I watched him for a long time. Five more people came to the van while I stood there. Two 10,000s, a 5,000, two 1,000s from a boy who looked about fifteen and who got laughed at and was given a chit anyway. I added them up without meaning to. Then I added the golf shirt.

"You're doing it again," Nobu said.

"What?"

"Your mouth. You count with your mouth."

I shut it.

That was when I heard the Silvia.

It came down the harbor road from the bypass end with its exhaust popping every time it lifted, rough and loud, and I knew it before its lights swung in, because I'd heard it once from the overpass on the first of August, chasing something it couldn't catch. White S13. The big wing. The black wheels. It was dirtier than I remembered, and there was a dent in the passenger door I didn't remember at all. It rolled across the lot in a slow curve, and people moved out of its way without looking, and it stopped in the middle with its nose pointed at Okabe's van. The lowered Cefiro's owner, a skinny guy about nineteen in a satin jacket, went over to it straight away and opened the driver's door.

"Goto-san."

So that was his name. Goto got out. Bleached hair, the shiny tracksuit jacket open over a white tank top, the gold chain, a cigarette already lit. He stretched with both arms up and his back cracked, and he looked round at the lot.

Then his eyes got to me and stopped.

"Oi."

He came over. He walked like his shoes were too good for the ground. He looked at me from about a meter away, then at Nobu, then back at me.

"I know you," he said. "Bridge kid."

"Evening."

"Evening," he said, in my voice, and laughed. The skinny guy behind him laughed when he laughed. "Bridge kid. You dropped your beer. You chased me down the stairs." He blew smoke up, away from my face, which I didn't expect. "Still chasing afterimages?"

"No."

"You find him? The rich guy in the toy?"

"No."

Beside me Nobu took a breath, the kind you take before a long sentence. I knew which sentence. I put my foot down on his foot, on the bare toes sticking out of the front of his father's sandal, not hard, and kept it there. He let the breath out through his nose.

"Course you didn't," Goto said. "Nobody finds it. It finds you, and then it leaves." He looked at Nobu's hand. "What's that."

Nobu had the camera. I don't think he knew he was holding it.

"Nothing," Nobu said. "A camera. It's off. It doesn't go off, it's disposable, it's just..."

"That comes out again, it goes in the harbor," Goto said. "You and it."

Nobu put it in his bag and zipped the bag and bowed, a whole bow, from the waist, in the middle of the Pier 3 lot at one in the morning. Goto looked at him. Then he laughed again, properly this time, and turned away toward the van.

"Tetsu," he said. "Who've I got?"

"The Chaser," the skinny one said.

* * *

The Chaser was grey, the new shape, two years old at most, and so clean it reflected the sodium lights in long orange stripes down the doors. Its driver was about twenty-two, with a perm and white loafers and no socks, and there was a girl in the passenger seat who didn't get out.

"Tourer V," Nobu said. He'd got his voice back. "280 PS. All the big ones are 280. The makers agreed it. Nobody's allowed more, so they all say 280 and some of them are lying upward. Goto's is a K's, probably. 205. That's seventy-five horses."

"I can subtract."

"I'm just saying. Seventy-five horses."

The crowd thought so too. I could see it at the van. People went up and said "Chaser" and held out their notes, and Okabe took them and wrote, and called the price out every few minutes in a pleasant greengrocer's voice.

"Chaser, ten pays sixteen. Silvia, ten pays twenty-five."

It took me a minute. Then I had it. Put a 10,000-yen note on the Chaser and if it won you got 16,000 back. Put it on the Silvia and you got 25,000. The car everybody thought would lose paid more. Of course it did. Otherwise nobody would bet on it.

Goto and the Chaser's driver each gave Okabe an envelope. Okabe counted them both, out loud, flat on the lid of the box. Five 10,000s. Five 10,000s. He wrote it down and put the money inside and turned the little key.

"A hundred thousand," Nobu said.

"He keeps ten," I said. I'd heard someone say it. "Winner gets ninety."

The Chaser's driver walked round his car. He did it three times. On the third time round he stopped beside a guy leaning on the Skyline and asked him something, low, with his hand making a shape in the air: right, then left, then a question mark. I was close enough to hear the end of it. "...after the silos?"

The guy on the Skyline told him. He told him loudly enough, and slowly enough, that the people around him heard. Then the people around them heard.

Goto didn't say anything. He went and crouched by the Chaser's front tire and ran his thumb along the shoulder, where the tread turns down into the sidewall, and held his thumb up to the light. Stuck to it were the little rubber hairs that new tires have on them from the mold.

"New rubber," he said, to nobody. "Never been warm."

He flicked the hairs away and lit another cigarette.

It went round the lot in about a minute. I watched it go. I watched it get to the van, where a man who'd had a 10,000-yen note out for the Chaser put it back in his wallet and stood there thinking. Okabe looked up over his glasses at the crowd for a while. Then he licked the pencil, crossed out a line in the notebook and wrote a new one.

"Silvia, ten pays fifteen," he called. "Chaser, ten pays twenty-two."

The money started going the other way. Nobody had driven a meter. The Chaser still had 280 horses and Goto still had a dent in his door.

"What happened?" Nobu said. "Why'd it go?"

"He asked the way."

"So?"

"So he's never driven it."

They lined up at the mouth of the lot, where the harbor road ran past, side by side across both lanes. Goto on the left, nearest the warehouses. The Chaser on the right. A kid with a long black flashlight stood between them on the white line, a scooter boy, maybe sixteen, with his helmet pushed back on his head. Everybody moved to the edge of the lot to see down the road. So did we. I stood on the last bollard, at the corner where the lot met the road, twenty meters behind the flashlight kid, so I was looking straight down the road over the two roofs. Nobu stood on the one next to it in his sandals, holding on to my shirt.

From there you could see the warehouse straight end to end. It ran a kilometer, dead flat, warehouse walls on one side and the container yard on the other, and at the far end, small, under its single light on its pole, the rail crossing we'd walked over. After the crossing the road bent left and you lost it behind the silos. After that it was the S-bend between them, and the on-ramp up to the bypass, and the long left-hander under the overpass, and the Higashi exit and then back down here to the harbor from the other end. Seven kilometers. Somebody behind us said a good run was four minutes.

The engines came up. The Chaser's was smooth and deep and got bigger without getting rougher. Goto's was rough at every speed, and it popped, and the popping made people grin. The music in the cars had been turned down. The kid with the flashlight held it up over his head and switched it on.

He held it there. I counted two. He brought it down.

The Silvia got away first. The Chaser's back tires spun, new and cold and slippery, and it squirmed, and Goto was a length clear before it hooked up. Then it hooked up. Its six came onto the turbo with a long rising whoosh I could feel in the bollard under my feet, and the gap stopped growing, and then it started shrinking. Halfway down the straight the Chaser was alongside. Two-thirds of the way down it was past, on the right, half a length, then a length, then three, its taillights pulling away from Goto's toward the little light at the end.

"Seventy-five horses," Nobu said. He had my shirt in both fists.

At the crossing, both sets of brake lights came on. The Chaser's came on later. It was still three lengths up. It hit the bump at the rails, and its nose went down onto the road, and a spray of orange sparks came out from under its front end and bounced along the tarmac. Its taillights wobbled, left, right, left. Behind it Goto's lights went steady over the bump and didn't wobble at all. He'd lifted before it. He'd put the car down soft.

Then they both bent left and the silos took them.

The lot went quiet. Somebody turned their stereo all the way off.

You could still hear them. That's what I hadn't known. Out there among the warehouses and silos the sound came back to us across the water and off the walls, and I closed my eyes to hear it better. The Chaser's six, ahead, smooth, going up through a gear. Goto's four behind it, rough, closer than it should have been.

Okabe's walkie-talkie hissed on the van floor.

"Silos." A boy's voice, high, half shouted. "Chaser, Silvia. Close. Close." Then: "Silvia inside! Silvia's inside!"

And I heard it, before he said it. The Chaser's engine dropped away. Just for a moment, a hole in the sound, a breath. When it came back it was behind the four. Not ahead of it. Behind.

A shout went up from Tetsu and a few others.

"How did you...?" Nobu said. "You knew before the radio."

"He lifted."

"How do you hear a lift?"

I didn't know how. I just heard it.

"On-ramp." A different voice, a scooter at the on-ramp. "Silvia. Then Chaser. Two lengths. Both up."

After that the sound went up onto the bypass and flattened into the hum the whole city makes at night, and I lost them. The radio didn't. Somebody had a scooter up on the overpass. My overpass.

"Straight. Chaser's coming. He's on him. He's on him."

A hiss with nothing in it. Okabe picked the walkie-talkie up off the floor and held it on his knee.

"Curve. Silvia's through. Chaser's braking. He's braking in the middle of it."

I knew what the curve looked like with nothing on it, at dawn. I could see the Chaser in it now, the brake lights coming on where no one who knew the road would brake. He didn't trust it. I didn't blame him.

"Exit," the radio said. "Silvia. Chaser."

A few seconds later I had them again, coming down off the bypass at the far end of the harbor road, the four in front and rough, the six behind it and smooth, and somebody shouted "There!" and pointed.

Headlights. Two pairs, from the far end of the harbor road, coming toward the lot from the opposite direction, fast. You couldn't tell which was which at first. Then you could, because the front ones were narrow slits and the ones behind were bigger and squarer. The Silvia, then the Chaser. Two lengths. Maybe a bit more.

They went past the flashlight kid like that. Two lengths. The noise hit us all at once, and then the brake lights, both cars standing on their noses, and the smell, hot brakes and rubber, came across the lot a second later.

Nobu was jumping on the bollard. One sandal came off and fell in the water. He didn't notice. Neither did I, then.

Goto got his ninety thousand from Okabe in nine notes. He fanned them out and held them up to the lot, and the lot cheered, the part of it that had money on the Silvia. Then he folded them and put them inside his tracksuit jacket, in a pocket with a zip.

Fifty thousand in. Ninety back. Forty thousand up. I did it before I'd decided to. Forty thousand was what was in my English dictionary at home, everything I'd kept since August.

The Chaser's driver was crouched at his front bumper with a lighter, looking underneath. There was a crack straight across the black lip under the bumper, and a piece of it was hanging down, scraping when the car moved. He tried to push it back up. It came down again. The girl had got out at last. She stood by the passenger door with her arms crossed and didn't say anything for a long time, and then said, "Can we go home," and he said "In a minute," and she got back in.

"A front lip for a Tourer V," Nobu said. He'd come up beside me, walking crooked on one sandal. "Forty thousand. Maybe sixty painted."

"Where's your sandal?"

He looked down at his foot.

"Oh," he said.

Okabe had taken ten thousand off the top of the stake without leaving the van, and something off every price he'd called, and he was writing it down.

He looked up from the notebook and found me looking at him.

"You," he said. Pleasant, not loud. "The one who counts with his mouth."

Nobu made a small noise.

"First time?" Okabe said.

"Yes."

"Welcome." He pushed his glasses down onto his nose and turned a page. "You betting next one?"

"I've got no money."

"Nobody's got money," he said, and smiled at me. It was a nice smile. I'd seen the same one on the man at the pachinko parlor by the bookstore, handing out tissues at the door.

The next run was being made up already. A red Civic with stickers down the side, and the white Starlet. The boy who'd been sitting alone in the Starlet with the door open was at the van now, standing too straight, with notes in his hand.

"Fifteen," he said. "That's all I've got. He wants thirty."

Okabe looked across at the Civic's owner, who was eating a cup of noodles on his hood and shook his head without stopping.

"Nobody backing you?" Okabe said.

The boy looked round at the lot. Nobody looked back.

"You can always bet on yourself," Okabe said. He said it kindly.

"That's what the fifteen is."

"Then bet more."

The boy's hand went to his back pocket. I watched it. It came out empty, and went back in, and came out empty again.

That was when the walkie-talkie went.

"Patrol. On-ramp. Coming down. Patrol, patrol..."

It was the same boy who'd called the on-ramp, but his voice had gone up an octave and there was an engine under it, his scooter, already moving.

Okabe closed the cash box and the notebook in one movement, put the notebook inside his shirt, and pulled his legs into the van. Somebody banged the doors shut on him from outside. Then every engine in the lot started at once.

* * *

It took about twenty seconds. I'd never seen anything empty that fast, and I'd seen a lot of things leave in a hurry.

Headlights everywhere, crossing each other. Doors slamming. The Supra went first, the girl in the white jacket half in and half out of it. Goto's Silvia reversed out of the lot and swung round on the road and was gone toward the Higashi end, away from the on-ramp, with its lights off. The Cefiro scraped over the curb and went after it. The cooler van went after them with its back door flapping and cans rolling out of it and bursting on the concrete. Scooters went between the cars. The Chaser went last of the fast ones, with its lip hanging down and screaming on the road the whole way, and I saw the girl's face in the passenger window, white, and her hand flat on the dashboard.

Nobody had room for two kids on foot. Nobody looked.

"Nobu."

He was standing in the middle of the lot on one sandal, turning round and round, with his bag held against his chest.

I took his arm and ran. Not toward the road. Away from it, toward the warehouses on the far side, where the lot ended in a strip of weeds and a row of loading doors set back into the wall. The first one was shuttered. The second one had a step and a recess, a doorway about a meter deep, and it was black, because the moon was on the other side of the building. I pushed him in and got in after him and pulled him down so we were crouched in the back of it against the steel door, my shoulder against the frame.

Now the other sandal was gone too. He'd run out of it somewhere, and I hadn't seen where.

The lot was empty. A minute before there had been a crowd and twenty-five cars and three songs arguing, and now there was moonlight on the concrete and the black circles, and cans, and cigarette ends, and the stink of tires, and the sound of everyone going away in every direction getting smaller.

Then a new sound, getting bigger.

It didn't have a siren on. It came down the harbor road from the bypass end at an ordinary speed, and turned into the lot slowly, and only then did the red light on its roof start to turn. A Crown, white on top and black below, with the prefecture's police crest on the door. Its headlights went across the lot and across the warehouse wall above our heads. I put my face down against my knees and kept my eyes open.

Nobu was breathing through his mouth. It was very loud. I put my hand over it.

He didn't fight. He nodded under my hand.

The Crown stopped in the middle, where Okabe's van had been. For a while nothing happened. The red light turned. I could hear the radio in the car, a woman's voice reading out something, numbers, flat. I could hear my own heart in my ears.

I knew what happened to boys from the place who got picked up. I'd watched it happen to two of them. One of them came back. If they took my name, they'd have the rest of it by Monday. Not the night job, not Kubo-san. The rest. The file. Then Mom would come to the police station in her supermarket vest, and she'd have to say who she was to someone behind a desk.

I didn't move. I know how to be still. I learned it somewhere I don't talk about.

The passenger door of the Crown opened, and a policeman got out.

He was older. Not old, but older than the one at the wheel by about twenty years, I thought. Grey at the sides under his cap. A little heavy in the middle. He didn't hurry. He stood next to the car and looked at the lot for a long time without moving his head much, the way Kubo-san listens to an engine. Then he walked out into it. His shoes were black and polished, and the heels were worn down on the outside edges.

He stopped by the place where the cooler van had been and looked at the burst cans. He went on. Near where Okabe's van had stood, he stopped again and bent down, with a hand on his thigh, and picked something up off the concrete. A scrap of paper. A corner off a page. He held it up into the headlights and read it, and then he folded it once and put it in his breast pocket and buttoned the pocket.

Then he straightened up and turned round, slowly, and looked at the warehouses.

At our wall. At our doorway. At the dark in it.

I couldn't see his eyes, with his cap and the headlights behind him. I could see that his face was pointed at us. Between him and us there was thirty meters of moonlit concrete and, about halfway, lying on its side in the open, one black rubber kitchen sandal.

He looked at the sandal. Then he looked at the doorway again.

Nobu's breath was hot and wet on my palm, and his whole body was shaking, very fast and very small, and against the frame my shoulder had started to hurt and I didn't move it. Twenty. Thirty. He didn't take out a flashlight. He didn't call to the other one. He just stood there with his hands at his sides and looked at the dark where we were.

Forty-one.

The one in the car said something through the open window. I didn't catch it.

The older one turned and walked back to the Crown. He walked the same speed as before. He got in and shut the door, and the red light stopped turning, and the car went out of the lot the way it had come and turned up toward the bypass, and its engine went up through the gears, ordinary, and was gone.

I took my hand off Nobu's mouth. He wiped his face with both hands, his mouth and then his eyes.

"Sorry," he whispered.

"For what?"

"I don't know." His voice cracked in the middle. "I thought I was going to cry. I think I did a bit."

"It's fine."

"Was that a real policeman?"

"No," I said. "That was a man in a costume."

He laughed, one breath, shaky, into his knees.

We stayed there a long time after that. I counted to three hundred, twice. The moon came round the corner of the warehouse and started to come down the wall toward us, and then I was looking at it, white over the harbor and the cranes, and I remembered I'd said I'd look at it properly. So I did. I looked at it for Sota for a while, crouched in the doorway with my shoulder hurting, until I could tell him what it looked like, which was like a moon.

Then Nobu went out across the lot on his bare feet and picked up his sandal, the one the policeman had looked at, and stood holding it in the middle of all those black circles, and looked at it too.

* * *

We went back the way we'd come, up the straight and over the crossing, and for a long time neither of us said anything.

Nobu walked on one sandal and one bare foot, which meant he walked in a kind of limp, clap, pat, clap, pat, and at the freight line I gave him my socks. He put both on the bare foot, one over the other.

"Your shoe's got tape on it," he said.

"Yes."

"I've got a whole drawer of shoes." He didn't say it to be mean. He said it the way you'd say you had a cold. "My father buys them when they're on sale. In the wrong sizes. He says I'll grow." He looked down at the sandal. "These were by the back door. You can't open our shutter quietly."

At the canal he started talking again, and once he started he didn't stop. Goto's lift at the crossing. The sparks. The prices. The way Goto had held the notes up. He'd seen everything and he said all of it in the wrong order, and twice he stopped dead on the path to show me with his hands how the Chaser had wobbled, and I waited.

"I took one," he said, near the station.

"One what?"

"Photo. Before. When they lined up." He patted his bag. "It'll just be lights, probably. Two red lights. I didn't use the flash." He looked at me sideways. "Don't tell Goto."

"I'm not going to tell Goto."

"You didn't tell him you say you rode in it." He'd been holding that one since the lot. "He asked you twice. You said no. You stood on my foot."

"Yes."

"He called you bridge kid. He knew you." He walked a few steps. "And he said afterimages. And it's a white S13 with a big wing. You said a white S13 with a big wing, and I laughed." He wasn't laughing now. "So either you were lying to me, or you were lying to him."

I didn't answer. He walked for a while, clap, pat.

"I'd lie to him," he said. "If I were you. He's scary."

At the shutter of Kawamata Ramen he stopped and looked up at the window over the shop, which was dark. It was ten to four. He had two hours before his father got up for the stock, and he'd go in through the back and lie down on his futon in his shirt and be studying.

"Same time next Saturday?" he said.

"No."

"Okay." He nodded, very seriously. "Next Saturday, then."

He went down the side of the shop toward the back door, with one sandal on and two socks, as quietly as he could, which wasn't very.

I had more than an hour to lose. Mom got up at half past four and was out of the door by half past five, Sundays too. Aya would have told her I was staying over at Kawamata's. If I came in before half past five, in the black T-shirt, at the end of a night of studying, she'd look at me and see everything. So I walked the canal the slow way.

At the driving school I stopped at the fence. The floodlight on the tower was off. The four white sedans were parked in a row by the office with their noses to the wall, the school's name on their doors, and the moon on their roofs. The little hill. The fake traffic lights, switched off, three dead eyes on a pole.

Forty thousand yen for four minutes. Seven of my nights, and a bit.

You needed a car for that. Not a good one. Goto's had a dent in the door. The Starlet boy's was a shopping car with a turbo in it, and he'd had fifteen thousand to put down on himself and nobody to put down the rest. But it had to be a car, and somebody in it who could drive.

I stood with my fingers through the fence wire for a while and did the shifting, left hand, very small, so that nobody would have seen it from the canal path. Four to three. Three to two.

Then the sky over the factories went grey, and somewhere behind me the first fish truck went down toward the harbor with its refrigeration unit rattling, and I let go of the fence.

I had 1,077 yen, the same as when I'd left. My shift started at ten that night. At home there was a phone book under the phone.
