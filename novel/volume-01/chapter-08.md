# Chapter 8: Timing Belt

Kubo-san came through Tanabe-san's gate at ten past three on Sunday afternoon, the second Sunday in October, on a green Super Cub with a red steel toolbox roped onto the rack behind him. The toolbox was older than me. You could tell from the handle, where the paint had worn off to bare grey in the shape of somebody's fingers.

I was sweeping round the press. I stopped.

I'd told him on Thursday night, at about four, between two fish trucks. Not all of it. A Roadster at Tanabe's, an eighty-nine, sixty thousand yen, in Tanabe-san's name. The back pushed in. Doesn't run.

"What did it do?" he'd said.

"Died at a red light. In March. The man sat there turning the key, and a fish truck went into the back of him."

He'd turned a page of his newspaper and said nothing else all night, and I'd thought that was the end of it.

That morning, in the back lot, I'd stalled four times instead of eleven, and Natsume had taught me to stop: clutch down before it shakes, not after. Then she'd put her seat back one click short of the end and gone. Then I'd swept, and sorted bolts, and pulled the wiring out of a van, and swept again. I hadn't expected anything else from the day.

Kubo-san put the Cub on its stand by the office and got off it slowly, with one hand on the seat. Tanabe-san had come out onto the step with her cigarette.

"Kubo," she said. "Still alive."

"Tanabe-san."

"If you've come to buy, I'm shut. If you've come for the boy, he's working."

"Till five," Kubo-san said.

She looked at the toolbox, and at me with the broom, for long enough that the radio inside finished one enka and started another.

"Behind the Corollas," she said. "He sweeps the office after."

He untied the toolbox and carried it himself, low, in one hand, with his bad knee going stiff on every step, through the weeds to where my car was. He didn't let me take it.

He walked round the car once, past the white fender and the folded-up back and the trunk lid standing up in its peak, and looked only at the tires, once each. He crouched at the front, with a hand on the bumper, and looked at the ground under the engine, where the weeds were dead and dry and there was no dark patch.

"Nothing's dripped," he said.

The cat was on the passenger seat, in the sun that came through the tear in the top. It looked at him. He looked at it.

"It came with the car," I said.

I reached in under the dash by my knee and pulled the hood release. The hood jumped up a finger's width. He found the catch under the front edge without looking for it and lifted the hood and set the rod, and we both looked in.

Dust. A drift of dry leaves in the corner by the windshield, and brown cat hair on everything, and under all that the engine, sitting long and low with its cover across the top. I'd never opened it. I hadn't wanted to look at something I didn't know how to read.

He pulled the dipstick and wiped it on his thumb and finger, and rubbed the oil between them, and smelled it.

"Black," he said. "Not white. No water in it." He put the dipstick back. "Battery."

"In the trunk. The trunk doesn't open."

He went back through the weeds to the office and came out with a battery from the shelf by the door, the ones Tanabe-san takes out of wrecks and charges, with a chalk tick on the top. He carried it against his stomach in both arms and set it down on a piece of cardboard by my front wheel. Out of the toolbox came a pair of jump leads, wound up neat and tied with string.

He followed a thick cable with one finger, from where it came forward along the inside of the car from the back, to where it ended on a bolt under a red rubber cap. He folded the cap back and put the red clamp on the bolt. The black one he put on a bracket on the side of the engine, bare metal, after scratching it once with the clamp's teeth.

"The old one'll drink some of it," he said. "Doesn't matter. Get in. Clutch to the floor. Neutral. Turn it when I say. Stop when I say."

I got in. The cat stood up, and stretched, and went out through the tear in the top without hurrying.

I put the clutch down. The lever wobbled loose in the middle. I put the key in, which I'd done before with nothing happening, and turned it one click. Two red lights came on in the dash, a battery and an oil can. The same two as in hers.

I sat there and looked at them.

"Now," Kubo-san said.

I turned it.

The starter went round. It went round fast and high, rrr-rrrr-rr-rrrr, with hardly anything in it. At the station I'd heard a hundred cars start. Every starter has a limp in it before the engine takes over, a heavy beat, uh, uh, uh, uh, steady, every time a piston has to come up and squeeze. This one limped on some beats and skipped others, too quick and too light, and never the same twice. It spun like it had almost nothing to push.

"Stop."

I stopped. The quiet came back. A gull, somewhere over the stacks.

"Again."

I turned it. He stood at the front of the car with his head down and his hands at his sides, not looking at the engine. Not looking at anything. In August an old sedan had come in squealing, and he'd said "Belt's going" without turning his head.

"Stop. What do you hear?"

I thought about it.

"It's too fast," I said. "Half of it isn't pushing back."

He nodded.

He unscrewed a cap on top of the engine cover, the oil filler, and set it upside down on the fender. He took a flashlight out of the toolbox and gave it to me.

"Shine it down there. Tell me when that moves."

I got out and looked. Down in the hole, under a shine of old oil, there was a curved shape of steel like the top of an egg, with a drop hanging off it.

"That's a cam," he said. "When the engine turns, that turns."

He got down then. He did it slowly, one knee and then the other, onto a flattened box he'd pulled off the pile by the fence, and then onto his back, and slid in under the front of the car with a long bar and a big socket. I heard the socket go onto something, and a grunt, and then a long creak.

"Well?"

The steel in the hole hadn't moved. The drop of oil hadn't even fallen.

"Nothing."

Another creak. Another.

"That's once round," he said, from under the car. "Down here."

He came out, which took longer than going in, and stood with a hand on the fender until the knee let him go. Then he took a socket off the rail in the top drawer of the toolbox, put it on a ratchet, and started taking out the bolts round the black plastic cover at the top front of the engine. His hand went into the drawer and came out with the right socket. He put each bolt on the fender as it came out, in a row, in the order they came, and wiped each one with his thumb before he put it down.

The cover came off. Behind it were two pulleys side by side with teeth round the edge, each as big as a saucer.

Round them should have gone a belt. There was nothing round them. The belt lay in a loop down past them, broken, going away into the dark behind the lower covers, and on the piece I could see, a stretch as long as my hand had no teeth left at all, just a flat strip with threads coming out of the edges. Next to the flat part it had snapped right through. Inside the cover he'd taken off, caught along its bottom edge, there were black crumbs.

Kubo-san picked one of them out and held it up between his finger and thumb. It was a rubber tooth, square, smaller than my little fingernail.

"There," he said.

He dropped it into my palm. It weighed nothing.

"The bottom of the engine turns the top," he said. He pointed with the ratchet: down, toward the pulley at the bottom where his bar had been, then up at the two toothed ones. "Crank down there. Cams up here. The cams open the valves. That belt holds the two together. Belt goes, the bottom keeps turning. The top stops."

He took the stripped piece of belt between his finger and thumb and turned it over to the light.

"Something's been eating that belt," he said. "Probably the tensioner. We'll see when it's apart."

I looked at the tooth in my hand. In March a man had sat at the red light by the fish market with his hazards going, turning the key, and the starter had spun just like this, high and fast and limping wrong. Then the fish truck.

Nobu had told me once, at lunch, about an engine he couldn't afford anyway: if the belt went on that one, the pistons came up and hit the valves that had been left hanging open, and you might as well buy a new car. He'd said it the way he says horsepower figures, happily.

"Did it break the valves?" I said. "When the man kept turning it."

"Some engines, it would." Kubo-san put the ratchet down on the cardboard. "Not this one. The one-six has room. The valves hang open, the pistons come up, and they come up short."

"How short?"

"Short enough. Get under there," he said. "The bar's still on."

I lay down on his flattened box and slid in under the front. Under the car it smelled of old oil and dust and cat. The bar came down to me from a bolt in the middle of the pulley at the bottom of the engine. I could see Kubo-san's shoes by the front wheel, and his hand on his knee.

"Pull it. Toward you. Slowly."

I pulled. It was stiff, and then it gave, and the whole engine turned on the end of my arm, heavy and then easier and then heavy again, with a soft sigh from somewhere inside, like a bicycle pump.

"Keep going."

I kept going. Round, and round again. Heavy, easy, heavy.

"Anything touching in there," he said, from above me, "you'd feel it. In your hands. Before you heard it."

There was nothing. Only the sigh, and the creak of the bar, and the weight going round and coming back.

I let go of the bar and lay there with my hands on my chest, and the tooth in one of them.

"So it's a belt," I said.

"It's a belt. Nothing's jammed, anyway. The rest, a gauge tells you, once it's got a belt on."

Tanabe-san came round the end of the Corolla stacks with two cans of coffee from the office fridge. She gave one to Kubo-san. She didn't give one to me, under the car with my feet sticking out. She looked in at the open front of the engine, at the two pulleys with nothing round them.

"A belt," she said.

"And whatever ate it."

"Thirty-seven years." She took the cigarette out of the corner of her mouth just far enough to drink. "They still bring me cars with a belt."

She went back to the office.

Kubo-san put the cover back on, loose, with two bolts, against the dust. He wiped his hands on a rag he'd wet from the red can of gasoline Tanabe-san keeps for the forklift. Then he wiped them again on a clean corner. His hands were the same as always: the white burn scar on the back of the left one, black in the creases of every knuckle.

"You've done this before," I said.

"Some."

He folded the rag in four.

"Where?"

He looked at the car for a while, and then at the cranes over the stacks.

"I had a garage," he said. "My own. Twelve years."

"What happened to it?"

"The bubble."

He put the rag in the toolbox and shut the drawer.

"Belt," he said. "Tensioner and the idler, whatever they look like. The pump behind it, while it's open, or you do all this again in a year. The seals at the front, same reason. Then you line up the marks, top and bottom, put the new belt on, set it, and turn it over twice by hand. If the marks come back to the same place, it's right."

"How long?"

"An afternoon. If nothing's seized."

"How much are the parts?"

"I'll find out." He picked up the toolbox. "You'll pay me when I've got them."

He looked down at my tires, the two flat on the rims and the two with cracks in the sides you could stand a coin in.

"It'll want to roll," he said. "Before I do it. It can't stand on those."

* * *

He went at five, on the Cub, and said "Ten o'clock" as he went, which was the shift. I swept the office. I skipped the yard tap and wiped my hands on the gasoline rag like he had, and went home.

Mom was at the stove with her back to the room, frying something in the deep pan, and the kitchen was all oil and noise. Sota was at the low table doing a sheet of fractions. Aya was home, at the end of the table, with her hair magazine.

"I'm back."

"Wash," Mom said, to the pan.

I washed at the sink, under the clinic paper on the side of the fridge. The black didn't come out of the creases. It never comes out of Kubo-san's, either.

Aya got up to fill the rice bowls. To get to the cooker she had to come past me, and behind me she stopped. She breathed in once through her nose, short. I felt her look at me.

Then she filled the bowls and went back to her magazine, and turned a page.

Mom brought the plate to the table. Mackerel, in batter, because it had been cheap on Saturday, three pieces each and the small one for Sota. She sat down and looked at all of us, one after the other, counting. Then she ate. She didn't say anything about the smell. The kitchen was full of fish and oil.

After dinner I lay down on my futon behind the fusuma to sleep before the shift. Sota came in a bit later with his inhaler and the tube, and did it sitting on his own futon, and I counted for him on my fingers in the half-dark, ten and ten. He rinsed. He came back and lay down facing me, with his glasses still on.

"Did you ask it?" he whispered.

"Ask what?"

"Its name."

"It went out through the roof when I turned the key."

His eyes went big behind the glasses.

"You turned the key? Did it go?"

"No." I could hear Mom running water in the kitchen. "It's the belt. There's a belt inside that makes the top half go round with the bottom half, and it broke."

He thought about that.

"Can you buy a belt?"

"Kubo-san says you can."

He thought about that too.

"How much is a belt?"

"I don't know yet."

He turned onto his back and looked at the ceiling. I took his glasses off and folded them, the left arm over the right, and put them on the windowsill by the inhaler. He was asleep before I'd got back under the blanket.

On Monday I went to school straight from the pumps and came home at four and slept. When I got up to wash my face, there was a new bar of soap by the sink.

It wasn't our soap. Ours is white and smells of nothing. This was a block of grey, with grit in it you could see, in a paper wrapper with a drawing of two hands on it. The kind the fish-market men buy. The price sticker was still on the paper, from the supermarket where Aya works: 168 yen. She'd have put it out that morning, before her shift, while I was still at the pumps.

I picked it up and put it down.

Mom was behind the fusuma, asleep under her towel. Aya came home at half past nine. She hung her coat on the hook and went to the sink and washed her hands with the white soap, and dried them, and went to soak her feet. She didn't look at the grey one once.

I used it every time I came back from the yard after that. It took the black out of the creases, mostly, and stung where the cut had been.

* * *

Tuesday was Sports Day, a holiday, and I slept till two and worked the night. On Thursday Natsume filled up at ten past two and asked if my brother was still doing his inhaler, and I said morning and night, and she said "Good," and went. I didn't tell her about the belt.

On Sunday she put an old tire in the middle of the back lot and made me stop with the front bumper level with it, from first and then from second, and said "Again" eighteen times. I counted. She didn't. Then she went, and I took the wheels off my car.

I broke the wheel nuts loose with the cross-brace while it was still on the ground. Then Tanabe-san lifted the back of it onto two stacks of tires with the forklift, and then the front, and I spun the nuts off, and rolled the four wheels out of the weeds one at a time. Plain steel, rusty at the edges, no covers. The two flat tires flopped on the rims as they went round.

"Okabe's," Tanabe-san said. "I've got Crown wheels to take him anyway."

I stopped with the last wheel.

"Okabe," I said.

"Okabe Tire. By the canal." She looked at me over the cigarette. "Even a thief has his three reasons, they say. Okabe's got four. But he's the cheapest thief there is."

Her truck was a two-ton flatbed with TANABE on the doors in the same faded pink as the gate. We loaded eight Crown wheels with good tires on them, and my four, and the forklift lifted the lot up onto the back on a pallet in one go. I got in on the left. The seat had a towel over it, and the floor was all sand and receipts. She drove with both hands on the wheel at the top, slowly, with the cigarette in, and leaned forward at every crossing as if she didn't trust it.

At the end of the harbor road she turned inland, along the canal, past the back of the fish market. I'd been to Pier 3 once. A man had sat in the back of a white van in the middle of it, with a cash box on his knees, and somebody had called across the lot, "Okabe-san," and he'd lifted his pencil without looking up.

Okabe Tire was a long tin shed open at the front, between a welding shop and a coffee shop with its shutter down and a FOR LEASE sign in the window. Tires stood in towers outside, used ones, tied in fours with rope. Inside it was dark, with a car up on a lift, and a machine for taking tires off wheels, and a balancing machine with a wheel spinning on it, whining. And parked to one side, nose out, the white van with the name painted over in a white that didn't quite match. In daylight you could read it easily. OKABE TIRE.

"I'm not getting out for him," Tanabe-san said, and pressed the horn twice.

He came out wiping his hands on a rag. The same soft round face, the same reading glasses pushed up into his hair. Navy overalls instead of a polo shirt, with the sleeves rolled. He bowed to the truck window and said "Tanabe-san, always a pleasure," and she said "Eight Crown wheels, and don't start," and he laughed. She left the engine running and turned the radio up.

I'd got down and gone round to the back, to the pallet. He came round after me to look at the wheels, and looked at me instead. Then he looked at me a second time.

"Ah," he said. Pleasant. Not loud. "The kid who watches money."

In the cab the radio was doing the horse racing.

"I didn't see you again," he said. "Most of them come back the next Saturday."

"I work."

"Good. Work's good." He looked at the four steel wheels on the pallet, and his face went straight into business. "Base car. Fourteen-inch steel. One-eighty-five, sixty, fourteen. What's it on?"

"An eighty-nine Roadster."

"Whose?"

"Tanabe-san's."

He looked along the truck at the cab. Tanabe-san was looking straight ahead through the windshield with her cigarette.

"Of course," Okabe said.

He took me round to the side of the shed, where the used tires for small cars stood in towers by size, with chalk marks on them. He pulled a set of four out and stood them up in a row.

"Bridgestones. All four the same. Five millimeters left. Four thousand each, on and balanced." He watched me. "You don't want those."

"No."

"No." He pulled out four more, two the same and two that weren't, and stood them in a new row. "These, then. Look."

He crouched, with a grunt, and put his thumb on the shoulder of the first one, a Yokohama. On the edge of the tread there was a little triangle molded into the rubber.

"Slip sign," he said. "Find the triangle, and in the groove under it there's a bar across. When the tread's down level with the bar, the tire's finished. One point six millimeters." He put a little gauge into the groove and showed me the number. "This one's got four."

He turned the tire round to the side, and found a small oval with numbers in it, among all the other numbers.

"Two, four, three. Twenty-fourth week, nineteen ninety-three. June. Rubber goes hard, old. These are young enough."

He moved to the third, an odd one. "This one's plugged." He showed me, in the middle of the tread, a little stub of brown rubber poking out of a hole, trimmed flat. "Nail. Somebody fixed it properly. I'd drive on it. I wouldn't drive on it fast." He gave me the gauge. "You do the other two."

I found the triangles. I put the gauge in the grooves under them. On the second Yokohama, four millimeters. On the last one, three and a half. I found the ovals. Two, four, three. One, one, two.

"This one's the same as the first one," I said. "This one's ninety-two."

"So?"

"Older."

"Harder." He took the gauge back. "The two Yokohamas, two thousand five hundred each. The other two, fifteen hundred each, because one's plugged and one's old. Better pair on the back. Fitting and balancing, seven hundred a wheel. That's eight thousand for the rubber and two thousand eight hundred for the work. Ten thousand eight hundred." He did the tax without stopping. "Eleven thousand one hundred and twenty-four. Old tires, three hundred each to get rid of."

"I'll take them back."

"Tanabe-san's got a press, hasn't she." He smiled. "Call it eleven thousand. The hundred and twenty-four's for coming back."

He did the four of them while I watched. The machine broke the old tires off the rims with a thump of air, one side and then the other. He levered the new ones on with a bar, quick, with soap on the bead from a pot with a brush in it, and the air hose put them on with a bang. Then the balancer. The wheel spun and stopped, and a needle pointed, and he hammered a lead weight onto the rim, and spun it again. One weight on the first. Two on the second. One on each of the last two.

I gave him a 10,000-yen note and a 1,000. The money from the dictionary. He took them without counting, the same as at Pier 3, and wrote a receipt on a pad with a carbon underneath.

"Name?"

"Tanabe."

He looked at me over his glasses for a second. Then he wrote TANABE, and tore off the top copy, and gave it to me.

"Business is business," he said.

We drove back with my four wheels on new tires lying on the pallet, and the four old ones loose beside them, rolling a little at every corner. At one light Tanabe-san said, "Four thousand a wheel. He said three and a half."

At the yard she lowered the car onto its wheels with the forklift, the back and then the front, slowly, and I did the nuts up in a star with the cross-brace, the order she showed me, and then again, harder. The forks came out from under. The car sank down onto the tires and sat there.

It stood level for the first time since I'd known it. Its right headlight was still stuck halfway up, staring at the fence. The back was still folded. But it stood on its own four feet in the weeds, and when I pushed on the front fender, it rocked on its springs and came back.

The cat came out of the weeds and walked round it once, slowly, smelling each new tire. Then it jumped in through the tear in the top, onto the passenger seat, and lay down.

I'd had 65,287 yen after payday. I had 54,287. I didn't do the sum with the 280,000 in it. I did the one with the 7,000 for Tanabe-san at the end of the month, and it came out fine.

* * *

On Monday I'd been awake since half past four on Sunday morning. The tires had taken the hours I usually sleep before the shift.

I made it through first period by holding my pen. In second, math, I was writing something down off the board, and then the bell was going for the end of it and my cheek was on my forearm and there was a line of ink across my notebook that went off the edge of the page. In fourth I didn't make it past the first five minutes. In sixth, English, Hotta-sensei's, I sat up straight with my eyes open, and the words on the board went in and out of focus, and then Okuda was kicking my chair, and Hotta-sensei was standing beside my desk with the textbook, not saying anything.

He saved it for the end of homeroom.

"Hayama. Staff room."

The staff room smelled of green tea and cigarettes. The desks were pushed together in islands, piled with papers and textbooks and tea cups, and a teacher at the far end was asleep in his chair with a newspaper over his face. Hotta-sensei had a grey cardigan on now, instead of the towel. The owl fan was standing in a jar of pens on his desk, closed.

He sat, and left me standing, and looked at a sheet of paper on his desk with names on it.

"Ono-sensei says you've slept through every math lesson on a Monday since September," he said. "And on Wednesdays. And Fridays. Iwai-sensei says the same for Japanese history, and she doesn't usually say anything about anybody." He put the paper down. "In my lesson you sit up straight and sleep with your eyes open. I'd almost rather you put your head down."

"Sorry."

"Midterms start on the twenty-fourth. A week tomorrow." He folded his hands on the desk. "Fail a subject for the year and you don't get the credit. No credits, no graduation. No graduation, no job. Every offer they send says on condition of graduation. Every one."

"Sanko hasn't said yet."

"No. They're slow. They always are." He looked out of the window, at the sports ground, where the baseball team was running with their caps on, the same as in September. "The cardboard factory had its exam on the fourth. I didn't send you. One company at a time, that's the rule, and I'm not breaking it for you."

I looked at the closed fan in the jar of pens.

"When they take somebody from here, they take him because the school says he's all right," he said. "I wrote it. I wrote, never late. I wrote, works hard. If you don't graduate, or you don't turn up in April, they remember the school. Next year they take three from us, not five. Not you. The boys after you."

At the next island, a teacher was marking papers in red with a hand that never stopped.

"I know you work nights," Hotta-sensei said, quieter. "Your mother told me, in September. I haven't written it down anywhere. Nobody's asked me." He looked at me properly. "I'd rather nobody had to."

"Yes."

"Sleep in the afternoons. Not in Ono-sensei's math."

"Yes."

He looked at my hands. They were on the edge of his desk. Even after the grey soap they were dark in the creases of the knuckles, and there was black round two of the nails that wouldn't come out.

"And wash before the exams," he said. "They'll think you've started at the press already."

He opened a drawer and took out a stack of English worksheets and began going through them, which meant I could go.

I walked home along the canal and didn't look at the driving school. At home I got my math textbook out for the first time since September and opened it on the low table, and woke up on it at seven with Sota standing over me holding his inhaler.

* * *

At six on Wednesday morning Kubo-san put the brown envelope on the counter with HAYAMA on it in pencil. Eight nights, written on the back in his small square numbers: Thursday the fifth to Tuesday the seventeenth. 44,800.

"Parts come Saturday," he said.

"How much?"

"Saturday."

I walked home with the envelope inside my jacket. Mom had gone to the factory, and Aya was asleep. I counted 22,400 onto the shoe cabinet, two 10,000s and two 1,000s and four 100-yen coins, in a row, the coins on top of the notes so they wouldn't blow. I put the other 22,400 in the dictionary. That made 76,200 in there, and 487 in my pocket.

I went to school. I stayed awake for all of math, and most of history, by pressing my thumbnail into the line on my palm. In English I was awake for all of it. Hotta-sensei didn't look at me once.

When I came home at ten past four, Mom was sitting at the low table.

She should have been asleep. She sleeps from half past two until six, behind the fusuma, with the damp towel over her eyes, and gets up and cooks and goes to the supermarket at half past seven. The towel was on the table in front of her, folded, dry. Next to it was an envelope. Long, white, with a window, and in the corner, printed in red: SANKO BRAKE INDUSTRIES, LTD.

Sota was on his cushion by the window with a comic. He wasn't reading it.

"It was in the box," Mom said. "When I came in."

It had my name in the window. HAYAMA DAISUKE. She hadn't opened it. She pushed it across the table to me with two fingers.

I sat down and opened it with my thumb, along the top, and took out two sheets folded in three.

*Notice of Provisional Employment*, it said at the top of the first one.

*Mr. Hayama Daisuke. Thank you for sitting our employment examination on 19 September. After careful consideration, we are pleased to inform you that we have decided to offer you employment, as follows.*

*Position: Production (Shiomi Plant).*

*Start: 1 April 1996.*

*This offer is conditional upon your graduation from high school in March 1996 and upon the results of the health examination. Please return the enclosed form through your school by 31 October. Your school has been notified separately.*

At the bottom was a red company stamp, and under it, *Personnel Section*, and Sugimoto's name, and the little round stamp that was his.

I read it to the end. Then I turned it round on the table and pushed it back to her.

She read it. She read the whole thing, down to the stamp. Then she went back to the top and read it again, slowly this time, with her finger under the lines.

She put it down. She put both hands flat on the table on either side of it, and stood up, with her weight on them.

She went to the stove. She took the kettle off the back ring and filled it at the sink and put it on, and lit the gas, and then she stood there with her back to the room and one hand flat on the counter beside the stove. Her shoulders went up. They stayed up, under the cardigan. She didn't make any noise. The gas ticked as the kettle warmed, and the clock on the wall above the phone went round, and outside on the stairs somebody's kid was going down two steps at a time, counting.

Sota had put his comic face down on the tatami. He looked at me. I looked at the letter.

Her shoulders came down. She turned the gas off before the kettle had even started to sing. She didn't make any tea.

She came back to the table and picked up the letter, and went to the refrigerator. On the door was the garbage calendar, held up by two round magnets from the gas company. She took one off, so that the calendar hung crooked by one corner, and put the letter up in the middle of the door with it, flat, and pressed it twice with her thumb to make sure.

Then she picked up the towel off the table, and wet it at the sink, and wrung it out.

"There's rice from yesterday," she said. "Heat it for him at six. I'll do the miso when I'm up."

She went behind the fusuma. I heard her lie down.

Sota picked his comic up again.

Aya came home at half past nine. She stood in front of the refrigerator with her coat still on and read the letter, all of it, with her bag on her shoulder.

"Good," she said, to the refrigerator. "Now you can pay me my three thousand yen."

* * *

Wednesday is a night off. I lay down at ten, and at a quarter to four my eyes came open on their own, in time for a shift I didn't have.

At four I was standing in the kitchen in the dark, in my T-shirt, with the floor cold under my feet. The only light came in through the frosted glass over the sink, from the lamp on the stairs outside, and it was enough to see the shapes of things. The low table. The phone on its shelf. The clinic paper on the side of the refrigerator, Mom's handwriting just grey lines from here. And on the door, a white square, with the calendar hanging crooked beside it.

I couldn't read it. I tried, by opening the refrigerator, but it was on the door, and when the light came on the letter swung away from me with the door, into the dark. I shut it again. I didn't need to read it. *Production. Shiomi Plant. 1 April 1996. Conditional upon your graduation.*

The refrigerator hummed. Behind the fusuma Sota turned over, and Mom breathed, slow, and somebody in the next flat ran a tap.

My jacket was hanging on the hook in the genkan. I went and put my hand in the pocket, the one with the gloves in it and Sota's list, and found it by feel, at the bottom, in the seam. The little square rubber tooth off the belt. I took it back into the kitchen in my closed hand, and stood in front of the refrigerator with it, and looked at the letter I couldn't read, until the first fish truck went by on the main road below, toward the harbor, grinding through its gears at the corner.
