# Chapter 2: Second Term

The opening ceremony for second term was in the gym at a quarter to nine, and the principal talked for twenty-two minutes. I know because the clock above the stage was the only thing in the gym I could look at without my eyes closing.

Eight hundred of us stood on the basketball lines in rows, by year and by class, in white summer shirts gone see-through at the back. The windows up under the roof were open and let in the cicadas and nothing else. Somewhere in the second-years a girl sat down on the floor without meaning to, and two teachers walked her out by the elbows.

I'd had an hour's sleep. An hour and five minutes, if you count the part where I lay on my futon doing third gear.

The principal talked about the earthquake in January and the gas on the subway in March. He said they showed that a life could change in one morning, and that was why we had to make every day count. He said that for the third-years this was the last second term we would ever have, and we should think hard about where we would be standing next September. I thought about the pump island. Then I thought about the overpass, and the new guardrail with its clean bolts, and I swayed forward and caught myself on my toes.

Hotta-sensei was standing at the side with the other teachers, towel round his neck, owl fan going. He didn't do anything. He just looked at me until I stood up straight.

Back in the classroom he had a stack of paper on his desk, sorted into piles with paper clips. The ones going to university got nothing, because there were four of them and they already knew where they were going. The ones going to vocational school got a talk about forms. The rest of us got called up by name.

"These came to the school in July," he said. "The companies send them. Read them at home. Read them with your parents. Applications go in on Tuesday, and the first exams are on the sixteenth. If you want a company that isn't on a sheet, come and see me."

He sat down and wiped his neck with the towel.

Okuda, who sat next to me, got a supermarket chain and a bakery, and held them side by side and couldn't decide which one he hated less. The girl in front got a bank, and turned round and showed it to her friend with both hands. The bank was a big one. You could tell from the stamp.

"Hayama."

Mine was one sheet, with a slip of paper clipped to the corner that said HAYAMA D. in his handwriting. It was typed, with a company stamp in red at the bottom.

SANKO BRAKE INDUSTRIES, LTD. SHIOMI PLANT.
Position: production line (brake linings).
Starting pay: 148,000 yen per month.
Bonus: twice yearly.
Social insurance: yes.
Dormitory: yes (single men).
Hours: 8:00 to 16:45. Overtime as required.
Holidays: Sundays, two Saturdays a month, summer, New Year.
Start: 1 April 1996.

I read the number twice. 148,000 yen. Kubo-san paid me 5,600 a night. I did the division before I'd decided to. Twenty-six nights and a bit. I'd never worked twenty-six nights in a month. Nobody has.

When the bell went, Hotta-sensei said, "Hayama. A minute," and the others went round me on their way to the door.

We stood in the corridor by the open window. Down on the sports ground the baseball team was running in the heat with their caps on, and their coach was shouting numbers at them.

"Sanko takes four or five from us most years," he said. "It's hot and it's loud, and they pay on the twenty-fifth of every month. The test's on the nineteenth. Written in the morning, interview after lunch."

"I didn't say I wanted it."

"No. Your mother did."

I looked at him. He fanned his neck.

"I phoned your house on Wednesday afternoon," he said. "She said you were asleep."

"I work nights."

"She told me." He folded the fan and opened it again. "She asked a lot of questions. What they'd want to know. What you should wear. I told her the truth. Your grades are bad. Your attendance is good, which surprised me. The rest, they don't need to know, and if they ask, you answer short and you don't get angry."

"And if they don't want me?"

"Then there's a cardboard factory on the fourth of October." He looked out of the window. "I've had boys leave here with nothing lined up. Some of them come back to the gate in the summer and ask if anything's come in. By then there's nothing."

He tapped the sheet in my hand with the edge of the fan, once.

"Come awake for the nineteenth. That's all I'm asking."

* * *

Kawamata was waiting at my shoe locker.

"Sanko," he said, reading the sheet upside down while I changed my shoes. "Brake linings. For trucks and delivery vans. Not the good stuff. Nobody puts Sanko on a GT-R. But every kei truck in this city stops because of Sanko. They did a page on brake linings once, and Sanko wasn't even in it. That's how you know it's a real company. Nobody writes about it. My father buys their linings for the delivery scooter."

I gave him his pen back. The four-color one. I'd carried it for a week.

"Thanks, Nobu."

It came out before I'd decided anything. He stopped with his hand halfway out, and looked at me, and then he took the pen and looked at that instead.

"You can keep it," he said.

"It's yours."

"I know. It's my only one with green." He put it in his shirt pocket and patted it. "Did you ride in it again?"

"No."

"See, that I believe. You're improving."

He didn't have a sheet. His father had decided years ago that Nobu was going into the shop, and Nobu had decided that he was going to write for *Street Tune* in Tokyo. They'd been not talking about it since spring.

"Who wins?" I said.

"My father. He's got the knives." He held the door for me. "But they printed my letter."

I walked home along the canal in the white heat. My shoe slapped. I didn't take the bus, because the bus was 160 yen.

The canal way goes past Shiomi Driving School. I'd walked past it for three years without looking at it once. That day I stopped at the fence.

It was a small course, all painted lines and little hills and a set of fake traffic lights that worked, with a tower in the middle where a man sat with a microphone. Four white sedans with the school's name on the doors were going round it at about 15 km/h. One of them was stopped on the slope with its hazard lights on. As I watched, it rolled back a meter, jerked, stalled, and rolled back again. Through the windshield I could see the girl at the wheel with her hands up by her face. The instructor beside her didn't move at all. Then his foot went down on the floor on his side, and the car stopped rolling, and I saw that he had a brake pedal of his own.

The girl started it again. It stalled again. The man in the tower said something through the speaker that I couldn't make out, in a voice that had said it a thousand times.

I stood there until the girl got up the hill. It took her five tries. Then I went home.

* * *

I was asleep on the tatami before Mom got back from the factory, and the smell of hot oil woke me at half past five.

It took me a while to know what it was. We didn't fry things. Frying meant a whole bottle of oil and the kitchen at forty degrees, and in September nobody in our block fried anything unless it was New Year. I lay there with my face on the mat and listened to it spit.

Mom was at the stove with a towel over her shoulder and her hair coming out of its bun in wet strands. There was a plate beside her lined with newspaper, and on the newspaper, piled up, karaage. Real karaage, brown and rough, the size of Sota's fist.

Sota was kneeling at the low table with his hands in his lap and his eyes on the stove.

Mom saw me in the doorway and pointed at my bag with the long cooking chopsticks.

"Hotta-sensei said there'd be a sheet."

It wasn't a question. I got it out of my bag. It had been folded in four and slept on, and she took it from me with the hand that wasn't holding the chopsticks and read it standing at the stove, with the oil going behind her. She read the whole thing. Then she went back to the top and read it again, and I watched her lips move on the number.

"Sanko," she said.

She put the sheet on top of the refrigerator, flat, where nothing would spill on it. Then she dropped four more pieces into the oil, and it roared, and she stood back from it with her face turned away.

The front door opened, and Aya came in from her shift with her name badge still pinned to her shirt. She looked at the plate. She looked at the kitchen bin, where the chicken tray was sitting on top. She picked the tray up between two fingers and turned it to the light.

"812 yen," she said. "No sticker." She put it back. "Who died?"

"Nobody died," Mom said. "Wash your hands."

"It's not even reduced. You bought chicken at full price. On a normal Friday."

"Wash your hands, Aya."

Aya washed her hands. She looked at me over the sink. I looked at the ceiling.

Sota had carried his cicada board to school that morning, and he'd been waiting all afternoon to tell somebody what his teacher said when she saw it.

"She said it was very honest," he said. "She said nobody else had any I don't knows. Everybody else knew all of theirs."

"Everybody else guessed," Aya said.

"That's what she said."

Mom put rice in all the bowls, put the karaage plate in the middle and sat down last. Nobody reached for it. Then Sota did, and everybody else did, and for a while there was only eating. The inside was hot and the outside cracked. There was ginger in it. I hadn't known she knew how to make it. I'd never asked where she learned anything.

The TV was on low with the weather. A typhoon was coming up from the south toward Okinawa, a white spiral on the map, and the man with the pointer said it would probably turn away from us.

"148,000," Mom said.

She said it to her rice bowl. Then she put the bowl down.

"Every month. On the same day. Not every other Wednesday, every month, and when you're sick they still pay you. You get a card with your name on it for the hospital. Twice a year a bonus." She was counting on the table with one finger, the way she counts at the end of the month with the bills. "There's a dormitory, but you won't need it. It's twenty minutes on the bus. You'd be home by six."

"I've got a job," I said.

"You've got a job at night that pays you in an envelope. When you're twenty it'll still pay you in an envelope. When you're forty." She picked up her chopsticks and put them down. "Kubo-san is a good man. He's not a company."

"It's a test. I'm bad at tests."

"You passed English."

"With forty-four."

"Forty is a pass. You'll wear your uniform. I'll wash the shirt on Sunday." She looked at me then, straight across the table. "Hotta-sensei says they don't want clever. They want someone who comes every day. Four mornings a week you come home with no sleep and go straight out again to school, and you've never once been late for it. You think I don't know."

I didn't have anything to say to that. Aya was watching me with her chopsticks stopped in the air. Sota was watching Mom.

On the newspaper under the karaage there was one piece left, a small one, burnt on one side.

"I'll sit it," I said.

Mom didn't say anything. She picked up the last piece of karaage and put it in my bowl, on top of the rice. Then she got up and went to the rice-shop calendar on the wall by the phone and wrote SANKO in the square for the nineteenth, in red felt pen.

"One week," Aya said.

"What?"

"I give him one week. Then he finds a reason."

"Aya," Mom said.

"I'm just saying. I'm saying it now so nobody's surprised." She took the last of the rice. "If he's still in next Friday I'll cut his hair for the interview. For free. Write that down too."

Mom didn't write it down. She put the cap on the pen.

I ate my rice from round the edges and left the burnt piece where she'd put it. When she ran the water for the dishes, I moved it onto a saucer and turned a teacup over it.

Sota leaned over to me with his chopsticks still in his hand.

"Nii-chan. If you make brakes, can you make some for the red car?"

"They make them for vans."

"Vans need brakes too." He said it as if somebody had been unfair to vans. Then he looked at Mom's back and lowered his voice. "Will you still do the feet? If you go to the factory?"

"The factory's in April."

"But will you?"

"Next week," I said. "I said next week."

After Mom had gone to the supermarket, I lifted the teacup off the saucer and ate the burnt piece cold, standing at the sink. It was the best one.

* * *

I don't remember Friday's shift. On Saturday I slept until noon, and in the afternoon Mom sat at the low table with the Sanko application and filled in the boxes for the parent in her small, square handwriting. Then she took the hanko out of the drawer by the phone, breathed on it, pressed it into the box beside her name, and blew on the red ink until it was dry.

On Sunday night, at one o'clock, a drunk wanted to pay for his gas with a watch. Kubo-san took the watch, looked at the back of it and gave it back. Then he took 2,000 yen out of the man's wallet himself, while the man watched, and buttoned the change into his shirt pocket for him.

At four, before the trucks started back, I told him about Sanko. I hadn't meant to. He was on the stool by the oil rack with his coffee and I was sweeping round his feet, and it came out.

He didn't say anything for a while.

"Brake linings," he said.

"For vans."

"Sanko linings are all right. Cheap. They squeal when they're cold." He drank some coffee. "When's the test?"

"The nineteenth."

"That's a Tuesday. Come in at ten. Don't come in early to tell me how it went."

"You think I should do it."

"I think you should sit the test." He put the can down on the oil rack. "Sitting it costs you a day. You can say no to a job. You can't say no to a job nobody gave you."

"And then?"

"And then I need a new boy for nights." He got up off the stool, slowly, with a hand on the rack. "Your corner. You missed it."

I'd missed it.

On Tuesday morning I gave the application to Hotta-sensei before homeroom. He checked the stamp and the boxes and put it on the pile.

That night the typhoon went past Okinawa and turned away, and all it left us was a warm rain that came sideways under the canopy at midnight and went straight in through the toe of my right shoe.

Kubo-san watched me squelch from the pump to the office twice. The third time he went into the back room and came out with a roll of silver tape. He didn't say anything. He pointed at the stool. I sat, and he crouched down with his bad knee cracking and wound the tape round the toe of my sneaker four times, tight, and tore it off with his teeth.

"Thank you," I said.

"Water gets in your shoe, you walk funny," he said. "Customers think you're drunk."

It held. It was still holding at six on Wednesday morning when he came out of the office with the envelopes.

He had two, the day man's and mine. Mine was brown, with HAYAMA in pencil on the front and the money inside in a fold. I didn't open it in front of him. I said thank you and he said, "Eight nights," and went back to the coin tray.

I opened it on the bench at the bus stop on the harbor road, with my back to the road. Four 10,000-yen notes, four 1,000s and eight 100-yen coins. I counted it twice. 44,800 yen. Eight nights in two weeks, and all of them in my legs.

I walked home with my hand on the pocket.

The apartment was grey and quiet. Mom had gone to the factory an hour before. I took my shoes off in the genkan and counted half onto the shoe cabinet, where I'd put it the first time: two 10,000s, two 1,000s, four 100s. 22,400. I put the coins on top of the notes so the fan wouldn't move them.

The rest went into my English dictionary, coins and all, into the envelope with the 17,600. It made 40,000 exactly. I liked that. I took it out and put it back in to feel it again.

"Nii-chan."

Sota was in the doorway of the tatami room in his pajamas, with his glasses on. He'd been waiting. I could tell by his face that he'd been waiting since the door.

"It's next week," he said.

"What is?"

"You said feet are next week. It's next week."

It was ten to seven. He had to leave for school at a quarter to eight. I hadn't slept since Tuesday afternoon, and I'd promised.

I made the pedals out of what we had. The clutch was the phone book from under the phone. The brake was my English dictionary, which was the right height and had 40,000 yen in it. The gas was a floor cushion folded in half, a little lower than the brake, as in my drawing. I put them in a row on the tatami and sat Sota down behind them with his back against his futon.

"Left foot's only for the clutch," I said. "It doesn't do anything else."

"Why?"

"Because it's stupid."

He looked at his left foot. "It's not stupid."

"It's stupid at driving. Right foot does the brake and the gas."

"Both?"

"Both."

He put his right foot on the dictionary and pressed, carefully, with his toes curled. I showed him with my hand on his ankle: ball of the foot on the brake, then roll the heel over onto the cushion, quick, a hiccup, and off again. His foot was too small. The heel didn't reach. He tried it four times and his face went red.

"Nobu says some cars have the pedals too far apart," I said, "unless you've got enormous feet."

"My feet aren't enormous."

"So use the side." I turned his foot so the outside edge came down on the cushion. "Some people do it like that. It depends on the car."

"Which car?"

"Yours."

He did it with the side of his foot, and it reached, and he did it again. I put his left hand on an invisible lever and talked him through the whole thing, slowly, in the order of the photographs in the book. Brake. Clutch in. Neutral. Blip. Lower gear. Then the clutch comes up, and not before the lever's home, because if you let the clutch up with the lever halfway, you've got nothing at all.

"Brake," Sota said, under his breath. "Clutch. Neutral. Blip." His chest made its little whistle, and he slowed down by himself without me saying anything, and kept going.

He did it nine times. On the ninth time it was nearly in the right order.

"When you get a car," he said, with his foot still on the dictionary, "can I sit in it?"

"You'll be first."

"Before Mom?"

"Mom won't want to."

"Before Aya?"

"Aya won't fit. Her mouth's too big."

Behind the fusuma something crashed, and then Aya's alarm went off and kept going until she hit it. The fusuma slid open. She stood there in her tank top with her hair flat on one side, and looked down at the two of us on the floor with our feet on the phone book and the dictionary.

"Day five," she said.

She stepped over the clutch on her way to the toilet.

At a quarter to eight I stood in the doorway and watched Sota go down the stairs with his bag, all four floors. On every landing he stopped, put the ball of his right foot on the edge of the step and rolled the side of it over, and went on down.

I didn't sleep. I washed my face at the kitchen sink, put my uniform on and went to school, and slept through third period with my head on my arms. Hotta-sensei let me.

When I got home in the afternoon the shoe cabinet was empty. Mom was at the low table with the rent envelope in front of her, and the 22,400 was already in it.

"Aya says you're teaching Sota to drive on the phone book," she said.

"Only the feet."

She nearly laughed. She put her hand over her mouth and looked at the envelope instead, and when she left for the supermarket that evening the nearly was still on her face.

Wednesday was my night off. After she'd gone I took the receipt out of my pocket, the onigiri one, folded in four and soft now as cloth, and did the sum again under the kitchen light.

280,000 take away 41,077 left 238,923.

The old number on the receipt was 261,213. Take 22,400 off that and you got 238,813. The two didn't match. I looked for the mistake for a long time before I found it. 110 yen. It was the coffee. The can I'd bought last Thursday at 1:40 in the morning, because I wanted something in my hand. It was in the sum now. It's still in there.

I wrote the new number under the old one and didn't cross anything out.

* * *

On Thursday night Kubo-san had me up a ladder at eleven, changing the price on the sign out by the road.

The numbers were big white plastic tiles that hung on hooks, and they were greasy, and gritty on the backs. REGULAR 116 came down. REGULAR 115 went up. The big station on the bypass had gone to 114 on Monday, Kubo-san said, and he wasn't going to 114 for anybody, but he'd go to 115 so nobody could say he didn't try.

"Is that how it works?"

"That's how it works this year." He held the ladder with one hand and his coffee with the other. "Next year they change the law, and anybody who wants can bring gas in. There'll be a pump in the fish shop."

I hung the 5 and climbed down. Kubo-san looked up at the sign for a while.

"Cheap," he said, and went back to the office.

After the rain on Tuesday the heat had come back worse, and the night was thick and still. The asphalt under the pumps gave the day's heat back up through my shoes, through the silver tape. The cranes on the container pier had their red lights going on and off at the top. A ship was in. You could hear it, a low hum under everything, all night, like a refrigerator in the next room.

I worked. A taxi at 11:40. Two trucks at midnight, one after the other, the drivers in undershirts, and then it was Friday. A man in a suit at 12:30 who wanted the toilet key and nothing else, and who gave it back to me wet. Between them I stood on the pump island and didn't practice anything with my feet. I didn't look at the clock in the office window more than about every four minutes.

At home, on the rice-shop calendar, there was a red word in the square for the nineteenth.

I had the words ready. I'd had them since Wednesday night, on my futon with the receipt on my chest, and I'd said them to the ceiling until Sota told me to be quiet. Two words. I'd tried longer ones, and all the longer ones turned into excuses.

At 1:30 I heard Kubo-san laugh at a caller through the back-room wall, one short bark, and then argue. At 1:55 I wiped down the regular nozzle, which was clean.

At 2:05 I heard her.

It came off the bypass and down onto the harbor road, far off, and it went up, the way nothing else on that road did. I didn't move at first. I stood on the island with the rag in my hand until I could be sure, and then the pop-ups came round the bend by the rail crossing, two round lights, and I was sure.

She came in slow under the canopy and stopped at the second island, as she always did, nose out toward the road. The engine idled for a second, a quick light tick under it. She switched the lights off and the headlights folded down into the hood, and then the engine stopped.

I walked over. I didn't run. It was about eleven steps, and I'd decided on Wednesday night that I'd walk them.

The window came down. She had her gloves on, black, with the knuckles cut out, and her hair was tied back low, as it had been every time. Her face was the same. I don't know what I'd expected. That missing a week would show on it somewhere.

"Hayama," she said.

"Evening."

"Regular. Fill it up."

I went round to the filler on the right rear fender and opened it and put the nozzle in. The pump started counting. It was very loud in the quiet, the gasoline going in, the numbers clicking over. I watched the liters. I could see her in the side mirror, looking ahead through the windshield at the harbor road with both hands still on the wheel, the way she'd sat on the pier lot after the lap. She didn't look back at me in the mirror. I'd have seen.

31.2 liters. At 115 yen, 3,588 yen. I hung the nozzle up and screwed the cap on and shut the little door, and I did her windshield, all of it, the corners too, while she waited and didn't say anything about it.

Then I went to the window.

"3,588 yen."

She held out four 1,000-yen notes between two fingers, Natsume Sōseki four times over, moustache and all. I took them to the office and got her change from the drawer without looking at Kubo-san's door, and brought it back. 412 yen. She held out her hand flat for it, and I put the coins in her glove, the 100s first and then the small ones on top, and that was when I said it.

"You didn't come last week."

"I had a shift."

That was all. A shift of what, she didn't say. Mom had shifts. So did I. It didn't tell me anything, and I didn't ask, because I hadn't come over to ask that. She closed her hand round the coins and put them in the tray under the radio without counting them.

I was supposed to step back then. I knew the order. Window, money, change, step back, she starts the car, the lights come up, she goes. I'd done it twice. I put my hand on the bottom of the window frame instead, where the glass goes down into the door.

"Natsume-san."

She looked at my hand. Then at me.

"Teach me."

The cranes blinked. Somewhere out on the bypass a truck went through its gears.

"I don't teach," she said.

"I know where neutral goes now. I read it. I know the order. Brake, clutch, neutral, blip, gear, clutch up. I've done it every night since the lap."

"On your futon."

"...Yes."

"On air."

"On air, and the pump island, and a phone book."

She didn't laugh. Her left hand was resting on the gear lever, on the black knob, and her thumb moved on it once, from the top to the side and back.

"I'll pay you," I said.

"With what."

"I got paid on Wednesday. Every other Wednesday, I get paid."

"Congratulations."

"I mean it."

"I know you do." She turned her face back to the windshield. "Hand."

I didn't move it. My heart was going so hard I could feel it in the hand, against the paint. I'd never kept my hand anywhere after someone told me to take it off. At the place, if they told you, you did it, or somebody did it for you.

"I'm not moving it," I said, "till you say something that isn't no."

For a long time she didn't say anything. Long enough for the truck on the bypass to go past and fade out, and for me to hear the moths ticking against the canopy lights over our heads. She sat with her hands on the wheel and the lever and looked at the harbor road as if somebody was coming down it.

"How old are you," she said.

"Seventeen."

"When are you eighteen."

"February."

She nodded once, not to me.

"Get a license," she said. "Then we'll see."

Then she looked at my hand again, and this time I took it off.

The engine caught on the first turn. She switched the lights on, and the pop-ups came up. She didn't look over. She pulled out from under the canopy and onto the harbor road and went left toward the bypass, and I stood and listened to her go up through second and third until the warehouses took the sound.

Then it was just the pump island and the price sign by the road with its new 5 on it.

I looked at the hand. There was a smear on it from the window frame, road dust and the wax off her paint, and I didn't wipe it off.

"280,000 yen," I said out loud.

Nobody heard it. I said it again anyway.
