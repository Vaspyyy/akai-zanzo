# Chapter 4: Tanabe Auto Dismantling

On Monday at lunch Nobu put a used-car magazine on my desk. It was as thick as the phone book at home, printed on paper so cheap the photos had gone soft at the edges, with paper chopstick wrappers sticking out of the top for bookmarks. Every wrapper said KAWAMATA RAMEN in red.

"The shop copy," he said. "Customers read it with their noodles."

I'd come to school straight from the Sunday shift, and all morning the blackboard had been a long way off. I opened it at the first wrapper.

Rows of little photos, each car shot from the same corner of a dealer's lot, under strings of plastic flags. Under each, the year, the color and the kilometers, and under that, as big as my thumb, the price.

EUNOS ROADSTER. 1990. Red. 52,000 km. 5MT. 980,000 yen.

"Nine in the whole magazine," Nobu said. He'd sat down backwards on Okuda's chair. "I counted on Saturday, the shop was dead. Most are red. Two silver. One green, a V-Special, that's the one with the wood wheel, that's over a million."

He turned to the last wrapper and put his finger on it.

EUNOS ROADSTER. 1989. White. 96,000 km. 700,000 yen. Beside the price, in two small characters I had to lean in to read: *repair history*.

"That's the cheapest," he said. "And that's an accident car, so don't. And seven hundred is before costs. Tax, registration, the dealer's fee, the insurance. My cousin says always add a hundred thousand to anything with an engine."

I looked at 700,000 for a while. It didn't get any smaller.

"Why are you looking at Roadsters?" Nobu said. "You can't drive."

"I'm looking."

"You looked at the red ones first." He said it in the voice he'd used on the walk home on Sunday morning, for *so either you were lying to me or you were lying to him*. Then he let it go. "This Saturday, though."

"No."

"I've got twenty-six left." He patted the side pocket of his bag, where the camera lived. "Keep the magazine. My father's got next week's."

I took it home in my bag, and my bag smelled of pork bones all the way down the canal.

* * *

At three on Wednesday morning I had the magazine open on the office counter at the cheap end, with the station pencil, and Kubo-san's call-in show coming through the back-room wall.

Nobody had come in since half past one except a tanker truck, which was parked out by the air pump with its driver asleep across the seat and one sock on the dashboard.

I did it in the margin, next to the white 1989 with repair history.

700,000. Add Nobu's cousin's hundred thousand: 800,000. The half of a night I kept was 2,800 yen. 800,000 divided by 2,800 was 285 nights and a bit, so 286, and that was without touching the 41,077 in the dictionary, because that was the license's. At four nights a week, 286 nights was seventy-one weeks and a half. I'd be nearly nineteen, with a car, and still 238,923 short of being allowed to drive it.

Then I did the other one. Sanko paid 148,000 a month. 800,000 was not quite five and a half months of Sanko, if I gave Mom nothing and ate nothing and walked to the factory. Five and a half months against seventy-one weeks.

I rubbed that one out. The page was so soft the rubber went through it.

Kubo-san came out with his can of coffee at half past three, the way he did, to stand in the door and look at the road. He didn't look at the road. He stood behind me and read the page upside down, and drank.

"Seven hundred," he said.

"That's the cheapest."

"With repair history." He drank again.

I closed the magazine. He looked at the tanker driver's sock in the window of the truck. He didn't look at me at all.

"If you want a car you can't afford," he said, "go where cars go to die."

"Where's that?"

"Past the silos. Second lane on the water side, after the block works." He went to the door and stood in it. "Tanabe. Tanabe Auto Dismantling. There's a sign. Half of it."

"Do you know them?"

"Everybody who ever owned a broken car knows her. Go in the morning. Don't say I sent you."

"Why not?"

"She'll charge you double."

He took the can back inside.

* * *

I didn't go in the morning. Wednesday and Thursday were school, and with Sanko next Tuesday Hotta-sensei was watching the register. Friday was Respect for the Aged Day.

Natsume came at twenty past two on Friday morning, in the middle of the Thursday shift. Regular, fill it up. 26.4 liters, 3,036 yen. She paid with four 1,000-yen notes, and I gave her the change, 964 yen, the 100s first and the small ones on top. The whole time my left hand was in my jacket pocket, going through the gears against my leg, which it had started doing without asking me. She looked at the pocket once. Then she looked at me, and started the car.

At six I signed the sheet and hung up the cap and walked out of the station in my station jacket, because I hadn't brought another one, and went along the harbor road toward the silos with the sun already clear of the cranes. The old houses by the block works had flags out for the holiday. A white Crown came up behind me there, slowly, and my shoulder was against the nearest wall before I saw the lamp on its roof. A taxi, going home empty. I stood there anyway until it had gone round the bend.

I heard the yard before I saw it. A diesel engine working hard and then idling, working hard and idling, and every time it worked there was a long squeal of metal on metal and then a bang that I felt in the pavement. A lane of crushed shells and potholes, a corrugated steel fence two meters high, and painted on the fence in letters taller than me:

TANABE AUTO DISMANTLING

The TANABE had faded to pink. The DISMANTLING was still red. Somebody had repainted the second half and stopped.

The gate was open. Inside it was bigger than the Pier 3 lot. Cars in stacks, three and four high, flattened so that each one was the thickness of a mattress, with their colors still on. Rows of engines on pallets. A wall of tires. A big yellow machine with a heavy steel lid on hinges, which I guessed was where the flat ones came from. A two-story prefab office by the gate with a radio playing enka inside, and over all of it the smell of old gasoline and wet rust, and the weeds, which grew everywhere the cars weren't.

The diesel was a forklift. As I watched, it set a white Corolla sedan down on top of a red one, very gently, and backed off. Then it came at me and stopped about a meter from my shoes.

The driver was a woman. Small, and square, and sixty at least, with grey hair cut as short as a man's and a white towel tied round her forehead. She was wearing a man's navy work jacket with TANABE AUTO stitched in orange over the pocket, the sleeves rolled up four times, and safety boots with the steel showing through at the toes. Her hands on the levers were brown and thick, too big for the rest of her. Her left thumbnail was black and half grown out.

There was a cigarette in the corner of her mouth, and her left eye was half shut against its smoke.

"We're shut," she said. "It's a holiday."

"You're working."

"It's Respect for the Aged Day. I'm respecting myself." She looked at my jacket. "Harbor station. You're Kubo's new boy."

I didn't say anything. She laughed through her nose and two lines of smoke came out of it.

"He said don't say he sent you."

"...Yes."

"That's how he sends people." She switched the engine off. It was very quiet after it, just the enka from the office and the gulls. "What are you after? Somebody kick your mirror off?"

"I haven't got a car."

"Then you're early. This is where they come after." She looked at me a while longer. "What, then."

"Have you got a Roadster? A Eunos."

The ash on her cigarette had got long. She didn't flick it. She tipped her head a little and it fell onto her jacket, and she left it there.

"Looking's free," she said. "Behind the Corollas. Mind the cat."

Behind the Corollas there was a strip of ground the forklift didn't go to, and the weeds there were up to my waist: the tall yellow weed that grows on every empty lot in Shiomi, and foxtail grass, which we called cat grass when we were small. I pushed through it with my arms up. Seeds went down my collar.

I saw the shape before I saw the color. Over the tops of the weeds there was a hump of grey-black vinyl, low, lower than anything else in the yard, with the top of a windshield frame in front of it. A soft top. I pushed closer, and the weeds gave out where the car had been sitting long enough to kill them, and there was the rear fender going up over a wheel and coming down again. Nothing else is made like that. I'd drawn it in the margin of my English book so often that Nobu had drawn a moustache on one.

Then I was standing next to it.

It had been blue. The tops of the doors and the fenders had gone chalky, so pale they were nearly white, where the sun had sat on them for who knew how many summers. Lower down, where the sun didn't reach, the blue was still there, dull, the color of a school swimsuit. I licked my thumb and rubbed a patch on the driver's door. Under the chalk the paint came up wet and dark and shining, and then dried pale again while I watched.

Mariner Blue. Nobu's magazine had a color chart in the back. I hadn't known I'd remembered it.

The left front fender wasn't blue at all. It was white. Not dirty. White. Somebody had bolted on a fender off another car and never painted it, and where it met the door the gap was wider than my finger. The hood was the dull grey of primer, the grey of a school desk. One headlight was down. The other, the one on the driver's side, on the right, was stuck halfway up, and stared at the fence.

The front was the good end.

The back had been pushed in. The bumper had split and folded under, the trunk lid was buckled up in the middle into a peak, and both taillights were gone, just holes with wires hanging out. The rear plate was bent into a V. Both rear fenders had a crease that ran forward, smaller and smaller, and stopped just short of the doors.

The top was black vinyl gone grey, and it had split above the passenger seat where the frame inside it rubbed, a tear you could put your arm through. The plastic rear window was so yellow and so cracked you couldn't see through it.

Through the tear in the roof, on the passenger seat, there was a cat.

It was big and brown and striped, with half a tail and a notch out of one ear. It lay on the seat in a nest of its own hair and looked at me through the roof without lifting its head. Then it opened its mouth and hissed, without any sound coming out, and shut it again.

Two of the tires were flat to the rims. The other two had cracks in the sidewalls you could have put a coin in. The inspection sticker was still on the windshield, a square with a big 3 printed in the middle and a small 8 above it.

I opened the driver's door. It came, with a creak, and the smell came out at me: mold, and cat, and old rubber. The rim of the steering wheel had worn smooth and pale at the sides, where somebody's hands had been. The gear lever stood up out of the middle of the floor in its boot, with a black knob on top. The shift pattern had been on top of the knob, and somebody's palm had rubbed off everything but the 5 and the R.

I didn't get in.

I leaned in over the seat, without sitting down, and put my hand on the lever, and moved it. It went forward and away, into first, with a small hard click that I felt in my wrist. Straight back into second. Across and up into third. Straight back into fourth. Five. Back to the middle. Then right and back into reverse, and back to the middle again.

All of them. I did it twice.

The odometer said 81,644. On the passenger side the carpet had a dark stain spread across it, and when I put my hand down on it, it was wet.

"Battery's in the trunk."

She'd come up behind me on foot through the weeds, and I hadn't heard her. I didn't know how long she'd been standing there. That doesn't happen to me much.

"On these," she said. "In the trunk, at the back. Somebody's idea. And the trunk doesn't open." She knocked the buckled lid with her knuckles. "So don't bother with the key."

"What happened to it?"

"It stopped." She looked at the car, not at me. "In March. At the lights by the fish market. A boy had it, about twenty-two. It went dead under him at a red light, just like that, no noise, and he sat there with his hazards on turning the key, and a fish truck came round the corner and went into the back of him."

"Was he hurt?"

"He wore a collar for a month. Then he came in here with the collar on and signed it all over to me on my desk, and left me the keys and the papers, and said he never wanted to see it again." She touched the white fender with one finger. "Somebody had the front off it before him. That's off a white one."

"Why did it stop?"

"I didn't ask it. I put a tape on the frame underneath. Corner to corner, both ways. The numbers match."

"What does that mean?"

"It means the back might only be ugly. Might. A tape's not a lift. Somebody still has to look at the rear end properly before I'd swear to it."

The cat hissed again, at her this time, and she said something to it I didn't catch.

"Is it yours?"

"The cat? It isn't anybody's. It moved in the week the car did." She looked at me. "Well. You've looked."

My heart was going. I kept my voice where it was.

"How much?"

"A hundred and fifty thousand."

I'd known it would be a number I didn't have. I hadn't known it would be one I could say out loud without laughing.

"It doesn't run," I said.

"If it ran, it'd be seven hundred."

"The back's folded. The top's torn. The trunk doesn't open. Two of the tires are flat and two are cracked."

"You can see. Good." She held up one thick hand. "The gearbox. You felt it go in, I watched your wrist. That's thirty thousand to a man in Nagoya, on the phone, today. The engine, if it's any good, fifty thousand to the man with the container. Seats, ten the pair. Wheels, eight. You want me to go on?"

"You don't know if the engine's any good."

"Neither do you."

"Twenty thousand," I said.

She laughed. It was a laugh like a starter motor on a cold morning, going round and round and not catching.

"Twenty," she said. "The steelworks pays me eight thousand for the whole car, by weight."

"Then twenty's more than they'd give you."

"The steelworks doesn't get the gearbox, boy." She pointed at me. "A hundred and twenty. Because it's a holiday."

"Thirty."

"You know what they say. Buy cheap, lose money." She looked up at the sky, which was going white over the fence. "A hundred. Because I'm old, and old people are soft, it's in the newspaper."

"Forty."

"Eighty."

"How old are you?" she said then, in the same voice, before I could answer the eighty.

"Seventeen."

"Seventeen." She looked at the car and then back at me. "So you can't drive it. You can't even put it in your name, not without a parent's consent and a pile of forms at the transport office. The shaken's good till March, that's the 3 on the sticker, March next year. Somebody let it run out once and had it done again in a spring. After March it's a hundred thousand to keep it on the road, and you still can't drive it. You're buying a very big chair."

It was true. I looked at the lever through the door, the knob with nothing left on it but 5 and R.

"I've got forty-one thousand and seventy-seven yen," I said. "That's all of it. That's everything I've got."

She looked at me for a long time.

"Seventy-seven," she said.

"Seventy-seven."

"Most boys who come in here owe somebody." She looked away at the stacks. "Nothing goes out of this yard on its own wheels, you know that? Thirty-seven years. It goes out on a truck to the steelworks, or in pieces in a box. The engines go in a container to Karachi. The rest goes in there." She nodded at the yellow machine. "Not one on its own wheels."

The cat had stood up on the seat and was stretching, front legs and then back legs, with its half a tail straight up.

"Sixty," she said. "And you sweep up."

I opened my mouth, and she held up a finger.

"Sixty thousand. You don't give me forty-one, because then you've got seventy-seven yen, and a boy with seventy-seven yen eats my cat. You give me twenty down. Then you work. Sundays, in the day. A boy's worth three thousand a day to me, if he can hold a spanner and doesn't cry. Twenty thousand in work. Then twenty thousand in money: seven at the end of October, seven at the end of November, six at the end of December. You miss one, the deal's off and it goes in the press." She ticked them off on her thick fingers. "Twenty. Twenty. Twenty."

I did it in my head. Twenty thousand in work at three thousand a day was six days and two-thirds.

"That's seven Sundays," I said. "Seven Sundays is twenty-one thousand."

"The extra thousand's for the cat."

"The cat isn't anybody's."

"It's yours now. You feed it Sundays." She turned away toward the forklift. "And it stays here, in my yard, in my name, till the last yen. Anybody asks, it's my car." She stopped. "And if your mother walks in here one day wanting her boy's money back, she gets it, and I'm out a car and a sweeper. That's my risk. Yours is the press."

She wiped her right hand on her jacket and held it out.

Her hand was rough and dry and very strong, and she shook once, hard.

"Tanabe," she said.

"Hayama. Daisuke."

"Sunday. Seven o'clock. Bring the twenty." She was already walking. "And don't wear that jacket, it's Kubo's."

* * *

I slept through Friday afternoon and worked Friday night. On Saturday half the class was out at company exams. The girl with the bank had gone in a new blouse. School finished at half past twelve, and I walked home along the canal and slept on the tatami until Sota sat on me.

Mom was home from the factory, and she had a sheet of paper.

"It came in the post," she said. "From Hotta-sensei."

It was a typed list with the school's stamp at the bottom. *Questions often asked at company interviews.* Sixteen of them. Mom put it on the low table and smoothed it flat with the side of her hand, and sat down on her heels in front of it.

"Sit," she said.

"Now?"

"I'm at the supermarket from eight. Tuesday's Tuesday. When else?"

I sat. Sota sat next to her, close, to see the paper. Aya was on her day off, lying on her front on the tatami behind us with a magazine about hair.

"Number one." Mom read it slowly, in her factory voice. "*Why did you choose our company?*"

"Because Hotta-sensei picked it."

"Daisuke."

"It's true."

"You don't say that." She looked at the paper. "You say... because it's a company that makes something important."

"Brakes."

"Because it makes brakes. And brakes keep people safe. And you want to do work that keeps people safe."

"Because it makes brakes, and brakes keep people safe," I said, "and I want to do work that keeps people safe."

Aya turned a page behind me. Mom went on.

"Number two. *What are your strong points?*"

I couldn't think of one that would go on that paper. Sota took his glasses off and put them back on, which is what he does when he wants to help and doesn't know how.

"You've never been late," Mom said. "Not once this year. Hotta-sensei said."

"I've never been late."

"Say it like it's yours."

"I've never been late."

"Better."

Sota had been reading ahead, upside down, with his lips moving. He put his finger on number nine.

"*What do you do on your days off?*" he read. "What do you do on your days off, Nii-chan?"

"Sleep."

"You can't say sleep," Mom said.

"Study," Aya said, into her magazine. "At Kawamata's. All night."

Mom didn't hear it, or she let it go. Her finger had stopped on the paper between nine and ten. I read ahead. Number ten was *Please tell us about your school life up to now.* She moved her finger past it to eleven without reading it out, and nobody said anything, and Sota read eleven.

"*Do you have any questions for us?*"

"Do you pay on the twenty-fifth?" I said.

"You know they pay on the twenty-fifth."

"Then I haven't got any questions."

Mom put her hand flat on the sheet. For a second I thought she was angry. Then I saw it was the other thing, the thing that had nearly been on her face when Aya told her about the phone book, and she pressed her lips together over it.

"You ask what a new person does in the first year," she said. "They like that." She folded the sheet in half and in half again and gave it to me. "Read it tomorrow. And Monday. Are you at Kawamata's again tomorrow?"

"In the morning," I said. "Early."

"Then take something for his mother." She got up, with a hand on the table, and went to the kitchen and came back with a supermarket bag with two big brown pears in it. The good kind, the round ones, cold from the refrigerator. "She fed you, didn't she? Last week. Tell her thank you from me."

"I'll tell her."

Aya put her magazine down.

"Two for 198," she said. "The reduced shelf, Thursday. Don't tell his mother that."

"Aya."

"I'm helping. I'm helping him not say it."

Mom left at half past seven in her supermarket vest. Aya went to the public phone at the bottom of the block with a phone card and didn't say who she was calling. Sota had his bath and got into his futon with his glasses still on.

I took the English dictionary down off the shelf and opened it on my knees, with my back to the fusuma. Four 10,000-yen notes in the envelope. I took two out and folded them once and put them in the inside pocket of my school bag, behind the sheet with sixteen questions on it. Then I put the dictionary back. It looked exactly the same on the shelf.

I got out the receipt and did the sum under the kitchen light. 41,077 take away 20,000 left 21,077. 280,000 take away 21,077 left 258,923.

I wrote it under the last number. It was the first number on there that was bigger than the one above it.

"Nii-chan."

Sota, through the fusuma. I slid it open a little. He was lying on his side with his glasses on, looking at me.

"You said you'd tell me," he said. "The moon. It's been a whole week."

I sat down in the gap, half in the kitchen and half in the room.

"It was white," I said. "Whiter than it looks from here. It came round the corner of a building, really slowly, and then it was over the water and the cranes. The water under it was all in pieces. Little bright pieces, and they moved, and they didn't go anywhere."

He was listening with his whole face.

"It was big," I said. "It was low. You could see the dark bits on it, all of them."

"Was the rabbit there?"

I thought about it. The doorway, Nobu's breath on my hand, my shoulder against the frame.

"The rabbit was there."

"Was he making mochi?"

"He was working."

"On the fifteenth night he's supposed to be making mochi."

"He was making mochi, then."

Sota nodded. He took his glasses off, finally, and folded them and put them on the floor beside his pillow, next to his inhaler.

"Thank you for looking," he said, and turned over.

* * *

On Sunday I left at twenty past six with the pears and the 20,000 and the sixteen questions, in my old school sports shirt, because it was the only one I had that I didn't care about.

Tanabe-san was already in the yard. She was sitting on an upturned crate outside the office with a can of coffee, and she held her hand out without getting up. I gave her the two notes. She held each one up to the light and put them in the breast pocket of the jacket and buttoned it.

I followed her to the office door.

Inside there was a desk, a phone, a kettle, a fridge, a radio, and a plywood wall covered in black marker, names and numbers going back years, most of them crossed through. Above the desk there was a poster, faded to blue and pink, of a beach with palm trees and a little green island off the shore with a long bridge going out to it. MIYAZAKI, it said. THE SOUTHERN COUNTRY. A couple in white were walking along the sand in it with their backs to the camera.

She uncapped the marker with her teeth and wrote on the wall, under the last name, in big square characters:

HAYAMA. ROADSTER. 60,000.

And under it:

MINUS 20,000. 17 SEP.

"Forty," she said, and put the cap back on. "Gloves."

They were white cotton work gloves out of a box of fifty, thin enough to feel the seams through. She was wearing leather.

"Those are for dirt," she said, seeing me look. "They're not for sharp. Nothing is for sharp. You put your hand where you can't see, sharp finds it."

The Corolla she'd put on the stack on Friday was down on the ground again, by the oil drums. A 1988 four-door, the most ordinary car in Japan. A family had brought it in after they took the keys off their grandfather.

"Fluids first," she said. "Everything wet comes out before anything else. Wet things catch fire."

She lifted the back of it with the forks onto two stacks of old tires, lay down on a sheet of cardboard with a funnel and a hose running to a red can, and knocked a hole in the bottom of the tank with a punch and a hammer. The gasoline came down in a stream, and the smell got into my eyes. "That goes in my truck," she said. The oil came out of the engine when I undid the drain plug, which was so tight I had to stand on the spanner, and it got my wrist before I could pull my hand away. The coolant went into its own drum. It was green, and smelled sweet.

"Lid on that one," she said. "Always. It tastes sweet, and it kills cats."

I put the lid on and pressed it down all the way round.

She showed me the brake fluid and told me what it did to paint, and to cuts, and to eyes, in that order. Then she gave me a 10 and a 12, a ratchet, two screwdrivers and a hammer, all of them older than me, on a piece of cardboard.

"A Japanese car is made of tens and twelves," she said. "Everything else is somebody showing off."

Then she went off on the forklift, and left me alone with it.

I took the Corolla apart for six hours. Not all of it: only what she'd chalked, with a price beside each in yellow crayon so I'd know what not to break.

The door-trim screws had rusted, and the cross-head chewed them round, and I had to tap it in with the hammer until it bit. The clips behind the trims broke, every one. She drove past on the forklift and saw the little pile of white plastic and said, "They always break," and drove on.

At eleven she put a little brown Mira with no engine in it into the yellow machine and pulled a lever. The lid came down with a long groan, and when it went up again there was a brown slab where the Mira had been. She lifted it out with the forks and put it on a stack and didn't look back at it.

At half past twelve she shouted from the office door. She was sitting on her crate with an aluminum lunch box on her knees: rice, an umeboshi in the middle, salted salmon, yellow pickle. She looked at my bag, which had the pears in it and nothing else.

"Where's your lunch?"

"I'm not hungry."

She took the lid off the lunch box, scooped half of the rice onto it with her chopsticks, put the pickle on top, and held it out.

"Nobody asked," she said.

I gave her a pear. She looked at it, turned it round in her fingers, took a folding knife out of her jacket, and peeled it in one long brown ribbon that went down to the ground without breaking. Then she cut it into eight and gave me four.

"My husband used to do that," she said. "Faster than me." She tipped her head back at the office door, at the wall inside. "That's him, on my poster. Well. It's not. It's two people from the travel company. But it's where we went. 1958. He was so seasick on the boat down he couldn't stand up. Then he stood on that beach for an hour with his trousers rolled up and said, Fumie, when we're old, we're coming back here." She ate a piece of pear. "Where'd you get these? These are good."

"My mother."

"Your mother buys good pears."

I didn't say what Aya had said. I ate the rice and the pickle and my four pieces of pear, cold and sweet and full of water, and the core of the last one too. The other pear was still in the bag. I'd take it to Kawamata Ramen on Monday, so that at least half of it was true.

It happened at about half past three, on the last window motor, which comes out through a hole in the steel behind the door trim, with your hand in where you can't see it.

The radio was on in the office, the sumo from Tokyo. I was thinking about the Roadster's gear lever, about second gear and the small hard click. I put my left hand in through the hole to hold the motor while I undid the last bolt with my right. The bolt came free. The motor dropped, and I grabbed for it, and my hand went across the edge of the hole.

It felt like someone had drawn a line across the bottom of my palm with a cold pencil. I took my hand out. The white glove had a red line across it, very neat, and then the line got wide and the glove went red all the way to the wrist, faster than I'd have believed, and started to drip on the Corolla's sill.

I didn't make any noise. I stood there holding my left wrist in my right hand, with the glove filling up, and looked for her.

She was on the forklift at the far end, and she was already coming. She'd seen me stand still. She stopped the forklift, climbed down without hurrying, pulled off one glove, and took my wrist and turned it over.

"Off," she said, and peeled the glove off me herself, and dropped it in the weeds.

It was across the heel of my hand, under the thumb, about four centimeters. The edges were white for a second and then they weren't.

"Up," she said. "Above your heart. Walk."

In the office she held my hand under the cold tap. The water went pink and then red and then pink again. She got an old cookie tin out from under the desk, the round blue kind, full of gauze and tape and scissors and a brown bottle of Oxydol. She poured the Oxydol on, and it fizzed white in the cut, and that hurt.

"Hold that," she said, and I held a pad of gauze on it with my other hand, hard, while she cut tape into short strips with the scissors.

She worked close to my hand with her head tipped back, so the smoke went up past her half-shut eye and not into it. The ash on her cigarette got long. I watched it, waiting for it to fall in the cut. It didn't. She pulled the edges of the cut together with her two thumbs, gently, and put five strips across it, then gauze, then a bandage round my palm and my wrist, tied off with her teeth.

"Doesn't need sewing," she said. "It'll need you to keep it shut. Don't bend it for a week."

A week. Tonight there were the pumps, and the nozzle, and Kubo-san looking. On Tuesday there was a table at Sanko with men behind it, and my hands on top of it.

"When did you last have a tetanus shot?"

"I don't know."

"At school. Sixth year, they do it."

I'd been in the place in my sixth year. They'd lined us up in a corridor once with our sleeves rolled. I didn't know what for.

"I had one," I said.

She looked at me, and then at the hand, and didn't say anything else about it.

The phone on the desk rang. She let it ring while she put the scissors and the tape back in the tin, nine times, and it stopped.

"My daughter," she said. "Yokohama. She wants me to sell up and go and live with her in two rooms by the sea wall." She pushed the tin back under the desk with her boot. "Gauze, tape, Oxydol. That's two hundred yen I'm out."

I looked at her.

"First one's free," she said. "Everybody gets one." She picked up the broom that was leaning by the door and held it out to me. "Sweep up."

You can sweep with one hand if you hold the broom short.

I did round the Corolla first, the broken clips, the screws, my own blood in the weeds, which I covered with dirt with my foot. Then the strip along the back fence behind the tire wall.

At the very back, against the fence, there was a long shape under a grey tarp.

Everything else in the yard that had a cover had a blue one, the plastic kind every roof gets after a typhoon. This one was heavy grey canvas, roped down, with an old tire on each corner. Long and low. A car, under there. Dead leaves had blown in along the bottom, so I lifted the edge with the head of the broom to get at them.

"Not that one."

She was on the forklift, twenty meters away, stopped, with the engine idling.

"Not for sale," she said. "Don't touch that one."

I let the corner down. The tire on top of it rocked once and sat still.

"I was sweeping."

"Sweep round it."

She backed the forklift up and drove off toward the stacks. I swept round it, and got the leaves out with my foot, and didn't look at it again.

At half past four I put the broom back by the office door. She was inside at the desk with her boots up on a box, listening to the sumo.

"Go and feed your cat," she said. "Tin of mackerel in the fridge. Don't give it the whole tin, it'll be sick on your seat."

I went back through the weeds with the tin, holding the bandaged hand against my chest.

The cat was on the passenger seat. It watched me open the driver's door. I put a little of the mackerel on the lid, on the dry bit of the carpet, and it looked at the mackerel and then at me, and didn't move.

This time I got in.

I sat down slowly, because of the hand, and the springs went down further than I thought they would. The seat was set for somebody shorter than me. My knees came up. Through the split in the roof there was a strip of sky going yellow, and through the windshield the back of a stack of flattened Corollas, white and red and white. The steering wheel was right there in front of me. Closer than I'd thought.

I put my right hand on it at three o'clock.

Then I put my left hand on it at nine, the bandaged one, carefully, with my fingers open, the way the book said. You don't grip. You hold it as if it might want to go somewhere.

It didn't go anywhere. Under me the car smelled of mold and mackerel, and somewhere at the front of the yard the forklift was working hard and idling, working hard and idling.

The cat got up. It stepped down off the passenger seat, past the mackerel without looking at it, and walked across the handbrake, and stopped and looked at me from about ten centimeters away. Then it climbed onto my knees, turned round twice, and lay down, heavy and warm, with its half a tail over my wrist.

I kept my hands where they were.
