# Chapter 5: Interview

Kubo-san saw the bandage at ten past ten on Sunday night, when I lifted the nozzle for the first car of the shift with my right hand and kept the left one against my jacket.

He didn't say anything until the car had gone. Then he held out his hand, palm up, and waited, and I put my left hand in it. He turned it over and looked at the gauze and the tape, and at where the bandage went round the wrist, and turned it back.

"Tanabe's?" he said.

"Window motor. Corolla."

"They all do that. The hole's got an edge on it like a can lid." He gave me the hand back and went and stood in the office door, looking at the road. "She's got you on Sundays, then."

"Seven of them."

He nodded at the road. For the rest of the night he did the windshields himself, and when a truck came in at three he did the filler cap too, and I took the money one-handed and made the change on the counter.

I went to school straight from the pumps with the second pear in my bag. After school I took it to the back door of Kawamata Ramen. Nobu's mother came out in her apron, drying her hands, and took the pear from me in both of hers and bowed over it, and asked me to thank my mother for her. So now two mothers were thanking each other through me. She had fed me: one umeboshi onigiri, on the back road behind the fish-market sheds, at midnight.

At home Mom was asleep behind the fusuma with a damp towel over her eyes. I lay down on the tatami and was gone. When I woke the window was dark, she'd left for the supermarket, and Aya was spreading newspaper on the floor.

"Sit," she said. "Not there. Under the light."

She put a kitchen chair in the middle of the newspaper, under the fluorescent ring, and I sat on it. She came at me from behind with a black garbage bag that had a hole cut in the bottom of it.

"Head through."

I put my head through. The bag came down over my shoulders and my arms and crackled every time I breathed. She tucked it into my collar at the back with two fingers, tight.

Then she went to her and Mom's room and came back with a cloth roll, the kind carpenters keep chisels in, and untied it on the table. Inside were two combs, a row of black clips, a spray bottle that had held window cleaner once and now held water, and a pair of scissors I had never seen. Long and narrow and silver, with a little hook on one handle for the finger. They didn't look like anything we owned.

"Don't touch them," Aya said. "They cost more than your shoes."

She sprayed my head until water ran down behind my ears, and combed it all straight down, over my face, so that I was looking at the newspaper through my own hair.

Then she started, and she didn't say anything else for a long time.

That was the strange part. Aya always says something. She talks while she eats and while she soaks her feet and while she brushes her teeth. Now she took up a piece of my hair between two fingers, lifted it, looked at it, and cut along the edge of her fingers in quick small bites, *snip snip snip*, and let it go and took the next piece. Her fingers were cold from the spray. She tipped my head over with one hand without asking, and cut round my ear, and tipped it back. Wet hair slid down the bag and dropped on the newspaper, on a photo of the prime minister.

I watched her in the dark window over the sink. She had her mouth shut tight and her eyes narrowed, and she was standing with one foot turned out, the way the girls in her hair magazine stand.

The bag had ridden up. She pulled it down over my knees and saw the bandage. She looked at it for a second, and went back to my ear.

She did the other side, and the back, with the comb flat against my neck so the cold of it made my shoulders come up. Then she came round to the front, and crouched, and combed my bangs straight up off my forehead to see where they fell.

The comb stopped.

I knew what she was looking at. A line through my left eyebrow, pale, where the hair doesn't grow, about as long as the nail on my little finger. I'd had bangs over it since I was fourteen. Mom had seen it once, the day I came home.

Aya looked at it for about a second. Then she let the bangs fall back down.

"This is coming off," she said. "They want eyebrows."

"Leave some."

"Did I ask you?"

She took the bangs between her fingers, a little above my eyebrows, and cut straight across. I heard it, and felt the air come in on my forehead, cool, where there hadn't been any air for three years.

"Nobody's going to look at your eyebrow," she said, combing. "They'll be watching your mouth, in case something stupid comes out of it."

She held my chin and turned my face to the window and back, once. Then she wiped the scissors on a cloth and rolled them up and tied the roll, and handed me Mom's little mirror out of the drawer.

I looked like somebody's son. The sides were short above my ears and the back was off my collar, and the bangs stopped a finger's width above my eyebrows, and there it was, the line through the left one, in the open, under the fluorescent ring. It didn't look like much. It looked like a scar.

"At the place by the station," Aya said, folding up the newspaper with the hair inside it, "that's three thousand yen. With a wash."

"Which place?"

"Doesn't matter which place." She tipped the hair into the bin. "You owe me three thousand yen."

"I'll pay you."

"You won't." She took the cloth roll back to her room. From inside, without coming out, she said: "And eat what Mom left you. She put an egg in it."

* * *

The iron woke me.

Not the sound of it. The smell: hot cotton, and the starch spray Mom keeps under the sink for New Year and funerals. I lay on my futon with my eyes open in the grey and knew from the smell what time it was, and that it was Tuesday.

Mom was kneeling at the low ironing board in the kitchen, in her factory smock, with her hair already up. The light was on only over the sink. My white shirt was on the board, the summer one with short sleeves, and she was doing the collar points from the tip inwards, so the iron wouldn't push a crease into them.

I sat in the doorway with my back against the frame.

"It's five," she said, without turning round. "Go back to sleep."

I didn't. She hung the shirt on a hanger off the top of the fusuma frame, where it moved a little in the air from the window. Then she laid my black trousers on the board, wet a tenugui under the tap, wrung it out, and laid it over them before she put the iron down. Steam came up round her hands. You do it through a cloth, or the black goes shiny. I hadn't known that.

"Next Monday I'm at the store all night," she said. "The stocktaking. Eight till five. I've swapped Tuesday at the factory." She lifted the cloth, looked, and laid it down again. "Aya's home after nine. You're off Mondays."

"Yes."

"Good."

She hung the trousers over the shirt. Then she held the hanger out to me, and I got up and took it, and she saw my hand.

She put the iron down on its heel.

"What's that."

"Work."

"Work what."

"The sign out by the road. You change the numbers by hand. They've got metal edges."

She looked at the bandage, and at my face, and at the bandage. It had been white on Sunday. A night at the pumps and a day at school had made it grey round the thumb, and there was a brown mark on it near the wrist.

"Is it clean under there?"

"Yes."

She got up, with her hand on the board, and took the medicine box down from the top of the refrigerator. It's a square tin that had rice crackers in it once, and now has Sota's things in it, his pills and the measuring spoon and a spare roll of bandage still in its wrapper. She put the roll in my hand and closed my fingers round it, carefully, the good ones.

"Put a clean one on before you go," she said. "A dirty bandage, people think you've been fighting."

I held the roll and the hanger.

"They'll give you lunch," she said. "Hotta-sensei said. Eat it, even if it's bad." She picked up her bag from beside the genkan and stepped down into her shoes. At the door she stopped, and looked back at the shirt on its hanger, the way you look back at a stove to check you turned it off.

Then she went, and the steel door closed behind her as quietly as she could make it.

The shirt was still warm when I put it on. I stood in the kitchen and did the buttons up, all of them, to the top, and felt the collar stand up stiff against my neck, and changed the bandage at the sink with my teeth and my right hand. The new one was very white.

* * *

The Sanko Brake plant was twenty minutes on the bus from the station, out where the river comes into the harbor, past the timber yards. 160 yen. I paid it myself.

There were eleven of us in the waiting room, in five different school uniforms, on folding chairs along one wall under a framed picture of the company's founder. Three from our school. I knew the other two by sight, and they knew me, from somewhere, and they sat down two chairs away. Everybody's hair was short. Everybody's collar was done up. One boy from a school across the river had a Band-Aid on his chin, and I looked at it and felt better, and then he caught me looking at it and I stopped feeling better.

The whole building smelled of hot brakes. Not strong, but everywhere, in the corridors and in the cushion of the folding chair. The last time I'd smelled it, it had come across the Pier 3 lot after the finish.

The written test was in the morning, in a room with desks. First kanji, readings and writing, half of which I knew. Then a page of sums with fractions, which I did badly. Then four hundred characters on *What I hope to achieve at work*, and I wrote Mom's answer about brakes and stretched it to three hundred and sixty.

Then a man in a grey work jacket came in with a stopwatch and gave out a different kind of sheet. Rows of single numbers, all the way across the page, line after line, nothing else on it.

"Add each number to the one next to it," he said. "Write only the last figure of the answer, in the gap between them. When I say *next*, go to the next line, wherever you are. Don't go back."

Seven and eight, five. Eight and six, four. Six and nine, five.

"Next."

I was good at it. I knew straight away that I was good at it. It was the only thing all day that felt like the pumps: numbers coming at you, one after another, and you just did them, and they were done. My bandaged hand held the paper flat. The boy next to me was moving his lips. I could feel mine wanting to move, and I kept them shut for fifteen minutes, then five minutes' rest, then fifteen more, and every minute the man said "Next" and every minute I dropped a line further down the page than the boy beside me.

Lunch was in the factory canteen, at a long table at the end, away from the workers. Curry rice and a cup of barley tea. The men from the line came in at twelve in grey jackets with SANKO in red over the pocket, all at once, and ate fast, with their heads down, and talked about baseball, and were gone again by twenty past. Their hands were grey in the creases. Not black, like Kubo-san's. Grey, like pencil.

I ate all of mine. It was bad.

At one o'clock they started calling us in for the interviews, one at a time, by name. I was eighth.

You knock three times. You open the door and say "Excuse me," and turn round and close it with both hands so it doesn't bang, and then you bow, and say your school and your name, and wait to be told to sit. Mom had made me do it four times at the low table on Saturday, using the fusuma for the door.

"Shiomi Higashi High School, third year. Hayama Daisuke."

"Please sit down."

There were three men behind a long table with a green cloth on it. In front of the table, about two meters out in the middle of the floor, there was one chair. Nothing in front of it. That was mine.

I'd thought about it since Sunday. A table at Sanko with men behind it, and my hands on top of it, where they could see. I'd been wrong about the table. The table was theirs. I sat down on the chair, in the open, and put my hands on my knees, and there they were anyway, with the new bandage on the left one, and below them the tape on my right shoe.

The one in the middle was in a suit, about fifty, with a pen he kept clicking and a folder open in front of him. The card on the table said SUGIMOTO, PERSONNEL. On his left was an older man in the grey work jacket, with big grey-creased hands folded on the cloth. On his right, a young one with a notepad who wrote down everything, even when nothing was being said.

"Why did you choose our company?" Sugimoto said.

"Because it makes brakes," I said. "And brakes keep people safe. And I want to do work that keeps people safe."

It came out in Mom's voice. I heard it come out. The young one wrote something short. Sugimoto nodded. He'd probably heard it ten times already that day, and it was still the right answer.

"Your strong points?"

"I've never been late."

He looked down at the folder. "Not this year." He turned a page. "Not once. That's good."

He turned back to the first page, the one with my photo glued at the top, and read for a moment with the pen still, not clicking. His eyes had stopped on one line near the top. I knew which line. The name of my junior high, and *graduated*, and the year, and nothing above it.

"Please tell us about your school life up to now," he said.

It was number ten on Hotta-sensei's sheet, word for word. He said it to the paper. Then he looked up, as if he'd remembered he was supposed to, and waited.

It came up the way it always comes up, from the back of my neck. Heat, behind my ears, and up into my face. And a voice I know, which is mine from when I was twelve, saying: *You know already. It's written down somewhere. Read it yourself.*

My left hand had shut into a fist on my knee. Under the new bandage the cut pulled. I didn't open the hand. I pressed it down, into my knee, harder, until there was a hot line across the bottom of my palm and the line was the only thing in the room, and the voice had to go round it.

Answer short, Hotta-sensei had said. Don't get angry.

I could feel the air on my forehead, where the bangs had been.

"From ten to fourteen I lived in a facility," I said, "and since then I've gone to school like everybody else."

Sugimoto looked at me for a moment longer. The pen clicked once.

"Thank you for telling us," he said, and wrote something. It wasn't long. The young one wrote something too. The man in the work jacket didn't write anything. He was looking at my face, and I let him, and after a while he looked at his hands.

"Do you have any questions for us?" Sugimoto said.

"What does a new person do in the first year?"

It was the older man who answered. His voice was rough and slow.

"First year, you sweep," he said. "Then you watch. Then they let you near the press, with somebody standing next to you." He unfolded his hands and folded them the other way round. "And you learn the sound it makes when something's wrong. Before anything breaks."

"Thank you," I said.

"Thank you. That's all."

I stood up and bowed, and at the door I turned and bowed again, and went out and shut the door with both hands, and stood in the corridor. I didn't know if I'd passed.

At the bus stop outside the gate I opened my left hand. There was a red dot on the new bandage, in the middle of the palm, the size of a grain of rice. I closed the hand round it again and kept it closed on my knee the whole way back, and Mom's shirt smelled of hot brakes all the way to the station.

* * *

On Tuesday night Kubo-san didn't ask about Sanko until four in the morning. Then he looked at my haircut for a while, and at my forehead, and drank some of his coffee.

"Did you shout at anybody?"

"No."

"Then it went fine."

At six he gave me the brown envelope with HAYAMA on it: 44,800 for eight nights. Half went on the shoe cabinet at home and half into the English dictionary with the 20,000 already there. 42,400 in the dictionary, and 757 in my pocket after the bus to Sanko and back.

At ten past two on Friday morning the red Roadster came round the bend from the bypass and stopped at the pump.

"Regular. Fill it up."

My left hand was in my pocket, but not doing anything. It hadn't been since Sunday. Every time it tried to go into second, the cut told it not to.

23.6 liters. 2,714 yen. She gave me three 1,000-yen notes, and I had to put them down on the roof of her car to get the change out one-handed, and the left hand came out of the pocket to hold them down in the wind, without my asking it to.

She looked at the bandage. Not long.

"Keep that dry," she said.

"I am."

I gave her the 286 yen. She put it in the tray under the radio without counting it, and the window went up, and she went.

* * *

On Sunday Tanabe-san swapped the bandage for a big square Band-Aid and sent me to take the seats out of a Bluebird. I did the Sunday shift after, went to school on Monday without sleeping, and slept on the tatami until seven.

Sota had had a cough since the weekend. The small dry one he gets when the weather turns. At dinner Mom felt his forehead with the back of her hand, and then with her lips, and said it was nothing. At half past seven she went in her supermarket vest, with a thermos of tea in her bag and two onigiri in foil.

"He's had his medicine," she said at the door. "If he coughs in the night, the inhaler's by his pillow."

"I know where the inhaler is."

"Two puffs, and you wait. It's on the fridge."

"I know."

She looked past me at Sota, who was doing his homework at the low table with his chin on his fist, and went.

Sota went to bed at nine with his glasses on and his inhaler by his pillow, and coughed a few times, and stopped. Aya came home at half past nine and soaked her feet and went to bed at eleven.

I should have been tired. On a night off my body still thinks it's at the pumps, and at one in the morning I'm wider awake than at any time in the day. I sat at the low table with only the light over the sink on, and Nobu's used-car magazine open at nothing, and opened and closed my left hand under the table. It closed most of the way now. The Band-Aid pulled at the end.

At ten to one I heard Sota through the fusuma.

I'd heard his chest whistle a thousand times. It whistles when he laughs too hard and when he runs up the stairs, and on cold mornings, like a kettle in somebody else's apartment. This wasn't that. This was higher, and thinner, and it was on the breath in as well as the breath out, and the breath in was too short.

I slid the fusuma open.

He was sitting up on his futon in the dark, bent forward, with his hands flat on his knees and his elbows out, and his shoulders were up by his ears. He'd taken his glasses off. He'd done it himself, and folded them, and put them beside his pillow, next to the inhaler. His mouth was open.

"Nii-chan," he said. Then he had to breathe. "Sorry."

"Don't talk."

I put the light on. I got the inhaler and shook it and took the cap off and gave it to him, and held his wrist steady while he got his mouth round it.

"Out first," I said. "All the way. Now."

He pressed it and breathed in, and I counted. He couldn't hold it. His breath came out after two, with a cough on the end. We did the second one. He held that for three.

"Slow," I said. "Breathe with me."

I breathed, slow, in through the nose and out through the mouth, kneeling next to his futon. I'd done it with him like that since I came home from the place. He tried to follow. He couldn't keep up with slow. His breath kept catching up with itself.

The clock on the kitchen wall said 12:53.

Taped to the side of the refrigerator, in Mom's writing, was the paper from the clinic, copied out from what the doctor said, with the important parts underlined twice. *2 puffs. Wait 20 minutes. Not better: 2 more. Still not better: hospital. Can't talk, lips blue: AMBULANCE.* I'd seen it every day for three years, getting the barley tea out.

So I waited the twenty minutes, because the paper said twenty. He could already hardly talk.

For a few minutes it was better. Not much. The whistle came down a little, and he sat back an inch, and I thought, there. Then it came back up.

I sat on the futon behind him, with my back against the wall, and pulled him back against my chest so he could lean, but he didn't want to lean. He wanted to be bent forward. He pushed my arm off and put his hands back on his knees. So I let him, and kept one hand flat on his back, between his shoulder blades. Through his pajama top I could feel his ribs working. They were working far too hard for what was coming in.

I counted his breaths against the kitchen clock. Forty-four in a minute. I didn't know what the number should have been. I knew it was too many.

The fusuma on the other side slid open. Aya, in her tank top and cardigan, with her hair flat on one side.

"What is it?"

"He's bad."

She came and knelt and looked at him, and he looked back at her over his own knees, and tried to smile, and couldn't spare the breath for it.

"Did you do the inhaler?"

"Two. At 12:53."

"Then it'll work. It always works."

At 1:13, twenty minutes, I gave him two more. He couldn't get his mouth to close round it properly the first time, and half of it went into the air, and I made him do it again. Then I pulled his pajama top up at the back to see, because I had to know.

Every time he breathed in, the skin between his ribs sucked in. Little hollows, a row of them, down both sides, in and out. I came round in front of him. At the bottom of his throat the hollow there sank in with each breath, deep enough to put a fingertip in.

He was sweating. His hair was stuck to his forehead in points.

"It's quieter," Aya said. "Listen. It's going quieter."

It was. His shoulders were still up by his ears, and his ribs were still pulling in, and his mouth was still open, and the whistle was going quieter.

Last winter the doctor at the clinic had said it to Mom, at the desk, with me standing behind her holding Sota's coat. The noise is the good part. The noise is air. When the noise goes and he's still working that hard, you don't wait.

"Sota," I said. "Say my name."

He looked at me. His lips moved.

"Nii..."

He couldn't do the rest.

Under the light his lips had gone a color I'd never seen on him. Not blue, exactly. The blue-grey of skimmed milk.

I stood up.

"Aya."

She didn't move. She was kneeling on the edge of the futon with her hands up in front of her mouth, both of them, the fingers pressed together, and she was looking at his lips, and she didn't move at all.

"Aya. Look at me."

She looked at me.

"Open the front door. Put the stair light on. Then go down to the bottom and stand by the road, and when they come, wave."

"When who comes?"

"The ambulance."

She stood up. She went to the genkan and opened the steel door and put it on the latch, and I heard the stair light switch click, and her sandals going down, fast, slapping on every step.

The phone was on the little shelf by the calendar. It was the old black one we rent from NTT, with the dial. I picked it up and put my finger in the 1 and pulled it round to the stop and let it go, and it went back. The 1 again. Then the 9. The 9 goes all the way round, almost to the stop, and comes back slowly, a long way, ticking, and I stood there with the receiver against my ear and watched it come back and couldn't make it go any faster.

It rang once.

"119, fire department. Is it a fire, or an ambulance?"

"Ambulance."

"What's the address?"

I told him. Higashi danchi, Building 6, room 402. Fourth floor. No elevator. My voice came out flat and clear. I'd never heard it sound like that.

"Who's the patient?"

"My brother. He's eleven. He's got asthma. It's an attack. He's had four puffs of his inhaler, two at 12:53 and two at 1:13, and it's not doing anything. He can't say more than one word. He's sitting up. His lips are going blue."

"Is he conscious?"

"Yes."

"Is he breathing?"

"Yes. Fast. Forty-four in a minute."

He asked my name, and the phone number, and I said them. He said an ambulance was on its way. He said to keep him sitting up, to let him sit however he wanted, not to make him lie down, to keep the front door open.

"It's open."

"They're coming," he said.

I put the receiver down. It didn't go in. It hit the edge of the cradle and slid off, and swung on its cord and knocked against the shelf, and I picked it up and put it down again, and it didn't go in the second time either. My hand was doing something. I used both hands the third time, and it went in.

I went back and knelt in front of Sota, so that he could see my face.

"They're coming," I said. "Seven minutes. Maybe less."

I didn't know that. I said it anyway.

He nodded. He had his hands on his knees and his eyes on me, and in between breaths he got one word out, and then, after two more breaths, another one.

"Ambulance... costs..."

"It's free," I said.

He nodded again. He put his hand out, and I took it. It was hot and wet, and he squeezed my left hand, the Band-Aid, hard, and the cut lit up all along the bottom of my palm, and I let him squeeze.

I heard the siren a long way off. Two notes, high and low, high and low, coming up out of the city, and it got bigger, and went round a corner somewhere and got bigger again, and then it was in the street below the block and I could see the red light on the ceiling, turning, coming in through the window over the sink. It stopped. Doors slammed down there. Then boots on the stairs, a lot of boots, fast, and Aya's voice under them saying "Fourth floor, fourth floor," and the stair light shining in round the edge of the open door.

There were three of them, in light grey with white helmets. The first one came in without taking his boots off and knelt by the futon and put his face close to Sota's and said, "Hello. I'm going to help you breathe, all right?" The second had a small black cylinder and a clear mask, and the cylinder hissed when he turned it, and they put the mask over Sota's nose and mouth, and it fogged and cleared, fogged and cleared. The first one put a clip on Sota's finger with a red light in it, and watched a little box in his hand. I'd never seen one of those.

"How long since it started?"

"Ten to one. About. He's had a cough since the weekend."

"How many puffs?"

"Four. 12:53 and 1:13."

"Has he been in hospital for it before?"

"Twice. When he was eight. Shiomi Municipal."

"Any other medicine?"

"Pills. Every day. In the tin on the fridge." I got the tin down and gave it to him, the whole tin, and he looked in it and gave it to the third one.

"His weight?"

I didn't know. I knew what the pharmacy had cost in August. 4,820 yen. I didn't know what my brother weighed.

"He's small for eleven," I said.

The first one looked at Sota for a second and said a number to the third one, and the third one wrote it down.

"Is your mother here?"

"She's at work. All night. My sister will phone her."

"Then you come with us," he said. "You know the times."

They brought a folding chair with straps and handles up the stairs and sat Sota in it, still bent forward, with the mask on and the black cylinder in his lap, and strapped him in, and two of them carried him. I took the inhaler. Then I went back for his glasses, beside the pillow, folded, and put them in the pocket of my shirt. Then I went after them, and put my shoes on in the genkan without doing the laces, and went down the stairs behind the chair.

On the third floor Mrs. Arai's door was open on its chain, and a strip of her face was looking out. On the second floor a man in an undershirt stood in his doorway with a cigarette and pressed himself back against his door to let us by. Forty-eight steps. Every landing they turned the chair, careful, and every landing Sota's eyes came round and found me over the mask.

Aya was at the bottom in her cardigan and sandals, with her arms wrapped round herself. The ambulance stood with its back doors open, white inside, and its red light going round and round over the concrete walls of the blocks and the bicycle shed.

"Phone Mom," I said.

"What do I say?"

"Say he's breathing. Then stay by the phone."

She looked at me. Then she turned and ran back up the stairs, and I heard her sandals all the way up, slapping.

They put Sota on the stretcher inside, sitting up, and put me on the bench along the side. The back doors shut. We didn't move. The first one was on the radio, talking in numbers, and a voice came back, and he said a hospital's name and another one, and waited. The red light went round outside and came in through the frosted windows, pink, and went away, and came round again. I counted it round eleven times.

"Municipal will take him," the first one said.

Then we were moving, and the siren came on over our heads, so loud inside that it was more like something you were sitting in than something you heard.

I held Sota's hand. The first one sat on the other side of him, watching the little box. Every so often he said a number into the radio. Eighty-nine, he said, once. Then, a while later, ninety. I didn't know what it meant. I kept it anyway. Eighty-nine, then ninety.

Sota's eyes were on me over the mask, very wide without the glasses. The mask fogged and cleared. His fingers were hot. My knee had started going up and down by itself, and I put my other hand on it and held it still.

I leaned down, close to the side of his head, where only he could hear over the siren.

"I've got a car," I said.

His eyes moved.

"It's blue. It doesn't run. There's a cat that lives in it." The ambulance went round a corner, and I braced my feet and held on. "Nobody knows. Not Mom. Not Aya. You're first. I said you'd be first."

He couldn't say anything. His fingers moved in mine, once, and stayed tight.

"Don't tell Mom," I said.

The mask fogged, and cleared.

* * *

The doors of the emergency entrance opened by themselves. Inside it was bright, brighter than daytime, and it smelled of disinfectant and floor polish, and people in white came out from everywhere at once and took the stretcher out of the crew's hands without stopping it, and went through a gap between two curtains with it. I went after them. A nurse, an older one, put her hand flat on my chest at the curtain.

"Wait here. Somebody will come."

The curtain closed. It was pale green, on rings, hung from a rail in the ceiling, and it didn't go all the way down to the floor. Under the edge of it I could see feet going back and forth. White shoes. White shoes. A doctor's brown slip-ons. The wheels of the stretcher. Behind it there was a new hissing, different from the cylinder, louder, and a man's voice, quick and calm, asking for something by a name I didn't know, and a woman's voice answering.

There was a row of orange plastic chairs against the wall. I sat down on the end one.

The first crewman came out from behind the curtain with his helmet under his arm and stopped in front of me. He asked how to spell Sota's name, and our phone number again, and wrote them down. Then he looked at me for a moment.

"You did it right with the times," he said. "Next time, don't wait the full twenty. If he can't talk, you call."

He went out through the doors to the ambulance, and they closed behind him.

I sat on the chair. A clock on the wall said 1:52. Somewhere down the corridor a phone was ringing and nobody was answering it. I put my hand on my shirt pocket and felt Sota's glasses through the cotton, folded.

My legs were shaking. Not my hands. My hands were fine, they were on my knees. It was my legs, from the knees down, very fast and small, so that my heels tapped on the floor. I pressed them down and they kept going under the pressing. I looked at them. I was still holding the cap of the inhaler in my right hand, the little blue cap, and I didn't know when I'd picked it up, and I'd been holding it so hard that it had left a ring in my palm.

The hissing behind the curtain went on. Under the edge, the white shoes had stopped going back and forth. They were standing still, three pairs, round where the stretcher was.

Then the curtain rings ran along the rail.

A nurse had pulled it back from inside, half a meter, and stepped into the gap. White uniform. A clipboard held against her chest with one arm. Her hair was black and pinned up off her neck, all of it, tight. She was reading the clipboard.

"Hayama Sota's family?" she said, to the clipboard.

I knew the voice. The last thing it had said to me was *keep that dry*.

She looked up.

It was Natsume.
