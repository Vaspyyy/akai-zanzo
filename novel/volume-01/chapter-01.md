# Chapter 1: Practicing

At half past six on Friday morning I was sitting on my futon in the shirt I'd worn all night at the station, with my eyes shut, changing gear.

There was no car. There was the tatami room: six mats, two futons, the fan on the floor turning its head from me to Sota and back again, and the smell of gasoline coming off my own hands. My left hand held a lever that wasn't there. First gear was forward and away from me. Second, straight back. Third, across and up. I did it slowly, then fast, then slowly again, because fast you could fake.

My feet were working too. Right foot on the brake. Left foot down on the clutch. Then the part I'd come home for: the ball of the right foot still pressing the brake while the heel rolled over and stabbed the gas, so quick it was more of a hiccup than a push. Then the lever home, the clutch back up, and all of it again for the next gear down.

Natsume had done that every time she slowed down, with her hands and feet busy and her face doing nothing at all. I'd watched it from closer than I'd ever sat to anyone in a car, and I'd watched it so hard I could still see it with my eyes shut. That was why they were shut.

"Nii-chan..."

Sota was sitting up on his futon with his hair flat on one side. His glasses were on the windowsill, and without them his eyes looked small and surprised.

"...What are you doing?"

"...Practicing."

"Practicing what?"

"Shifting."

He felt along the sill for his glasses and put them on so he could look at me properly. There wasn't much to look at. A guy in a gas station shirt on a futon, holding air.

"We don't have a car," he said.

"I know."

"Is it for the red car?"

I didn't answer. That's a mistake with Sota, because to him no answer means yes, and his whole face went wide, the way it did the time Aya came home with a melon somebody had dropped at the supermarket.

"Did you see it again? Was it fast?"

"Go back to sleep."

"I'm not sleepy." He crawled across the gap between our futons on his knees and sat down next to me, cross-legged, copying me. Then he held up his right hand and started moving it.

"Other hand," I said.

"Why?"

"In a Japanese car you sit on the right. The lever's in the middle. So it's your left hand."

He changed hands. He moved the lever in circles, like he was stirring miso soup, and I took his wrist and showed him: forward and away, straight back, across and up. His wrist was so thin my fingers went round it with room to spare.

"Why do we sit on the right?"

"Because we drive on the left."

"Why do we drive on the left?"

"The man who decided was left-handed."

"Really?"

"Probably."

He shifted for a while with the tip of his tongue between his teeth. His chest had the little whistle it gets in the mornings, high up, like a kettle in somebody else's apartment. He pretended it wasn't there. He's good at that.

"What do the feet do?"

"Feet are next week."

"Promise?"

"Sleep, Sota."

He lay down, but on his side, facing me, with his glasses still on. "I won't tell Mom," he said.

"There's nothing to tell."

"I won't tell her anyway."

Mom had gone to the bento factory at half past five. Her 500-yen coin for Sota's lunch would be on the shoe cabinet in the genkan, in the same spot where I'd left half my pay on Wednesday. Behind the fusuma Aya was still asleep, next to an alarm clock she'd bought for being loud, so she could never say she hadn't heard it.

I lay down with my arm over my eyes. My left hand kept going without me.

Four hours earlier I'd been in the passenger seat of the Akai Zanzō with my knees up near my chin and a seatbelt I hadn't fastened myself. She'd done it, with one gloved hand, the way you'd do it for a kid, and told me to hold on to something. Then the harbor road came at us faster than I'd ever seen a road come at anything. On the bypass I looked up through the side window as the overpass went over us, at the walkway where I'd stood on the first of August with a can of beer. That night I was up there. Now I was down here. I'd laughed. I still didn't know why. On the pier lot she'd said, "You were supposed to be scared," in the same voice she used for "Regular. Fill it up," and I'd told her the truth: I was, but I was watching her feet.

Then she'd driven me back to the station and told Kubo-san I had no sense. But good eyes.

I slept two hours. It felt like a blink. Then my own alarm went off by my pillow, and it was the last day of summer remedial classes, and I had to go and pass English.

* * *

Remedial English for the third-years met at nine in an empty classroom on the third floor, the hottest floor in the building, because nobody else wanted it. There were nine of us and forty desks. We were spread around the room like the last onigiri in a convenience store case at night, the ones with the fillings nobody picks.

All the windows were open. It didn't help. The cicadas in the cherry trees along the sports ground were so loud they seemed to be in the room with us. My uniform shirt was stuck to my back before I sat down.

Hotta-sensei taught it. He was our homeroom teacher too, a thin, tired man with a towel round his neck. He fanned himself with a paper fan from a cram school. It had a cartoon owl on it and said WE GET YOU INTO UNIVERSITY! in red letters, and I don't think he'd ever read it.

"Re-test," he said. "Fifty minutes. Forty points is a pass. I'm not asking anybody in this room for more than forty."

It was the subjunctive. He'd spent the whole summer on it. If I were rich. If I were a bird, I could fly to you. I wrote them out the way he'd shown us and didn't see the point of any of them. English seemed to be a language for people who wanted things they couldn't have. You'd think that would make me good at it.

My pencil broke on question eleven. I heard the little snap and sat there holding the stub. Then something tapped my shoulder: a four-color ballpoint, the fat kind with four sliders on top, held out from the desk behind me.

I took it. I didn't turn round. Nobuyuki Kawamata had sat behind me every day for three weeks of remedial classes and hadn't said one word to me, which was normal. Most people at Shiomi Higashi had decided about me in the first week of first year, when somebody found out where I'd been before.

I finished with eleven minutes left and guessed the last two. Hotta-sensei collected the papers and sat at his desk marking them with a red pen, fanning with his other hand, and gave the rest of us a worksheet so we wouldn't talk. It was the same subjunctive with different birds.

I did three lines. Then I turned to the back of my notebook, to a page I'd started the night before at the station, between trucks.

Three pedals, drawn the way you see them past your knees. Clutch on the left, brake in the middle, gas on the right, the gas set a little lower than the brake. Over the brake and the gas I'd drawn a foot, badly, with an arrow from the heel to the gas. Underneath I'd written the order: BRAKE. CLUTCH. BLIP. GEAR. CLUTCH UP. Then I'd crossed it out and written it again differently, and crossed that out too, because I couldn't remember where the lever went to neutral. I clicked the pen to black and went over the foot again.

"That's heel-and-toe."

He'd said it to the back of my head, very quietly, the way you'd tell somebody his fly was open.

I put my arm over the page.

"Sorry. Sorry. I wasn't looking. I was looking a bit." Kawamata leaned forward over his desk until his face was nearly on my shoulder. He smelled of soy sauce and pork bones, the way he always did. His family had a ramen shop by the station. "Ball of the foot on the brake, heel on the gas. Or the side of the foot, it depends on the car. The magazine says in some cars the pedals are too far apart and you can't do it at all unless you've got enormous feet, and in some cars they put them perfect on purpose. Do you drive?"

"No."

"Then why are you drawing heel-and-toe?"

"Why do you know what it is?"

He took that as an invitation. Out of his bag came a magazine rolled into a tube and gone soft from being rolled: *Street Tune*, this month's, with a black Skyline GT-R on the cover, shot from down on the ground, spitting a flame out of its exhaust. In the bag's side pocket was a disposable camera, still in its cardboard.

"Every issue since I was thirteen," he said. "Except one. My mother threw it out by mistake. She says by mistake." He opened it near the back, at a page of small print, and put his finger under four lines. *Kawamata N. (17), Shiomi.* A question about tire pressures in the rain. "June. They didn't answer it. But they printed it."

"Kawamata," Hotta-sensei said, without looking up from his marking. "Worksheet."

Kawamata sat back and wrote very fast for about thirty seconds. Then he leaned forward again.

"So who did you see do it?"

"Somebody in a Roadster."

"A Eunos?" He nearly came over the desk. "120 PS. The magazine says it's the most honest car in Japan. Honest means slow. They always say honest when they mean slow, and fun when they mean slow, and kind when they mean ugly. I've been studying it."

"It's not slow."

"It's 120 PS."

"It beat a Silvia."

"Which Silvia? A K's has 205 PS. So either you're lying or..."

He stopped. He looked at me the way you look at a vending machine that has just dropped two cans.

"The harbor one," he said. "The ghost Roadster."

"The Akai Zanzō."

"You know about it." He grabbed the edge of my desk. "A truck driver told my father about it in July, at our counter. He said it beat a GT-R on the bypass, and when it went under the bridge there was nobody in the driver's seat."

"It was a Silvia. A white S13 with a big wing. And somebody was driving."

"How do you know?"

"I was on the bridge."

His mouth stayed open. Then his eyes went narrow, and I could see him remembering every customer who had ever leaned on his father's counter and sworn they'd seen it. "Everybody was on the bridge," he said. "One of my father's customers saw it at the harbor. One saw it on the expressway near Nagoya. One said it doesn't have headlights, it just knows where the road is."

"I rode in it," I said. "Last night."

Kawamata laughed. He sounded impressed.

"Okay," he said.

"I did."

"Sure. Who drives it?"

I opened my mouth and shut it again. I thought of her glove on the gear lever, and of her at the pumps, never saying one word she didn't need. I didn't know much about her. I knew she wouldn't want her name going round Kawamata Ramen with the gyoza.

"I'm not telling you that."

"See?" He was delighted. He sat back and pointed at me, then looked at his finger, and put it away, as if he'd remembered who he was pointing it at. "Hayama-kun. I didn't know you were funny."

I went back to my drawing. My ears were hot. I gave the arrow a second head, and then a third.

At ten to twelve Hotta-sensei read out the marks. Kawamata got seventy-one. I got forty-four. Everybody passed.

"Good," he said, and wiped his neck with the towel. "Go home. Sleep. Friday, second term."

When he handed me my paper he held on to it for a second, so I had to look at him.

"Hayama. Come awake."

Kawamata caught up with me at the shoe lockers. He changed out of his indoor shoes, whose heels he'd trodden flat, and talked all the way across the yard. He'd failed the first term because the composition was "My Family" and he'd written about the GT-R.

"Why?"

"It's more interesting than my family."

At the gate he stopped, and his face went careful.

"Can I ask you something? Is it true you were in reform school?"

"It wasn't reform school."

"What was it?"

"A place."

He waited for more. There wasn't any more.

"Sorry," he said, and I believed him, and twelve seconds later he was on to driving schools. His cousin had done his license at Shiomi Driving School in the spring. "280,000 yen. My uncle still talks about it. My father says he'll pay half of mine if I pass everything." He held up his re-test with the 71 on it in red. "So far I've passed this."

280,000 yen. I didn't say it out loud.

"I have to go," he said. "Lunch rush. I'm the one who carries." He turned to go, and turned back. "Call me Nobu. Everybody does. Well. My father does."

He went off toward the station at a trot, his bag banging on his back.

I didn't call him anything yet.

* * *

The bookstore was in the covered shopping street behind the station, a long narrow shop between a shoe-repair stall and a pachinko parlor. At half past twelve the pachinko parlor was already full, and every time its doors slid open, cold air and the roar of the balls came out onto the street together. I walked past it fast. I always do.

Inside it was dim and smelled of paper. There was a handwritten sign on the magazine rack, NO STANDING READING, and under it four men standing reading. The car books were on the bottom two shelves past the magazines, between Fishing and Go.

I found it in two minutes. A thick paperback with a white cover: SPORTS DRIVING FROM THE BASICS. Inside there were photographs of feet.

Somebody had lain in the footwell of a car with a camera and photographed a man's feet on the pedals, a row of pictures numbered one to six. Brake. Clutch in. Lever to neutral, with the hand in a little photo of its own. The heel rolling over. The lever into the lower gear. The clutch coming up. I read them all twice. The second time, my right foot started doing it on the floor of the shop, and the loose sole of my sneaker squeaked on the lino.

So that was where neutral went. My drawing had been nearly right.

A few pages on, the book said beginners look at the corner, and good drivers look through it, to where they want to go. I thought of her eyes last night, way down the road, further than the headlights reached.

That was when the feather duster came.

The owner was a small old man in a cardigan, in August, with a feather duster. He came down the aisle dusting the tops of the books. He dusted Fishing. He dusted Go. He dusted the shelf a hand's width from my face, and a little cloud came off it into the light from the door.

I moved one step to the left and kept reading.

He followed me and dusted the shelf there. Then he dusted my shoulder.

I moved another step. He dusted my other shoulder. By then I was at the end of the aisle, with the book open at a photo of a steering wheel held at nine and three, and there was nowhere left to go.

He dusted my head.

"This isn't a library," he said.

"Two more pages."

"Buy it or put it back."

I turned it over. 1,800 yen. With tax, 1,854 yen. That was two hours and forty minutes under the canopy, and I already had most of it in my head.

I put it back. He dusted the place where it had been, in case I'd left fingerprints.

At the convenience store on the corner I bought a tuna mayonnaise onigiri, 110 yen plus 3 for the government, and sat with it on the low wall by the bus stop, in the strip of shade under the timetable. Kawamata's pen was still in my shirt pocket. I turned the receipt over and clicked the pen to black.

I had 18,787 yen in the world: 1,187 yen in my pocket, and 17,600 yen in an envelope inside my English dictionary at home, which was the safest place in the apartment, because nobody in my family had ever opened it, including me.

280,000 take away 18,787 left 261,213.

5,600 yen a night, four nights a week. Kubo-san paid every other Wednesday, so 44,800 yen a payday. Half of it went on the shoe cabinet. That left 22,400 yen.

261,213 divided by 22,400. I did it the long way, carrying numbers down the edge of the receipt. It came out at eleven and a bit, and nobody pays you a bit, so twelve. The next payday was the sixth of September. I counted the Wednesdays forward on my fingers, ran out of fingers, and went round again. The twelfth one was the seventh of February.

I'd be eighteen two days later.

If I never bought anything. Not a coffee from the machine. Not an onigiri. I looked at the onigiri. 113 yen. About ten minutes of standing under the canopy. I ate it anyway, because it was paid for.

Then I wrote the other sum underneath, the one I'd been not writing. 261,213 divided by the whole 44,800. Six paydays. The fifteenth of November.

I looked at that for a while. Then I drew a line through it, and another, and kept going until the pen went through the paper and into my knee.

I folded the receipt in four and put it in my pocket with the 1,187 yen.

* * *

At two in the afternoon the apartment was like the inside of a rice cooker with the lid on.

Sota was at the low table in his undershirt with his summer free research spread out in front of him: a big glass jar that used to hold pickled plums, full of cicada skins, and a sheet of cardboard he'd ruled into boxes with a pencil. He'd been picking them off the trees round the danchi since July. There were forty-seven. He told me the number before I had my shoes off.

"It's due Friday," he said. "I have to glue them on and write what they are."

"They're shells."

"They're not shells, Nii-chan. They're what's left when the cicada climbs out." He took one out of the jar and turned it over, very carefully, so I could see underneath. "Some are boys and some are girls. You can tell from here."

I looked. I couldn't tell.

I lay down on the tatami in my uniform trousers with the fan aimed at my face. The air it pushed at me was as hot as the air it had taken away. The last thing I heard was Sota counting under his breath, "forty-five, forty-six," and starting again because he'd lost one.

The smell of boiling water woke me. Mom was in the kitchen in a T-shirt with the window over the sink wide open, and the big pot was going, which made the kitchen worse. She'd been up since half past four and on her feet at the factory until two, and in an hour she'd be stacking shelves at the supermarket until midnight. She was cutting a cucumber into thin strips.

"You're up," she said, without turning round.

"Yeah."

"You slept in your school trousers."

"Yeah."

"Hang them up before you go. Second term's on Friday."

Somen, then. In August it was nearly always somen: cheap and cold, and the only thing anyone could face. She tipped the noodles into the colander and ran the tap over them, lifting and turning them with her fingers, and her hands were red from the factory, the knuckles dry and split from a summer of rinsing bento trays.

The front door went, and Aya came in with a supermarket bag in one hand and her shoes coming off with the other, which is a thing she can do. She put a plastic tray on the table. Four croquettes under plastic wrap, with a yellow sticker: 30% OFF.

"From the deli," she said. "320 yen, 30% off, 224. They were going in the bin at seven. I paid. Somebody say thank you."

"Thank you," Sota said.

"Somebody who isn't eleven."

We ate at the low table with our knees touching, because there isn't any other way to eat at it. The somen sat in a big glass bowl of water with ice cubes knocking against the sides. Mom put a croquette on each plate and cut hers in half and moved half of it onto Sota's when she thought nobody was looking. Everybody was looking. Nobody said anything.

The TV was on with the sound low. A man in a suit was talking about Aum again, with a photo behind him of the leader's face and his beard. Then baseball: Nomo, in America, a small figure in a white uniform and a blue cap turning his back to the batter before he threw, and Sota stood up and did the windup with a chopstick.

"Sit," Mom said.

He sat.

"I paid the pharmacy on the way home," Mom said to me. "With your money."

"It's yours. I gave it to you."

"I'm telling you where it went." She didn't look up from her bowl. "Pharmacy, 4,820. Sota's things for second term, 2,150. The rest is in the rent envelope."

I looked at my somen. There wasn't anything to say back to that, so I ate.

Under the table, without asking me, my right foot had found the edge of the floor cushion and was rolling over it, heel and ball, heel and ball.

"Stop it," Aya said.

"What?"

"Your foot. You're kicking me."

"I'm not."

"You've kicked me five times. I counted."

Sota put his chopsticks down. "He's practicing!"

Aya turned and looked at him. "Practicing what?"

Sota looked at me. He looked at his somen. He looked at Aya.

"...Kicking," he said.

"Practice on the wall," Aya said.

I sat on my feet the rest of the meal, in seiza, which you can't kick anyone from. Mom watched me across the table with the look she gives the electricity bill.

At a quarter to eight she went to the genkan in her supermarket vest and stopped with one shoe on. She was looking at my sneakers. The sole of the right one had come away at the toe. When you walked, it opened and closed.

"Your shoe's open."

"It's fine."

"When it rains it won't be fine." She put the other shoe on. "The shoe shop by the station has canvas ones for 2,980. I looked."

Then she went, and the steel door shut behind her softly, the way she shuts it when Sota's asleep, even though Sota wasn't.

I left at half past nine. The sole slapped on every step from the fourth floor down, all forty-eight of them.

* * *

I got through Friday's shift on five hours' sleep, and I don't remember much of it.

Sunday nights were slow at the station until the trucks started back toward four. At three in the morning the harbor road was empty under the sodium lights, and I was standing on the curb of the pump island with the ball of my right foot on the edge and the heel hanging off it in the air.

Ball of the foot down. Heel over. Off.

Kubo-san was watching me from the office doorway with a can of coffee.

"What's wrong with your foot."

"Nothing."

"You've been doing nothing with it since midnight."

I kept my foot on the curb. I'd been thinking about it for two days, since the bookstore, and the book had told me how but not why, and Kubo-san was the only person I knew who might know why.

"Kubo-san. Why do you have to blip?"

He looked at me over the can for a long time. Then he came out and sat down on the stool by the oil rack, which meant it was going to be more than three words.

"Ever jump off a bus before it stops?"

"...Once."

"How'd you land?"

"On my face."

"Your feet hit the road going slower than the rest of you." He drank some coffee. "Car's the same. The wheels are going one speed. You drop a gear, the engine has to spin faster to match them. You don't give it gas, the wheels have to drag it up there themselves. The tires pay for that. The back wheels skid, and the back end steps out. On a wet road you're in the wall."

"So you blip to get the engine up to speed first."

"So it lands on its feet."

"And the heel?"

"Because she's braking at the same time. Two feet, three pedals. One of them has to do two jobs."

He had the coffee in his left hand, and in the light from the office I saw the back of it properly for the first time. There was a burn scar across it, white and shiny. The creases of his knuckles were black, the kind of black that soap doesn't reach.

"Can you do it?" I said. "Heel-and-toe?"

"I can pump gas."

He went back into the office and sat down at the desk with the coin tray. I followed him as far as the door and asked the thing I'd actually been wanting to ask all weekend.

"Does she ever come on other days?"

"Thursdays." He was counting change into stacks of ten and didn't look up. "Like burnable garbage."

I stood on the curb anyway, after he'd gone in. Ball of the foot. Heel over. Off.

Tuesday was trucks. Wednesday was a night off, and I slept fourteen hours. Thursday was the last day of summer vacation, and Sota spent it at the low table gluing forty-seven cicada skins to the cardboard in rows, and writing under each one in pencil: BOY, or GIRL, or I DON'T KNOW.

* * *

On the last Thursday of August I was at the pumps by a quarter to ten with my cap on straight.

Kubo-san looked up from his newspaper when I came in, looked at the clock, and looked at me.

"Early," he said.

"The bus was early."

"You walked. Your shoes are white with dust." He turned a page. "Sit down. You're making the pumps nervous."

I didn't sit down. I wiped both pump islands, all four nozzles, and when there was nothing left to wipe I did the office windows from the outside, so that Kubo-san had to watch me do it from the inside, with his coffee, the way you watch a fish in a tank.

By then I knew the harbor road by ear. You learn it in three weeks of nights if you have nothing else to think about. The container trucks came first, the big tractors grinding down through the gears at the light by the rail crossing, a sound like someone dragging furniture across a floor upstairs. After midnight came the taxis, Crowns and Cedrics running on LPG, which sigh instead of revving. Then the drunks in their fathers' sedans. Then nothing, and the cranes, and the mosquitoes.

None of them sounded like her car. Her car didn't sigh and it didn't drag. It went up. That was the only way I could put it, even to myself: every other engine on that road went along, and hers went up, like a voice.

At 12:10 a kei truck came in for 1,000 yen of regular and the driver paid in coins. At 1:00 Kubo-san took his radio into the back room, where he listened to a late-night call-in show and argued with the callers. At 1:40 I bought a can of coffee from the machine by the air pump. 110 yen. I didn't want it. I wanted something in my hand.

At 1:50 I started listening properly.

I told myself it was nothing. Three Thursdays in a row isn't a law of nature. Kubo-san had said every Thursday, and I'd built my whole week around a sentence an old man said while he counted change.

She'd said one lap. Maybe she'd meant one.

At 2:00 a car turned off the bypass and my heart did something stupid. Pop-up headlights. I saw them flip open far down the road, two little eyes waking up, and I was walking toward the pump island before the sound reached me.

It was a 180SX. Silver, rust at the rear arches, a guy about my age driving, a girl asleep against the window. He wanted 2,000 yen of regular and the toilet key. I gave him both. I cleaned his windshield, and I did a good job of it, because it wasn't his fault he wasn't her.

At 2:30 Kubo-san came out and stood next to me under the canopy. He lit a cigarette and held it the way he holds a dipstick, between two fingers and away from himself, like it might drip. He didn't say anything for most of it.

"She's not a train," he said finally.

"I know."

"People get sick. People get busy. Sometimes a woman just doesn't feel like buying gasoline from a kid who stares."

"I don't stare."

He let the smoke out through his nose. It hung in the canopy lights, which were full of moths. I'd never noticed how many moths there were.

"First Thursday she's missed since I started noticing," he said, and went back inside.

I stayed under the canopy until it got light. The trucks started again around five, the first ones heading for the fish market with their refrigeration units rattling. The sky over the cranes went grey, then the color of weak tea. At six the day man came, chewing gum with his mouth open, and I signed the sheet and handed over the cap.

I went home the long way, up the bypass on the footpath. I don't know why. Yes, I do. I stood on the overpass for a while and looked at the curve, empty and clean in the morning, nothing on it but a page of newspaper lifting and settling in the wind from the trucks.

On the outside of the curve there was a section of guardrail newer than the rest. I'd stood up there maybe twenty nights and never seen it. It was brighter, and the bolts hadn't rusted yet, and below it, on the concrete at the foot of the overpass stairs, somebody had scrubbed at a dark scrape, a long time ago by the look of it, until the concrete went pale.

Somebody had hit that hard.

I went home. Mom had already left for the factory. Sota's cicada board was propped against the shoe cabinet, where he couldn't forget it. Sota was asleep with his mouth open and his glasses still on, and I took them off and folded them and put them next to his inhaler, the way he likes.

Second term started at half past eight. I set the alarm for ten to eight. I lay down and closed my eyes and did the shifting again, four to three, three to two, the heel rolling over onto the gas, until I didn't know if I was practicing or dreaming.

She didn't come. That was the whole night. I'd tell it shorter if I could.
